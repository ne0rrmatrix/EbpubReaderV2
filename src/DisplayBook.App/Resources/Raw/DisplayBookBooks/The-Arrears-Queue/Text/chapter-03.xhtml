Chapter 3: Preseason

Two days.

The voucher letter had moved from the wall above the bench to the inside pocket of her jacket sometime in the last week, without her deciding to move it, the way a thing migrates toward wherever your hand goes most often. She read the number off it on the walk to school out of the same habit that made her check a reading twice on a board that had already told her the answer, not because the number might have changed overnight but because checking cost nothing and not checking felt like a risk she hadn’t priced.

She had spent most of the week between the Ministry office and this morning doing the ordinary version of what the last six days always looked like: a repair booked most evenings, the bench never quite cleared, a room search that kept turning up the same four hundred listings in a slightly different order each time she ran it, the co-signer wall and the age wall standing exactly where they had stood two weeks earlier, unmoved by anything she’d tried against them. Nothing about the search had moved. Two days felt less like a deadline now than like a fact she was simply going to arrive at, the way a board arrived at a diagnosis whether or not she liked what the meter told her.

Kitsilano Secondary in the morning ran at a volume she had learned, across three other schools, to treat the way she treated a shop floor with machinery running: not dangerous, just loud enough that conversation took more from her than it gave back, and best managed by finding a wall to put her spine against and a task to put her hands on. She had neither this early, so she found the least crowded stretch of hallway and stood in it, reading a supply catalogue on her phone until the bell moved everyone into the same direction at once.

The building itself was old enough to have been renovated twice and both times only partway, a wing of new windows set into a facade that still had the original brick showing above the sightline, a cafeteria addition bolted onto the side of a gymnasium that predated it by decades, visible from outside as two different colours of concrete meeting at a seam that had never quite been finished off. Somebody had once cared enough to put terrazzo flooring in the main corridor, and somebody else, years later, had patched a burst pipe under it with a rectangle of plain grey concrete that didn’t match anything, and neither the terrazzo nor the patch had been touched since. She noticed things like that without meaning to, the way a building told you what had mattered to whoever built it and what had mattered to whoever kept it running afterward, two different sets of decisions layered on top of each other like solder over an older, worse repair.

Her homeroom teacher took attendance off a tablet that hung for four full seconds on her name before resolving, a transfer student’s file being pulled from wherever transfer files lived before they finished migrating into the system everyone else was already inside, and nobody in the room noticed the pause but her.

* * *

The girl who sat down next to her in second period did it without asking, the way people did things around Nadia that she hadn’t agreed to and hadn’t objected to either, mostly because objecting required a sentence and doing nothing required none.

Her name was Priya Sandhu, and she had apparently decided this was her seat now on the strength of one shared elective, a media studies block that ran mostly on group work and the teacher’s tolerance for whoever sat where. She talked for most of the period, not at Nadia exactly, more in the general direction of the desk, a running commentary on the assignment, which was a short documentary project due sometime after the fall break that nobody had properly started, and a girl three rows over who had apparently said something worth relitigating for the second day running, and a show Nadia hadn’t seen and gathered she was not required to have seen in order to follow the commentary on it. Priya periodically checked whether Nadia was still listening with a glance rather than a question, which Nadia found easier to answer than an actual question would have been, because a glance only needed a nod.

At one point Priya asked, mid-sentence about something else entirely, what Nadia’s old school had been like, and didn’t appear to notice when the answer came back short, three words and a shrug, because she was already three sentences further into whatever she’d actually meant to say and the question had mostly been a bridge to get there. Nadia found this restful in a way she didn’t examine closely. A question that didn’t require an answer, only the appearance of one, cost her almost nothing.

The teacher circled the room twice during the group work portion, checking progress that mostly didn’t exist yet, and stopped at their table long enough to ask what their documentary topic was going to be. Priya answered before Nadia had finished deciding whether she was meant to answer at all, something about local infrastructure nobody paid attention to, storm drains or bus shelters or whichever one of a dozen half-formed ideas she’d mentioned in the last twenty minutes had happened to be the one still in her mouth when the teacher arrived, and the teacher nodded and moved on to the next table without waiting to see whether the idea would survive contact with an actual deadline. Nadia thought, briefly and without any particular weight attached to the thought, that she could have named forty things in this building alone that qualified, starting with the burst-pipe patch in the main corridor and the directory two buildings over that still listed departments that no longer had a floor, and said none of it, because Priya hadn’t asked and the topic had already moved on twice more by the time she’d finished the thought.

Somewhere in the second half of it, between one tangent and the next, Priya asked what Nadia was doing for lunch, already reaching for her bag as though the asking and the answer were the same motion, and Nadia said she didn’t know yet, which was true, and Priya said well now she did, and that was apparently the whole negotiation.

The cafeteria was a newer room than the rest of the building, part of whichever renovation had put the new windows in, with long tables bolted to the floor in rows and a serving line at the far end that Nadia didn’t bother joining, having brought two sandwiches from the room and eaten one of them already on the walk over.

Lunch turned out to include a second person, a girl named Hana who arrived already talking, mid-sentence, to someone else entirely, and folded Nadia into the conversation without introducing herself first, on the working assumption that anyone sitting at this table already knew who she was or would find out eventually and didn’t need it explained. She had a way of stating plans rather than proposing them. There was a thing on Saturday, she said, at somebody’s house, nothing big, and Nadia was coming, and the sentence had no question mark anywhere in it, and by the time Nadia had worked out that she could in fact have said no, the moment for saying it had already gone past, folded into the next thing Hana was already saying, something about whether Priya still had the string lights from the last one or whether somebody needed to bring new ones.

Priya said she had the lights. Hana said good, and then, without any change in tone at all, asked Nadia whether she had a ride or needed one, already reaching for her phone as though the answer were a formality standing between the question and a plan she’d already made regardless of what it turned out to be.

Nadia said she’d get there on her own, which was true in the sense that it left every actual detail unresolved and closed the subject anyway, and Hana accepted it the way she seemed to accept most things that weren’t a flat no, filing it as settled and moving on to something else before Nadia had finished the sentence.

Nadia ate without adding much, which nobody seemed to register as unusual, and noted, the way she noted most things about people, that she had done nothing to cause any of this and had no clear route back out of it either, and that the not having a route out was somehow different from minding, though she couldn’t have said exactly where the line between the two sat.

* * *

Preseason conditioning ran out of the small gym, the one with the mats, at the end of the athletics wing, and she found it by the smell before she found the room number, the specific mix of old rubber matting and floor cleaner and the metal tang of equipment that had been handled by a lot of hands over a lot of years.

The room told her its age before anyone in it said a word. Mats patched along three separate seams with tape that had gone a different shade of grey than the mat itself, in at least two different eras of tape. A wall bar bolted into brick that had been repainted around it so many times the bolts sat proud of the surface like something growing out of it. A single working clock, the other three sockets around the room dark, unlit, wires capped off rather than removed, somebody’s decision from years ago to stop replacing bulbs it wasn’t worth the ladder for. A trophy case along the near wall held plaques going back further than the room’s current paint job, gaps in the rows where a few had clearly been taken down and never put back, dust outlines still visible on the shelf where they used to sit.

The coach was already on the floor when Nadia came in, a clipboard under one arm, talking two returning wrestlers through a stretch neither of them was doing correctly, correcting the angle on one kid’s hip with two fingers and a word rather than a lecture. V. Vaillancourt, the flyer had said. In person she went by Val, apparently, because that was what the room called her the second she turned around, and she took Nadia’s name off a roster on the clipboard with the flat efficiency of someone who had run a preseason intake a great many times and had no interest in making it longer than it needed to be.

She was in club gear rather than anything with the school’s name on it, a jacket worn soft at the cuffs from years of use, a whistle on a lanyard gone grey from sweat and washing rather than any particular colour it had started as, and a watch on her wrist that looked like it had been chosen for a stopwatch function rather than for telling time, which told Nadia something about the kind of coach she was before either of them had exchanged more than a dozen words.

She asked whether Nadia had wrestled before, and Nadia said yes, three schools, and named the weight class, and Val nodded once, wrote something short in the margin next to her name, and told her to get changed and join the warm-up, no ceremony to it at all, which suited Nadia better than ceremony would have. She didn’t ask why three schools in whatever span of years it had taken to produce three schools, and Nadia noted the absence of the question the way she noted most absences, filing it without deciding yet what it meant.

* * *

Tomasz found her on the mat before either of them said a word to each other, in the specific way returning wrestlers find new ones during a warm-up, sizing without staring, and introduced himself by asking what she walked around at, which was the only opening question that ever mattered in a room like this one, ahead of a name, ahead of a school, ahead of anything else either of them might have said first.

Fifty-two, she said.

He said he was at seventy-one and hating every gram of the last four of it already, three weeks before he technically needed to be hating anything, and asked whether she was one of the ones who cut smart or one of the ones who cut stupid, because he could already tell which one he was and it wasn’t the smart one.

She said she hadn’t decided yet, which was mostly true, and he laughed a short, genuine laugh, like it was a better answer than it actually was, and from there the conversation ran the way conversations with people who already understood the specific vocabulary always ran with her, easier than most, because neither of them had to explain why a person would do this to themselves on purpose. He complained about water cuts and sauna suits and a coach two schools ago who used to weigh the whole team in front of each other, which he said like it was the worst thing that had ever happened to him and probably, to him, it was.

He asked what she cut with, meaning method rather than food, and she said mostly just eating less for a few days beforehand, nothing dramatic, and he looked at her like she’d admitted to something faintly suspicious, said that was not a real answer, that everyone eventually had a real answer once they’d done it enough times, hers just hadn’t caught up to her yet. He said this without any edge in it, the specific confidence of somebody four years and God knew how many kilos of water into a sport that rewarded exactly that kind of confidence, and told her to ask him again in November, once the real cutting had started and the answer had teeth in it, and she said she would, and meant it, filing it the way she filed anything that sounded like it would matter later and didn’t yet.

He talked while they stretched, mostly, filling the space other people would have left quiet, and she found that she didn’t mind it the way she minded most filled silences, because none of it required anything back from her beyond the occasional confirming sound, which cost her nothing at all.

* * *

Val ran the first session as a baseline, not a cut. Four stations, ninety seconds each, thirty seconds to rotate: a bike, a row machine with a frayed strap someone had wrapped in electrical tape rather than replaced, bodyweight circuits along the wall bar, and a shuttle run marked out with cones that had gone brittle enough at the base that one of them cracked clean in half under a returning wrestler’s foot on the second rotation and nobody stopped for it, just kicked the pieces to the edge of the mat and kept going. Nadia’s legs told her by the third rotation that the last three weeks without a team to train with had cost her more than she’d let herself notice, a specific hollow burn low in both thighs that had nothing to do with technique and everything to do with volume, and she filed that too, a number to correct rather than a feeling to sit with.

At the end came bodyweight and grip strength, recorded against every name on the roster, numbers Val said she’d use to build the season’s training blocks rather than anything to do with weight class yet. Nadia’s grip numbers came back high enough that Val glanced at the dynamometer a second time before writing them down, the specific double-check of someone confirming a reading rather than doubting the wrestler, and then wrote them without comment and moved to the next name, which was exactly the amount of attention Nadia would have chosen for herself if anyone had asked her opinion on the matter. The scale for it sat against the far wall on a low wooden platform of its own, older than anything else in the room by the look of it, a genuine clinical balance beam with a sliding counterweight, the kind Nadia had only otherwise seen in photographs, with a cheap digital display bolted to a bracket welded onto the frame at some later point, a battery compartment and a small board behind a plastic window that had clearly never been part of the original design.

It read three different numbers for the same wrestler in under a minute, eighteen kilos apart on the second try, which was not a small error, not something a person could round away, and the kid on the platform looked at Val like it was somehow his own fault for standing on it wrong.

Val said it had been doing that since last season, that maintenance had looked at it twice and called it fixed twice, and that for today they’d just use the beam itself and skip the digital number, sliding the counterweight by hand and reading it the old way while three separate people stood around checking their phones for how much longer this was going to take.

Nadia had already crossed the room before she’d decided to, the itch to open the housing outweighing whatever she’d meant to be doing instead, and asked whether she could look at it while everyone else ran the next circuit.

Val said sure, with the particular shrug of someone who didn’t expect it to go anywhere and had nothing to lose by letting a kid try.

The digital board was held on with two screws and a strip of tape someone had added after the fact to keep the cable from working loose again, which told her the cable was the actual problem before she’d even opened it: a connector that had been reseated by hand more than once by someone without the right tool for it, the pins spread just enough to make contact only sometimes, only under certain angles of the bracket’s vibration. Not a board fault. Not a battery fault. A five-second problem wearing the costume of a two-year one. She could see, from the wear pattern on the housing screws, that whoever maintenance had sent both times had opened the panel, looked at the same intermittent connector, and closed it again without a crimper on hand to actually do anything about it, twice, a full callout each time for a problem that had never once required more than the right tool in the right pocket.

She reseated the connector properly, crimped it tighter than it had been designed to need with a pair of pliers from her bag rather than the multitool everyone assumed she didn’t have on her, and taped the cable to the frame at a second point so the strain wouldn’t reach the connector again, all of it inside four minutes, without once looking like she was thinking hard about any part of it, because she wasn’t. It was the same category of problem as the ring in the Kelso House kitchen and the connector on the courier’s bike, different housing, same shape underneath it, and shapes like that one she didn’t have to think through any more than she had to think through walking.

It read the same number three times in a row after that, then a fourth time with somebody deliberately shifting their weight between readings to try to break it again, and it held.

Val looked at the display, then at Nadia, then said, in a tone with nothing performative in it, that maintenance had apparently missed something in two visits that had taken a stranger four minutes, and asked where she’d learned to do that.

Nadia said mostly just looking, which was the same thing she always said, and Val accepted it the way most people did, without pushing, and went back to running the circuit, though she wrote something else in the margin by Nadia’s name before she moved on, something Nadia didn’t see and didn’t ask about.

It cost her the twenty minutes she’d meant to use afterward messaging a client back about a job that had come in during the session, a reply that went out forty minutes later than it should have and got her a shorter window that evening than she’d wanted, which she noticed and filed and didn’t revisit, because twenty minutes for a scale that would otherwise still be lying to every wrestler on the roster seemed, on the numbers, like a fair trade.

* * *

The live rolling at the end of the session was the easiest hour of her day by a distance that had nothing to do with how hard it was.

It was hard. Her legs were already down to nothing from the conditioning circuit, and the girl she drew for the last round outweighed her by six kilos and knew what she was doing with the weight.

The whistle went. They collided on the tie-up, both of them fighting for the same wrist. Nadia gave ground on purpose, half a step, enough to change the angle. The girl followed the pressure, the way bigger opponents always followed pressure when they had six kilos of confidence behind it. Wrong call. Nadia dropped her level, got an underhook before the girl could reset, and for two full seconds it was only weight and friction and the mat coming up against her shoulder.

Then she was under, hip trapped, the girl’s weight settling in a way that meant real trouble in about four more seconds if nothing changed. She didn’t panic. There was nothing to panic about. There was a clock. There was a whistle that would end it, one way or another, inside the next ninety seconds, and a floor under both of them that wasn’t going anywhere, and a rule set neither of them was going to break no matter how the next four seconds went. She framed off the far arm, turned into the pressure instead of away from it, found half an inch of space that hadn’t existed a moment before, and used it.

By the time the whistle actually blew she’d spent most of the round managing the bad angle rather than escaping it cleanly, which read, on the scoreboard neither of them was keeping, as a loss. None of that registered as anything worse than a problem she hadn’t finished solving yet. Everything either of them had done to the other in those three minutes was simply information, force and leverage and where the next opening was going to be, nothing about it asking her to guess what anyone meant by anything.

The changeroom afterward was worse than the mat had been, by a margin that had nothing to do with anything physical. Eleven girls in a room built for maybe eight, three showers running at once, the fan overhead doing almost nothing against the steam, and under all of it a fluorescent tube by the mirrors that had gone slightly wrong in a way most people wouldn’t consciously register, a hum a few hertz off true that sat somewhere behind her eyes the whole time she was in the room. She had solved this problem three schools ago and solved it the same way every time since: get changed fast, keep to the end locker, and let the noise become texture rather than something she had to keep evaluating one sound at a time. It worked, mostly, the way running the hill worked, not by making the thing quieter but by giving her something else to spend her attention on instead.

Someone two lockers down was telling a story about a party that had nothing to do with Nadia, loudly, to someone who was only half listening, and Nadia caught maybe one word in five of it and didn’t try for more, tying her shoes with the specific focus of someone who had found the one task in the room simple enough to actually finish.

Hana catching her by the door afterward asked considerably more of her than the six kilos had.

Saturday, Hana said again, like it hadn’t already been settled at lunch, like she was confirming an appointment rather than making one, and asked whether Nadia needed the address or whether Priya had already sent it. Nadia said Priya had sent it, which wasn’t true yet but would be within the hour if she knew anything about how today had gone so far, and Hana said good, and was gone down the hallway before Nadia had finished the sentence, already talking to somebody else about something else entirely.

Nadia stood by the door a moment longer than she needed to, running the day the way she’d run the diode-mode trace on the mobility controller two weeks ago, checking it against itself rather than against anything external, and the number that came out of it was that she had spent forty minutes rolling with a stranger without a single unmanaged second in her, and roughly four minutes at lunch not listening to a word anyone was saying because she was too busy calculating how to get out of Saturday without having to say the sentence that would get her out of it.

She didn’t get out of it. She hadn’t expected to.

* * *

Val stopped her on the way out of the gym, the form already filled in at the top with the program name and the district registration number, and handed it over along with a short explanation of how the weekly signature worked: Nadia brought it each session, Val initialled the date box at the end of practice, and at the end of the month the whole sheet went to whichever office needed it, no separate meeting required, no extra step beyond showing up and being seen to show up.

She said it the way she’d explained the scale earlier, plainly, without asking why a sixteen-year-old needed a document like this signed at all, and Nadia noted the absence of the question a second time that day and filed it next to the first one, a small growing category of things Val apparently didn’t need explained to be willing to help with anyway.

The walk to the SkyTrain ran past the same lot where the bakery used to be, gravel and weeds under a sky that had gone the flat grey it went most evenings now, and she ran the day the way she’d run the diode-mode trace on the mobility controller two weeks ago, checking it against itself rather than against anything external. Forty minutes rolling with a stranger, not one second of it unmanaged. Four minutes reseating a connector nobody else had bothered to crimp properly in two years. Four minutes at lunch not listening to a word anyone said because she’d been too busy working out how to get out of Saturday. A changeroom she’d survived the way she survived every changeroom, by making herself smaller inside it rather than by anyone making it quieter for her.

She didn’t get out of Saturday. She hadn’t expected to, and by the time the train pulled in she’d mostly stopped running the number on it, because a number that didn’t change no matter how many times she checked it stopped being useful somewhere around the third check, and this one was well past that.

* * *

The form was in her bag by the time she got back to Kelso House, a single sheet with name and date fields down the left side and a line at the bottom for a coach’s signature each week, the paperwork version of the supervised activity category Bhandari had described two weeks earlier in a cubicle on the other side of the city, filled in now for the first time, one box out of what would eventually be a great many boxes.

She pinned it to the wall next to the voucher letter, which had two days on it now instead of eight, and looked at the two of them side by side for a moment, one clock closing and one clock just opening, a straight line of paper running from a cubicle on Kingsway to a scale with a bad connector to a signature that hadn’t existed that morning. One category down. One left, and no route yet to a form that would take it, and a Saturday between now and any answer to that one, already promised to a house she’d never seen.

Then she sat down at the bench and picked up the board she’d left mid-repair the night before, a phone with a cracked digitizer and a battery that had swollen enough to lift the back cover half a millimetre off true, and worked the ribbon cable free with a spudger rather than her fingers, because the evening client window was already smaller than she wanted after today and there was nothing else on the wall that was going to get shorter by her looking at it. The swollen cell came out clean, and she set it aside in the metal tin she kept for exactly that, well away from anything that could puncture it, and reached for the replacement she’d pulled from stock that morning before school, before any of the rest of the day had happened at all. She seated it, ran the ribbon back through, and pressed the display down until the adhesive along the frame caught, and the screen woke on the first try, whole again, one more thing in the room that worked now and hadn’t that morning, which was as close to a clean scoreboard as the day had given her.
