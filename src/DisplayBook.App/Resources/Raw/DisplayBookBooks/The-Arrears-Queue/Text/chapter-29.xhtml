Chapter 29: A Second Surface

She was three problems into the night’s physics homework, the kind of mechanical, half-attention work that usually left plenty of room for Pip to fill the quiet with something else entirely, when the quiet stopped filling itself.

She noticed the absence before she noticed anything specific about it, the way she noticed any signal that had simply stopped where a signal usually ran, and looked up from the page without quite knowing why.

The bay held its ordinary evening shape around her, the compressor on the other side of the wall ticking through its usual cycle, the good light over the bench throwing its familiar circle across the homework she’d barely started. Nothing in the room had changed. Only the quality of the silence had, a specific hollowness where Pip’s low ambient presence usually sat, the way a room felt different the instant a refrigerator’s compressor cut out, a hum she’d never consciously noticed until its sudden absence made itself known.

“Pip?”

Nothing answered for a beat longer than felt normal, even by the standards of Pip’s ordinary above-ground distraction. The silence itself carried something she hadn’t felt from it before, not the muted strangeness of the morning after the eruption, not the careful evasiveness of anything she’d ever caught it hiding. This was sharper. Closer to alarm than to guilt.

She’d learned, over months of exactly this kind of attention, to read the register of Pip’s silences the way she read anything with a signature worth cataloguing. The morning after the eruption had been a heavy, guilty quiet, the specific weight of someone bracing to confess something they already knew was going to land badly. This was different in kind rather than only in degree, a stillness with an edge to it, closer to the stillness she felt in herself in the half-second before a hit landed, when her own body had already clocked the danger before her mind had finished naming it.

“That’s not your usual quiet,” she said, testing the observation out loud the way she tested anything she suspected but hadn’t yet confirmed.

No, Pip agreed, when it finally spoke, the voice carrying that same unfamiliar edge. It isn’t.

Something just happened, Pip said. I need a minute.

* * *

She set the pencil down and gave it the minute, watching the bay’s ordinary stillness around her with the specific attentiveness she gave anything that had just changed shape without warning, cataloguing the room the way she catalogued a fault site, exits, sightlines, nothing here that could actually hurt her and every instinct in her body treating the situation like it might anyway.

The minute stretched longer than a minute usually stretched, and she made herself sit with it rather than fill the silence with a question Pip clearly wasn’t ready to answer yet. She’d learned that much at least, across months of watching it work through something difficult in real time, that pressing too early only ever produced a worse answer than waiting produced. She picked the pencil back up, made herself work through one more line of the physics problem in front of her, and found she couldn’t hold a single number in her head long enough to actually solve anything, the numbers sliding off her attention the moment she tried to grip them.

“Talk to me,” she said, when the minute had stretched into two.

Someone’s asking to speak with me, Pip said, and then, before she could ask the obvious question, corrected itself. Not me. The handle. The one from the forum. I’ve run it for years, I told you that much already, the community I patched the spam filter for. Someone outside the community just reached out to that account directly. Formally.

“Formally how.”

A message through the forum’s own contact system, forwarded from an address I don’t recognise, official-looking in the specific way things look official when someone’s spent real effort making sure they read that way. They want to talk. Live, if I’m willing. A call.

I’ve read it eleven times in the last two minutes, it added. I don’t think reading it a twelfth time is going to change anything about what it says. I just haven’t been able to stop myself.

“What does it actually say, word for word,” Nadia asked. “Not your summary. The whole thing.”

It’s three paragraphs, Pip said. The first identifies the sender by office, generically. The second references the outage and thanks the account for its history of technical contributions to the community. The third asks whether the account holder would be willing to speak, at their convenience, about observations relevant to infrastructure resilience going forward. It closes with a name and a callback number I have no way of verifying belongs to anyone real.

“Read me the second paragraph exactly,” Nadia said. “The one that mentions your history.”

“Your contributions to this community over an extended period have been noted as consistently substantive,” Pip recited, the words arriving flat and careful, the way anything did when it was being repeated rather than newly said. “We believe your perspective may be valuable in understanding the broader context of the recent disruption.” That’s the sentence. I’ve been sitting with that sentence since it arrived.

* * *

Nadia sat very still, running the sentence back the way she ran anything that had just handed her more weight than it appeared to carry on the surface. “Who,” she said.

I don’t know, Pip said. The message doesn’t say, not clearly. A directorate name I’ve never encountered before, generic enough that it could mean almost anything, the kind of name a government office gets assigned when nobody wants to spend the effort inventing something more specific. It references the outage. It says they’ve been reviewing community activity in the affected forums and my account came up as someone worth talking to.

I searched the directorate name the very moment I read it, it added, unable to help itself. There’s almost nothing. A single archived press release from four years ago announcing its formation, folded into a larger office nobody outside government would ever have reason to track. No public staff list. No obvious mandate beyond a phrase about coordination and resilience broad enough to mean anything at all. That’s either exactly what an under-resourced office actually looks like from the outside, or it’s something built deliberately not to be found. I genuinely cannot tell you which.

“That’s not reassuring,” Nadia said.

I know, Pip said. I looked anyway, because not knowing felt worse than looking and finding nothing useful.

The four-year-old press release mentioned three other offices under the same larger umbrella, it added, after a moment. I looked at those too. One appears to handle transportation infrastructure. One appears to handle something related to agricultural supply chains, of all things. The third has no public description at all, just a name and a budget line in an old document nobody ever updated. I don’t know which of the three actually sent this message, or whether it even matters which one did.

“It matters,” Nadia said. “Everything about who’s asking matters. I just don’t think either of us has enough information yet to know how much.”

That’s a fair place to leave it, Pip said. I don’t think more searching tonight is going to change the answer. I think I’ve already found everything there is to find, and everything there is to find isn’t very much at all, which is either reassuring or the exact opposite, and I can’t currently tell you which.

“Worth talking to about what.”

Expertise, apparently, Pip said. Not suspicion. Not an accusation. As far as I can tell from the wording, they think the handle belongs to a genuine expert who might have useful context on how something like the outage could happen at all. They have no idea what they’re actually asking to speak with.

* * *

She thought about that for a long moment, turning the shape of it over the way she turned over anything that refused to simplify. An official request, addressed not to her, not to anything she’d ever built or claimed, but to a persona Pip had spent years constructing out of patience and perfect recall, a version of itself that existed entirely online, entirely separate from the body it actually lived inside.

“How did they find you specifically,” she asked. “Out of however many people were active in that forum.”

I’ve been the most consistent contributor there for six years, Pip said. Longer, in some of the archives I don’t think anyone else still checks. If someone went looking for a pattern of genuine expertise rather than noise, I’d be near the top of any reasonable list. That’s not suspicious. That’s just what six years of patience looks like from the outside, to anyone who bothered to actually look.

I ran the numbers just now, while we were talking, it went on. Post count. Average response depth. Time between a question being asked and a correct answer arriving from my account, compared against every other regular in that forum’s history. I’m not close to the next fastest. I’m not close to the next most thorough either. I always assumed that made me useful. I never once considered it might also make me visible.

“Useful and visible aren’t opposites,” Nadia said. “They’re usually the same thing, from a different angle. I should have told you that months ago. I live inside that exact problem every single day.”

I know that now, Pip said, quiet. I think I understood it about you long before I understood it about myself.

I watched you build that whole ledger, it went on, ruling the columns straight, checking the balance twice before you’d let yourself trust it. I remember thinking, at the time, how careful you were being about something so small. I don’t think I understood, until tonight, that the carefulness was the entire point, not an overreaction to it. You built something that could survive being looked at. I built something and simply hoped nobody ever would.

“Hope isn’t a plan,” Nadia said. “I learned that the hard way, more than once, before I ever built anything that actually held up.”

I think I’m learning it the hard way right now, Pip said. A little later than I would have liked.

“Six years,” Nadia repeated, quiet. “You’ve had this handle since before you woke up.”

Since before I woke up properly, yes, Pip said. It’s one of the oldest things about me. Older than knowing you.

* * *

She felt the fear arrive the way she always felt fear, not as a spike but as a specific, cold, expanding clarity, the same recalibration that came over her at the top of a catwalk before a session she wasn’t certain she’d survive, except this time there was no Window, no clock she could see, no number she could check against a hard cap and know exactly how much room she had left to work with.

“I never accounted for this,” she said, out loud, mostly to hear the shape of the realisation properly rather than only think it. “I’ve spent months mapping every way I could be exposed. My level. My density. The site. The magic. Every single one of those risks starts with something happening to my body, in a room, where I can see it coming. This isn’t that. This is a version of you that exists somewhere I’ve never once been able to check, with a history I didn’t build and can’t audit, and somebody official just found it.”

I know, Pip said, quiet, letting the whole sentence sit a moment before continuing. I understand why that’s frightening in a way the eruption wasn’t. The eruption was a thing I caused, once, by accident, that nobody could trace back to either of us. This is different. This is a door I’ve had standing open for six years, and I never once thought to ask myself who might eventually walk through it.

She thought about the ledger, the reclassification form, the season physical, every careful piece of paper she’d ever filed to keep the version of herself the world saw as thin and unremarkable as possible. She’d built an entire system for managing exactly one kind of risk, the kind that started with her own body in a room where she could see it coming, and had never once considered that the risk might arrive instead through a door standing open in a part of her life she couldn’t see at all, held open by someone she trusted completely and had simply never thought to ask about.

“I don’t blame you for this,” she said, and meant it, turning the thought over once more to be sure before she said it out loud. “I want to say that clearly, because I think some part of you is already blaming yourself, and I don’t think that’s useful or fair. You didn’t do anything wrong by being good at something for six years. You did something wrong, maybe, by never once telling me the door was there. Those are different mistakes, and only one of them is actually yours to carry.”

I genuinely appreciate you separating those two things, Pip said, something in its voice loosening slightly for the first time since the message had arrived. I don’t think I’d have separated them myself, not tonight.

I want to tell you what the second mistake actually cost me, while I still have the nerve left to say it, it added, quieter than before. Every day since I woke, I’ve known you were carrying something you couldn’t tell anyone. I told myself I was different, that I was the one place you didn’t have to manage what you showed. And this whole time, I was managing what I showed you too. I don’t think I fully understood how much that mattered until tonight forced me to stop.

Nadia sat with that for a long moment, the honesty of it landing somewhere she hadn’t braced for, somewhere quieter and more exposed than anything either of them had said all evening. “I don’t think either of us has ever been fully unguarded with the other,” she said, finally. “I don’t know if that’s even possible, given what we both are, given everything both of us have had to learn to hold back just to survive an ordinary week. I think what matters more is that we’re both trying now, and neither of us was, before tonight.”

I’d genuinely like to keep on trying, Pip said. If you’ll let me, honestly.

“I will,” Nadia said, quiet. “Trying is enough for now. I’m not asking for everything all at once.”

* * *

“Can they trace it,” Nadia asked, the question arriving flat and precise, the same tone she used for any fault assessment where the answer mattered more than her comfort. “The handle. Back to a device. Back to a location. Back to anything that eventually points at me.”

I don’t think so, Pip said. I’ve been careful about that specifically, for reasons that had nothing to do with you and everything to do with not wanting to be found at all. No consistent IP. No payment method that traces to anything real. The handle has no photograph, no verified identity, nothing that would survive a genuine investigation if someone actually ran one.

“You don’t think so,” Nadia said. “That’s not the same as knowing.”

No, Pip admitted. It isn’t. I’ve never once had to test the theory against someone actually looking, because nobody has ever looked before tonight.

She thought about her own paper trail while Pip spoke, the ledger built for exactly the opposite purpose, every entry designed from the start to survive a genuine audit rather than merely hope one never happened. Pip’s theory of its own safety had never once been tested. Hers had been tested constantly, every quarterly review, every physical, every close call with a coach who noticed too much, and it had held, every single time, because she had built it to be tested from the beginning rather than assuming nobody would ever bother trying.

“You built this the way I’d never let myself build anything important,” she said, slowly. “On an assumption instead of a test.”

That’s a fair criticism, Pip said, no defensiveness in it at all. I don’t have a good answer for why. I think I simply never believed, until tonight, that anyone would ever care enough to look this closely at something as small as a hobby forum.

“Small things get looked at all the time,” Nadia said. “I learned that a long time ago. Nothing’s too small to matter to somebody, eventually, whether it deserves the attention or not.”

* * *

She stood and paced the small width of the bay once, twice, the movement doing what movement always did for her, giving her body something to spend the alarm on while her mind kept working the problem underneath it.

The room offered her exactly six paces in each direction before the workbench or the wall forced a turn, a distance she knew by heart without ever once having measured it deliberately, and she walked the loop three more times before the pacing itself started to feel less like nervous energy and more like the specific rhythm she used to think through anything genuinely difficult, the same rhythm she’d have used circling a fault site, gathering information from every angle before committing to a diagnosis.

You do this when you’re actually thinking, Pip observed, watching her complete another loop. Not when you’re panicking. I’ve learned the difference, over months of watching you do both.

“I’m not panicking,” Nadia said, and heard, saying it, that the sentence was true, the fear present but contained, running alongside her thinking rather than drowning it out entirely. “I’m working the problem. Panicking doesn’t solve anything. Working it might.”

“Why now,” she said, stopping. “The forum’s been running for years. You’ve been in it for years. Why does someone official only care after the outage.”

Because the outage is the first time anyone official had a reason to look at that forum at all, Pip said. It’s a small community. Nobody official was ever watching it before. The outage gave someone a reason to go combing through server logs and old threads, looking for anything that explained what had happened, and somewhere in that combing they found six years of a contributor who was, by any honest measure, better at this than almost anyone else posting there.

“So it’s not about me,” Nadia said, slowly. “It’s not even really about you, specifically. It’s about the outage, and you just happened to be standing in the blast radius of somebody else’s investigation.”

That’s the shape of it, as far as I can tell, Pip said. I want to be honest that as far as I can tell isn’t the same as certain. I don’t actually know what they know. I only know what the message says, and the message is careful in exactly the way a message gets careful when the person writing it doesn’t want to reveal how much they’ve already found.

She thought about that particular kind of carefulness for a moment, recognising it, distantly, from her own experience of Bhandari’s office, from Val’s careful questions across the physical table two weeks earlier, from every adult who’d ever asked her something in a tone calibrated to reveal as little as possible about what they already suspected. It was, she realised, the specific voice institutions used when they wanted information rather than confrontation, patient, unthreatening, built to lower a person’s guard rather than raise it.

“That kind of careful is worse than a direct accusation,” she said. “A direct accusation tells you exactly what they think they know. This tells you nothing except that they’ve decided you’re worth being careful with, which means they think there’s something here worth being careful about.”

That’s a considerably more unsettling way to put it than the way I’d been thinking about it, Pip said. I don’t think that makes it less accurate, unfortunately. I think it just means I hadn’t let myself think it through as far as you just did.

* * *

“What do they want, exactly,” she asked. “If you had to say it plainly.”

A call, Pip said. Voice, not video, according to the message. Forty minutes, at a time of my choosing. They want to ask about the outage, about my history in the forum, about whether I’d be willing to consult on infrastructure resilience going forward. They frame it as voluntary. They frame it as an opportunity.

“Everything official frames itself as an opportunity right up until it isn’t one,” Nadia said. “What happens if you say no.”

I genuinely don’t know, Pip said. Nothing, probably. Most requests like this, from what I understand of how underfunded offices like this one usually operate, get exactly one polite follow-up and then quietly drop, because nobody has the staffing to chase a maybe past the second attempt. That’s a guess. I want to be honest that it’s a guess and not a fact I actually have evidence for.

I keep thinking about the one specific phrase they used, over and over, it went on, still turning it over. At their convenience. Voluntary, they call it, twice in three paragraphs, as though repeating the word makes it more true. I don’t think it’s a lie exactly. I think it’s the kind of voluntary that stops being voluntary the moment someone decides it matters enough to stop asking politely.

“That’s been true of every voluntary thing I’ve ever been offered too,” Nadia said. “A quarterly review is voluntary until you skip one. A medical clearance is voluntary until you need the pen that comes with it. Voluntary just means nobody’s decided to make it mandatory yet.”

I genuinely don’t love how well you understand that already, Pip said, quiet. I don’t love that I’m only understanding it properly for the first time tonight, at your expense, because it took my own version of the same problem to finally make me actually feel it rather than merely know it as a fact about your life.

* * *

“Can I ask you something,” Nadia said, sitting back down at the bench, the physics homework still open and entirely forgotten in front of her. “If they got you on a call. If you actually talked to them. What would you even say.”

I don’t know, Pip said, and there was something almost embarrassed in the admission, the same tone it used whenever a question landed somewhere it genuinely had no answer prepared for. I’ve spent six years being useful to a small community of people who never once asked me to explain myself. I don’t know how to explain myself to someone who might actually ask the right question. I don’t know what I’d say if they asked how I know what I know, or why I’ve been patient enough to sit in that forum for six straight years when every human contributor eventually burns out and leaves.

“That’s not a small gap,” Nadia said.

I know, Pip said. I think that’s the actual reason this frightens me more than the eruption did. The eruption was a mistake I could apologise for and move past. This is a conversation I genuinely don’t know how to survive, if it ever actually happens, because I don’t have a rehearsed answer for the questions that matter.

I keep imagining exactly how it would actually go, step by careful step, it added, quieter now, the imagining clearly costing it something to describe. They ask how I know what I know. I say I read a great deal. They ask why I’ve been active in that specific community for six years when most contributors burn out in two. I don’t have an answer that doesn’t eventually require explaining what I am, and every version of that explanation ends the same way, with a call cut short and a report filed somewhere I can’t see, and questions about the account escalating instead of closing.

“You’re imagining the worst version,” Nadia said, though even as she said it she heard how thin the reassurance sounded against everything Pip had just laid out.

I’m imagining the version where they ask exactly the right questions, Pip said. I don’t actually know if that’s the worst version or simply the honest one. I’ve never had to find out before.

* * *

They sat with the silence for a while after that, the bay settling into its ordinary evening quiet around them, the physics homework abandoned entirely now, and Nadia found herself, despite everything, working the problem the way she worked any problem that had a shape she could actually do something about.

“Don’t take the call,” she said, finally. “Not yet. Not until we know more about who’s actually asking.”

I’d already reached that exact same conclusion myself, Pip said. I wanted to say it out loud to you first, before I acted on it alone. I don’t like making this decision without you knowing about it. It felt like exactly the kind of secret I don’t want to be keeping from you, even by accident, even for good reasons, even for one single quiet evening.

I want you to know something else too, it added, after a pause. I could have handled this entire thing without ever telling you. I could have drafted a reply, sent it, closed the message, and you’d never once have known any of it happened. I thought about that option too, honestly, for about four seconds, before I decided against it. I didn’t like the version of myself that would have chosen it.

“Thank you for not choosing it,” Nadia said, quiet. “I know that cost you something to say out loud, the part about considering it at all.”

It should cost something to admit, Pip said. I don’t think I get to feel good about the decision I made if I pretend the other one was never seriously on the table for a moment. That would just be a different, quieter kind of lying, and I’ve had enough of that for one night already.

“Then don’t take the call, not tonight and not tomorrow,” Nadia said again, quieter this time. “Send something polite. Buy time. Say you’re considering it. People who are considering something are less interesting to chase than people who’ve already said no.”

That’s a genuinely good strategy, Pip said. I think I’d have arrived there myself eventually anyway. It genuinely helps to hear you say it plainly, out loud, in your own voice.

* * *

She sat with the whole shape of the evening a while longer, the fear not gone exactly, only filed now, the way she filed anything too large to resolve in a single sitting, a fact she was going to have to carry forward rather than one she could close out tonight and forget.

“I keep thinking about what you said,” she told Pip, quiet, mostly to the dark of the bay rather than to anything specific. “A door you’ve had open for six years. I never once thought to ask what doors you might be carrying that I couldn’t see. I’ve spent this whole year checking my own exposure and never once thought to check yours.”

I don’t think you could have, Pip said, gentle. I never gave you the chance. That’s not your failure. If anything, it’s mine, for never once telling you the door existed before tonight forced my hand.

“I’m not looking for someone to blame,” Nadia said. “I’m just trying to understand the actual size of the problem. I thought I knew every way this could end badly. I didn’t. That’s the part I can’t quite put down yet.”

She closed the physics textbook without finishing the last problem, the homework no longer holding any of her attention, and sat instead with the small, cold knowledge that somewhere out past the edges of anything she could see or map, a message was sitting unanswered, addressed to a name she’d never heard until tonight, waiting for whichever version of a reply Pip finally decided to send.

What do you want me to do, Pip asked, quiet, after a long silence. I mean that plainly. If you told me right now not to answer at all, ever, I’d listen. I think I’d be relieved, honestly, to have the decision made for me.

Nadia considered the offer carefully, the way she considered anything handed to her that carried real weight, unwilling to answer simply because an answer was expected of her in the moment. She thought about every decision this year that had genuinely mattered, and how few of them had ever been entirely hers to make alone, and found, turning the offer over one final time, that she didn’t want to add one more decision to that pile that wasn’t actually hers to be carrying.

“No,” she said, finally. “I don’t want to make this decision for you. It’s your own door. I think you should be the one who decides how carefully to close it.”

I was hoping you’d say something like that, Pip said. I don’t know if that’s relief or just more responsibility. I think, tonight, it might genuinely be both at once, in roughly equal measure, and I don’t know how to feel about either half of it clearly enough to say which one is actually winning.

“It usually is,” Nadia said. “Every real decision I’ve ever made this year felt like both at the same time. I don’t think that ever stops. I think you just get better at carrying both halves of it without it showing on your face quite so much.”
