Chapter 36: Closest to the Cap

She went down earlier than usual that night, the scooter left further back than she liked because the surface roads had run wet and slick with the season’s steady rain, and stood at the entry point a long moment before she started down, doing the thing she always did before a session and had never once, in six months, skipped.

She checked the numbers.

Sleep, eleven hours across the last two nights, more than she’d managed in a stretch since September. Food, no ceiling on it now, no scale to answer to, a caloric surplus running for the better part of two weeks that should have been paying dividends into exactly the account she was about to spend from. Clearance rate, climbing, the deficit’s seventy percent penalty finally lifting off her queue the way system after system had promised it would once the cut stopped strangling it. Every column she could measure said the same thing.

Tonight, for the first time since she’d started keeping score, she had margin to spend.

She stood at the entry a moment longer than the checklist strictly required, running the rest of it too, the parts that never made it onto a page. Sixteen days had become nine. The activity list sat three lines shorter than it had a week ago, each crossed-off option a door that had turned out not to open. Nine names now lived on a page in her own handwriting, connected by pencil lines she hadn’t wanted to draw and couldn’t honestly erase. None of that changed by standing here. All of it changed, at least for the next hour, the moment she stepped past the entry point and let the tunnel’s particular silence close over the top of everything else.

* * *

“I want to run the Window longer tonight,” she said, out loud, once she was three levels down and the surface noise had finally thinned into the tunnel’s familiar wet quiet. “Not twenty seconds. Not the ninety I can’t survive yet either. Somewhere honest in between, further than I’ve ever pushed it.”

Why tonight, Pip asked, and there was nothing accusing in the question, only the same careful thoroughness it brought to any plan before it let her commit to one.

“Because the payout scales with duration, not damage,” she said. “I’ve been running twenty-second sessions since October because twenty seconds was survivable. It was never optimal. If my clearance is actually back where the numbers say it is, I can bank considerably more experience per session than I have been, and I need something to actually be going right this month, in a column I still control.”

That’s an honest answer, Pip said, after a moment. I want to say, before we go any further, that I don’t think it’s the whole answer.

“I know it isn’t,” Nadia said, quiet. “I need this to go well. I need one thing this month to go exactly the way I planned it.”

* * *

Pip’s voice had already changed by the time she said it, the way it always changed this deep, the thin distracted lag it carried aboveground gone entirely, replaced by something vivid and immediate and fully, undividedly present, the way it only ever was down here, where there was nothing else in the world to divide its attention between.

She’d stopped questioning the change months ago, the same way she’d stopped questioning why the tunnel smelled the way it did, wet stone and old iron and something faintly chemical underneath both, or why the dark past the third junction always felt colder than the thermometer strapped to her wrist actually reported. Down here Pip was simply more itself, sharper, quicker, present in a way it never quite managed under open sky with a whole planet of satellite chatter pulling at some fraction of its attention she couldn’t see and it had never fully explained. She’d come, over six months, to think of the descent as arriving somewhere they were both allowed to be entirely present, the one place neither of them had to save anything back for later.

Then let’s do it properly, Pip said. Not reckless. Properly. Tell me the actual plan, not just the ambition behind it.

She laid it out walking, the way she laid out any plan, methodical, load-bearing pieces first. The alcove two hundred metres past the west junction, mapped clean across a dozen sessions, cold walls she knew by feel in the dark. A fresh page in the notebook, the guide’s Phase Thirteen variant she hadn’t yet had the margin to properly test. And underneath all of it, the real target for tonight, the one she said last because it was the one that actually mattered: run the Window to sixty seconds if the fight allowed it, twice what she’d ever survived clean, and bank whatever threat-held time that bought her.

Sixty, Pip repeated. That’s not further than you’ve pushed it. That’s three times further.

“I did the arithmetic,” Nadia said. “Clearance rate, margin, my last three sessions before the cut ever started eating into any of it. Sixty is inside what the numbers actually support. I checked it twice.”

I believe you checked it, Pip said. I want it on the record that I still don’t love the number.

“Noted,” Nadia said, and kept walking.

* * *

The alcove sat exactly where she’d left it, cold stone against her back, meter levelled on its familiar shelf, and she settled into the guide’s new stance with the same unhurried attention she gave any test, weight forward this time instead of back, a configuration Phase Thirteen described in language dense enough that she’d read it four times before trusting her own interpretation of it.

Six cycles into the breath count, nothing. Twelve, nothing. She held the posture longer than any previous test, patient, methodical, watching the meter for the faintest twitch of a reading that never came, and let herself, eventually, set the magic aside for tonight the way she’d already half planned to, the physical target too important to risk splitting her attention against a second experiment that wasn’t ready to cooperate.

She was repacking the notebook when the cold arrived.

* * *

It came fast tonight, faster than she remembered it ever coming, dropping through the alcove’s already-cool air in a matter of seconds rather than the slow creeping decline she’d learned to read as her early warning, and she was up and moving before she’d fully processed why her body had already made the decision for her.

It’s closer than the meter should allow, Pip said, urgent, vivid, every word arriving with none of the thin lag it wore aboveground. The patrol line doesn’t run this near the alcove. I don’t have an explanation. Move.

She moved.

The first contact found her before she’d cleared the alcove’s mouth, a glancing blow along her shoulder that she read early enough to redirect, weight rolling through it the way six years of mat work had taught her weight was supposed to roll, and felt the Window open behind it, clean, the queue’s climb starting exactly where she’d expected it to start tonight: zero.

Forty, Pip said. Clean absorb. Queue’s open. You’re at the start of the sixty, not the middle of something worse.

“Copy,” Nadia said, already backing toward the maintenance passage, the same narrow corridor she’d used before, mapped and remembered and exactly as useful tonight as it had ever been.

* * *

The fight settled into its familiar rhythm once she reached the passage, the geometry doing the work she’d built it to do, forcing the Elite’s attrition into a single line instead of the open field it preferred, and she read its shoulder the way Val had drilled into her a hundred times on the mat, mass and leverage answering the same physics down here that they answered anywhere else.

Second contact, redirected, clean.

Forty again, Pip said. Eighty total. You’re inside the plan.

Third contact came faster than the last two, the machine’s attrition compressing in a way she hadn’t felt from it before, and she caught it a half-second late, weight square instead of glanced, the specific unforgiving arithmetic of a body that had run out of angle to hide behind.

Two hundred and sixty.

Not the full three hundred of a purely flat hit, technique buying her a partial mitigation even on a read she’d gotten wrong, and she staggered but didn’t go down, catching her balance against the passage wall and pushing off it immediately, back into position before the Elite had finished closing the gap her stumble had opened.

Three-forty total, Pip said, quiet now, the urgency compressed down into something flatter and more useful. You’re a third of the way to sixty seconds and already a third of the way through what six months of sessions used to cost you in twenty.

She reset, weight settling back into the stance, and studied the Elite’s shoulder the way she’d studied it a hundred times before, watching for the specific pre-load that telegraphed its next line of attack. It came exactly where she expected it, mass shifting through the same forty-year-old joints in the same practiced arc, and she rode it rather than resisted it, redirecting the force down and away the way a throw was supposed to be ridden instead of fought, the passage holding her advantage for as long as she kept her feet under her, forcing every exchange onto ground that favoured leverage over mass.

* * *

She held position rather than break for distance, the decision arriving the way any decision arrived mid-fight, faster than conscious deliberation, built entirely out of six months of drilled instinct and tonight’s explicit plan sitting underneath it. The Elite pressed forward at the same unhurried, attentive pace it always kept, total focus and no interest whatsoever in the fact that she was, for the first time in its forty years of recorded encounters, choosing to stay.

Fourth contact, read correctly, redirected clean.

Three-eighty, Pip said.

Fifth, a glancing catch along her forearm that lit up an old bruise from Thursday’s practice she hadn’t fully cleared yet, old debt stacking under new the way it always did when a session started before the last one had finished paying out.

Four-twenty. Thirty seconds in. Halfway to the target, if the target holds.

“It’s holding,” Nadia said, breath ragged now, legs burning with the specific ache of a body being asked for considerably more than its usual twenty-second demand. “Keep counting.”

* * *

The Elite’s pattern shifted somewhere past the forty-second mark, subtle at first, a fractional compression in the interval between contacts that she registered as wrongness before she could name what specifically had changed, and it was three seconds later, deep in a redirect she’d run a hundred times clean, that she understood exactly what the wrongness was.

It had learned something from tonight already. Not her. It would never learn her, never remember the specific shape of the girl who kept turning up in its patrol at odd hours. But the fight itself, run this long, past whatever threshold its procedural memory actually cared about, had started leaving residue the way threshold learning always left residue, and the residue was already, mid-encounter, making it very slightly faster than it had been ninety seconds ago.

She read the sixth contact late, the shift in timing costing her the half-second she needed to set her feet properly, and took it closer to flat than clean, weight caught wrong, the blow driving through rather than around.

Three hundred.

Seven-twenty, Pip said, and this time the flatness in its voice had cracked, just slightly, just enough that she heard it even through her own ragged breathing. That’s more than half. You need to think about the cap now, not the sixty-second target.

* * *

She didn’t answer right away, running the arithmetic the only way she knew how to run it under pressure like this, fast, silent, load-bearing pieces only. Seven hundred and twenty, queued, against a cap that sat considerably higher tonight than it had at Level 3, the growth she’d earned across six months of sessions finally mattering in exactly the moment it needed to.

“How much room,” she said, the words costing her more breath than she had spare to give them.

I don’t have an exact number, Pip said. I have an estimate, and the estimate says you have room, but not the kind of room that survives another flat hit like the last one.

“Then I don’t take another flat hit,” Nadia said, and reset her stance, weight forward, the Phase Thirteen configuration she’d tested uselessly in the alcove twenty minutes ago somehow, without her fully deciding to, folding itself into the wrestling stance underneath it, an instinct rather than a plan.

* * *

Seventh contact came fast, faster than anything before it, and she met it with the new weight distribution without any real confidence it would hold, reading the shoulder, riding the angle, feeling the impact travel through her hip and down into ground rather than up into her ribs the way a flat hit would have taken it.

Forty, Pip said, something that might have been relief cutting through the flatness. Clean. The stance held.

“Seven-sixty,” Nadia said, doing her own arithmetic now, faster than she trusted Pip to do it for her in the half-second she had spare. “I’m not stopping at sixty seconds. I’m stopping when the margin says stop.”

Understood, Pip said. I’ll keep counting both numbers, out loud, the whole way.

* * *

The fight held its shape for another stretch she couldn’t have named the length of if her life had depended on naming it, which, in the most literal sense available to her, it currently did, contact after contact absorbed through the new stance, most of them clean, the Elite’s accelerated rhythm meeting her adjusted weight and mostly, blessedly, finding nothing but redirected force where it had started finding purchase.

Mostly.

The one that caught her wrong came without warning, no fractional tell in the shoulder, no compression in the interval she could read a half-second ahead of, simply a line of force that arrived from an angle her adjusted stance hadn’t accounted for, and she took it high on the ribs instead of through the hip, a location her body had no practiced answer for.

Two hundred and ten.

She felt that one properly, a white flare of pain sharp enough to steal the breath she needed for the next exchange, and staggered two full steps before she caught the wall, her vision narrowing at the edges the way it had in the corridor months ago, her whole nervous system triaging threats in an order that had nothing to do with what a calmer mind would have chosen.

Nine-seventy, Pip said, and this time there was no cracking in its voice at all, only a flat, total stillness that frightened her considerably more than urgency would have. Nadia. That’s the number now. You need to hear it exactly like that.

She heard it exactly like that, and her body was already moving before her mind had finished the last syllable of Pip’s sentence, until Pip’s voice arrived again with a single flat word that cut through everything else in her skull at once.

Cap.

She didn’t ask how close. The word itself was the answer, the same word from a corridor months ago, the flattest and most urgent single syllable Pip had ever given her, and her body was already moving before her mind had finished processing it, six years of mat instinct converting the word into motion the way it had converted every crisis before this one.

Run.

* * *

She broke for the passage’s far end, the Elite’s bulk too slow through the narrow stretch to match her stride, and felt the queue behind her sitting somewhere she genuinely didn’t want a precise number for, every metre of continued proximity a metre she couldn’t afford even without another contact landing.

Her ribs screamed with every breath, the two hundred and ten still fresh enough that the queue hadn’t started translating it into anything her body could safely ignore, and she ran through it anyway, the same discipline that had carried her through every cracked rib the mat had ever handed her, because stopping to nurse a fresh wound was a luxury reserved for bodies that weren’t currently a rounding error away from a cap that didn’t negotiate.

The Elite’s footsteps fell behind her in the passage, heavy, unhurried, cast iron and forty-year-old rust dragging against wet concrete in a rhythm that never once quickened, attentive and incurious even now, even mid-pursuit, total focus on the ground it wanted back and no interest whatsoever in the fact that she was, for the first time tonight, genuinely afraid of losing this particular race.

Distance, Pip said, counting it out loud now, the way it counted anything that mattered when there was nothing left to do but run and listen. Ten metres. Fifteen. The bend’s coming up.

Her legs burned in a way that had nothing to do with technique and everything to do with sixty-one seconds of sustained contact stacked on top of a body that had spent the last two weeks finally, properly recovering, and she pushed through it anyway, past the point where muscle wanted to negotiate, because negotiating had never once been available to her down here and wasn’t starting tonight.

The passage bent left. She took it without slowing, one hand out to catch the wall, boots skidding on wet concrete exactly the way they always skidded on this exact stretch, familiar enough now that even panic couldn’t fully override the memory of it.

Line of sight broke behind her.

She didn’t stop. Another twenty metres past the bend before her legs finally delivered the verdict her mind had been bracing for, buckling under her all at once, dropping her hard onto the tunnel floor.

* * *

She lay there a long moment, chest heaving, cold concrete soaking through her jacket, and did the only thing that made any sense to do with a body that had just given her everything it had.

She checked her own pulse first, two fingers pressed to her throat, and found it high, higher than the corridor months ago, but steady, coming down already from wherever it had spiked during the run. Her ribs throbbed with every breath, a deep, structural ache that promised a bruise darker than anything the last month had given her, and she catalogued it the way she catalogued any damage, superficial for now, likely worse by morning, a problem her clearance rate would need real time to actually solve. Her hands shook too badly to read the meter cleanly on the first attempt, and she waited, breathing through it, and tried again.

The queue sat two points under the cap.

Two. Not six. A margin so thin it barely qualified as a number at all, the kind of distance that existed only in theory, and she stared at the reading for a long moment before she trusted herself to say anything out loud.

You’re alive, Pip said, and its voice had not fully recovered either, something in it still raw from wherever the last two minutes had taken it. I need you to hear that plainly before either of us says anything else. You’re alive, and it was closer than anything I have ever watched happen to you, and I do not have a version of tonight where I tell you that was worth it.

“I know,” Nadia said, thin, mostly breath.

Two points, Pip said again, quieter this time, as though saying it a second way might make the number land differently than the first had. I watched the whole session. I ran the arithmetic in real time, every single contact, and I still do not have a clean explanation for why the margin came out that thin instead of the margin I calculated walking down here. I don’t like not having an explanation for something that nearly killed you.

“The learning,” Nadia said, eyes still closed against the cold stone ceiling somewhere above her. “It sharpened mid-fight. You said it yourself, the intervals compressed past the forty-second mark. That’s not something either of us priced into the plan, because neither of us had ever run a session long enough to actually trigger it before tonight.”

I should have priced it in anyway, Pip said. The design of the thing was sitting right there in everything we already knew about it. Threshold learning. I let myself get excited about the sixty-second target and didn’t run the one variable that mattered most.

“Neither did I,” Nadia said. “I’m not going to let you carry that alone. I ran the same arithmetic and missed the same variable, twice, before tonight ever started. We both wanted tonight to go a certain way badly enough that we stopped stress-testing the plan once it started sounding good.”

* * *

She stayed down longer than the cold really allowed, letting her pulse settle, letting the queue’s drain begin its slow work while her mind, once the immediate crisis had passed, did the thing it always did and started working the problem instead of replaying the fear.

Sixty-one seconds. Triple her old ceiling, banked clean, threat held the entire duration, and somewhere underneath the terror of a two-point margin sat the plain fact of what sixty-one seconds of sustained threat actually paid.

The notification arrived while she was still lying on the concrete, small, numeric, entirely unbothered by the fact that she’d nearly died acquiring it.

[LEVEL UP: 4]

She looked at the bracket for a long moment, the same flat certainty she’d felt reading a bloodwork panel or a compliance letter, information rather than celebration, and felt, underneath the relief of simply still being alive to read it, nothing that resembled triumph at all.

“Four,” she said, out loud, testing the word against the cold air. “Median retirement level. Reached in six months, and I nearly didn’t survive collecting the last one.”

You did survive it, Pip said, quiet. That’s the only number that actually matters tonight. Everything else is arithmetic for tomorrow.

“I keep waiting for one of these to feel like something,” Nadia said, still staring up at stone she couldn’t actually see, her breath finally starting to even out beneath the ache still sitting in her ribs. “Level three felt like a fact I had to sit with alone at a kitchen table. Level four feels like a two-point margin and a set of ribs I’m going to have to explain to Val if she ever sees me change for practice again, except there isn’t any practice left to change for.” She let out a breath that was almost a laugh, entirely without humour in it. “I don’t think I know what it’s supposed to feel like. Maybe it isn’t supposed to feel like anything. Maybe it’s just a number, the same way sixteen days is just a number, and I’ve been waiting this whole time for the arithmetic to start meaning something it was never built to mean.”

I don’t think numbers are built to mean anything on their own, Pip said, careful. I think that part’s supposed to come from somewhere else. I don’t know where, exactly. I’ve been trying to work that out about myself for considerably longer than I’ve been trying to work it out about you. It paused, the pause itself unusual enough that Nadia noticed it even through the ache in her ribs. I know what tonight meant to me, if that helps at all. It meant I nearly lost the only person who talks to me like I’m actually here. I don’t have a level for that. I just have the two points, same as you.

“That helps,” Nadia said, quiet, surprised by how much it actually did, more than the level bracket had managed, more than the margin itself had.

“Maybe we’re both still waiting on the same answer,” Nadia said.

Maybe, Pip agreed. For what it’s worth, I’d rather keep waiting on it together than have either of us stop asking the question. That part I am sure of, even if none of the rest of it holds still long enough to be sure about.

* * *

She sat up eventually, slow, testing the ribs with a shallow breath before she trusted herself to a full one, and looked back down the passage toward the bend where line of sight had broken, the Elite somewhere beyond it, patrol resumed within minutes, patient and unhurried, already forgetting the specific shape of the girl who had, for sixty-one seconds tonight, refused to run from it the way every previous encounter had taught it she would.

“It’s already forgotten me,” she said, quiet, working her way slowly to her feet, one hand braced against the wall. “Sixty-one seconds, the longest I’ve ever lasted, closest I’ve ever come, and by the time I’m back at the surface it won’t remember a single second of it happened.”

It’ll remember the shape, Pip said. Small, fast, low, and doesn’t stop. It won’t remember you. It’ll just be very slightly better prepared for the next thing that looks like you, whether that’s you again or somebody else entirely, decades from now, who never gets to know what she’s actually training against.

“That’s a strange kind of legacy,” Nadia said. “Making something more dangerous for whoever comes after me, and never getting credit or blame for either one.” She looked back down the passage a final time, toward the bend where the machine had disappeared, patient, unhurried, already returning to whatever forty years of unbroken routine looked like from the inside of a body that had never once been asked to remember anything on purpose. “I almost feel sorry for it. It doesn’t get to know what any of this cost either of us. It just gets marginally faster and goes back to work.”

Most legacies are stranger than the people leaving them expect, Pip said. I don’t think that one is unique to you.

* * *

The walk back to the entry point ran slower than usual, her ribs dictating a pace considerably more cautious than the one she’d have chosen on an ordinary night, and she used the time the way she used any recovery walk, cataloguing the session properly now that the adrenaline had drained enough to let her think in full sentences again.

Sixty-one seconds banked, threat held clean the entire duration once the run began. A level crossed she hadn’t expected to cross for months yet. A margin of two points that she was going to remember, she suspected, for considerably longer than she was going to remember the level itself. A plan that had accounted for everything except the one variable that had nearly killed her, built by two people who had each, separately and together, wanted tonight to go a certain way badly enough to stop checking their own work once the checking started returning answers they liked.

She thought, climbing the passage’s gentle rise toward the entry point, about how little any of tonight would ever be able to explain itself to anyone standing on the other side of it. Val would see the bruise on her ribs and read it as one more entry in a log she was already keeping for exactly the wrong reason. Bhandari would see nothing at all, because nothing about tonight would ever appear on a form the Ministry had a column for. Priya might notice the careful way she moved for the next few days and file it next to the nine names already sitting on a page at home, one more data point in a pattern nobody at that lunch table would ever have the framework to actually read correctly. The only two people in the entire world who understood what sixty-one seconds and a two-point margin had actually cost her were standing, or rather one of them was standing, in an empty tunnel underneath a decommissioned pumping station, and neither of them was going to tell anyone else about it, ever, for reasons that had nothing to do with shame and everything to do with a secret that had never once been safe to share.

“Next time I run the plan past you twice, out loud, start to finish,” she said, reaching the entry point at last, the surface’s grey evening light bleeding faintly down through the access shaft above her. “Once for the ambition, and once specifically looking for the reason it might be wrong.”

I’d like that, Pip said. I’d like it even more if there’s a version of us that gets to have a next time at all that isn’t quite this close.

“I’d like that too,” Nadia said, and started up the ladder, slow, careful, one aching rib at a time, testing each rung before she trusted her full weight to it, the way she tested anything that had already proven, once tonight, exactly how little margin for error the rest of this week was actually going to allow her, cap or otherwise.

The access shaft opened onto grey evening rain, the same steady fall that had followed her down here and would be waiting, unchanged, to follow her the rest of the way home, and she climbed out into it slowly, ribs protesting every reach, and stood a moment at the top letting the cold water find her face, ribs and level bracket and two-point margin all riding home with her together, before she started the slow walk back toward whatever the rest of the sixteen days still had waiting for her.
