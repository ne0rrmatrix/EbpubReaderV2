Chapter 30: The Pen

Val finally caught her after practice, the rest of the team already filtering out toward the locker room, and asked her to stay behind for a minute in a tone that told Nadia, before a single further word had been said, that the minute was going to cost her something.

“Sit down,” Val said, the same two words she’d used at the season physical, and Nadia sat, the same folding chair, the same fluorescent light, the room emptied out now instead of crowded, which somehow made the whole thing feel worse rather than better.

The gymnasium held its after-practice quiet the way it always did once the last stragglers had cleared out, mats stacked against the far wall, the overhead lights buzzing faintly in the specific register they only reached this late in the evening, and Nadia found herself cataloguing the room the way she catalogued anything before a conversation she suspected was about to cost her something, exits, the distance to the door, the particular angle of Val’s shoulders that told her this had been rehearsed rather than improvised.

Val set her clipboard down on the folding table between them, screen side down, a small deliberate gesture that struck Nadia as significant even before either of them had said anything further, the specific care of someone choosing not to let a printed sheet of numbers do the talking for her this time.

* * *

“I’ve been sitting with your panel for three weeks,” Val said, no preamble, the specific plainness of someone who had rehearsed this conversation enough times that rehearsal had stripped it down to only what actually needed saying. “I told you at the physical I wasn’t going to stop watching. I meant it. I’ve watched. Nothing about the picture has gotten better.”

“I feel fine,” Nadia said, the same sentence she’d given Val three weeks ago, and heard, saying it again, how much thinner it sounded the second time, worn down by repetition into something closer to a reflex than an actual answer.

“I know you keep saying that,” Val said. “I believe you believe it. I don’t think it changes what I’m required to do next.”

“What changed,” Nadia asked. “You signed my verification three weeks ago, right after the physical. Nothing about the numbers is different now.”

“Nothing about the numbers is different,” Val agreed. “What changed is that I’ve had three weeks to sit with them instead of twenty minutes at a folding table with a line of thirty other kids behind you. I signed that day because I hadn’t fully processed what I was looking at yet. I’ve processed it now. I can’t unsee it, and I can’t keep pretending three weeks of thinking about it hasn’t changed what I’m willing to put my name to.”

Nadia considered that, turning it over the way she turned over anything that revealed a decision had been building slowly rather than arriving all at once. “You’ve been building up to this conversation the whole time,” she said. “Not just today.”

“Since the day of the physical,” Val admitted. “I kept hoping I’d find a reason to talk myself out of it. I never did.”

* * *

“What are you required to do,” Nadia asked, the question arriving flat, the careful neutral tone she used for anything she needed to hear clearly before she let herself react to it.

Val didn’t answer right away, and when she finally did, she said it plainly, the way she said anything that mattered too much to soften, her eyes steady on Nadia’s the whole time rather than dropping to the clipboard still sitting face-down between them. “I can’t sign your supervised-activity verification again without an outside medical clearance. Not from me. From an actual physician, addressing the specific numbers on that panel directly, in writing, confirming you’re fit to keep training at this level.”

The sentence landed the way a hit landed when she’d misjudged the angle, all at once, no warning, no half-second to brace before the full weight of it arrived.

She kept her face carefully neutral through the impact of it, the same discipline that carried her through anything on a catwalk that had just gone worse than planned, breathe, assess, do not let the room see the number climbing. It was harder here than it had ever been underground, where at least the danger had a clock and a shape she could measure against a meter. This danger had neither. It simply sat in front of her, patient and immovable, wearing the face of someone who genuinely wished she didn’t have to say any of it.

You’re doing the thing you do at the site, Pip observed, quiet. Holding perfectly still while something lands.

She didn’t answer, watching Val instead, unwilling to spend attention on anything but the actual problem sitting across the table from her.

“You’re not signing,” Nadia said, testing the words out loud to see if saying them made them any easier to hold.

Val let the silence hang a moment before she finally answered, the specific pause of someone choosing her next words with real care rather than reaching for whatever came easiest. “Not without clearance,” she said. “I want to be exact about that. I’m not pulling you from the team. I’m not telling anyone anything about what I saw. I’m telling you I can’t put my name on a document saying you’re medically fit to keep doing this when I have four weeks of data telling me I genuinely don’t know that’s true.”

“You could keep me on the team without signing the verification,” Nadia said, working the distinction the way she worked any technicality that might actually matter. “The two things aren’t the same requirement.”

“They’re not,” Val agreed. “You’d still be welcome at every practice. But I’m not going to pretend that solves your actual problem, because I don’t think practice attendance is what you’re actually worried about right now.”

Nadia said nothing to that, because there wasn’t an honest answer that didn’t confirm exactly how correctly Val had read the situation.

“I looked into it,” Val added, quieter. “Before I called you in here. I know what that category feeds into. I’m not naive about what I’m asking you to solve in eleven days. I just don’t have a version of this where I sign something I don’t believe, no matter what it costs you on the other end.”

* * *

“Can I ask why now,” Nadia said, once the silence had stretched long enough that someone needed to break it. “Why not just quietly let the season finish first and deal with it after.”

“Because that’s exactly the kind of decision I’d be making for my own comfort instead of for you,” Val said. “Kicking the can to the end of the season doesn’t make the numbers better. It just means I’ve spent three more months signing something I don’t actually believe, and I’ve had enough practice this year noticing when I’m doing that to catch myself doing it again.”

* * *

Nadia sat very still, running the sentence through every filter she had, professional first, personal second, the way she ran anything that had just changed the shape of a problem she thought she understood. Val’s reasoning was sound. Val’s reasoning was, by every standard that mattered to a coach responsible for a sixteen-year-old’s safety, exactly the reasoning a good coach was supposed to apply.

She thought, briefly and uselessly, about every version of this conversation where the outcome might have gone differently, a coach less careful, less thorough, someone who’d have skimmed the panel at the season physical and signed without a second thought the way Nadia suspected most trainers actually did, somewhere on this exact team, in a hundred other gymnasiums across the province where nobody had ever once looked closely enough to notice anything worth pausing over. She’d have been safer, in the narrow sense that mattered to her secret, with a worse coach. The thought arrived and left almost as fast, because wishing for a worse coach wasn’t a plan, and she’d never once found any use in wanting a version of a person other than the one actually sitting in front of her.

It was also, without either of them naming it that way, the single most dangerous sentence anyone had said to her all year.

“How soon,” she asked. “Before you need the clearance.”

“The verification renews every four weeks,” Val said. “Yours is due in eleven days. I can’t backdate it. I can’t sign it early on good faith. I need something in writing from a doctor before that date, or I have to report the category as unmet.”

* * *

Eleven days. She turned the number over the way she turned over any deadline, already running the arithmetic before she’d consciously decided to start, the same reflex that had carried her through a hundred smaller crises this year and had never once, until this exact moment, felt quite this inadequate to the size of what it was being asked to solve.

She thought about every deadline she’d survived so far, the voucher’s eleven days back in September that had started the whole year moving, the review’s six weeks, the reclassification form’s narrow window before the roster locked. Each one had eventually yielded to the same basic method, break it into pieces, work each piece methodically, trust the arithmetic over the panic. She reached for that method now out of pure habit, the way her hand reached for a wrench before her mind had finished diagnosing the fault, and found, for the first time all year, that the method didn’t immediately produce a visible first step. There was no obvious piece to start with. There was only a locked door and a person standing in front of it who had every right to keep it closed.

You’ve gone very still, Pip said, quiet, present in the empty gymnasium in whatever way it managed to be present anywhere above ground, thin but attentive, tracking the conversation as closely as it ever managed to above ground.

Nadia didn’t answer, watching Val instead, cataloguing the specific weariness in her coach’s posture, someone who had clearly spent three weeks working up to this exact sentence and hadn’t enjoyed a single day of the wait.

“I know what this costs you,” Val said, quieter now. “I don’t know the details. I know enough about your file to know wrestling isn’t just wrestling for you. I’m sorry. I want to say that plainly too. I’m not doing this because I want to make your year harder. I’m doing it because I looked at that panel and I couldn’t find a version of myself that could sign your name to fine without lying.”

* * *

“What happens if I can’t get the clearance in time,” Nadia asked, the question arriving carefully, testing the exact shape of the trap before she let herself react to any part of it.

“Then the category goes unmet for this cycle,” Val said. “I have to report it that way. I don’t control what happens after that. That’s between you and whoever reviews your file.”

Nadia knew exactly what happened after that, the whole chain of it laid out in her head with the same clean precision she’d have used tracing a fault through a circuit, one broken connection cascading through every system downstream of it. A supervised-activity category unmet meant Bhandari’s next review flagged short. A flagged review meant a reassessment, the exact reassessment Bhandari had warned her about in a different office, months ago, back when eleven days had meant something considerably smaller than it meant right now. A reassessment for an unaccompanied minor with no guardian on file meant, by the system’s own established logic, a recommendation for congregate care.

Everything. The bay. The site. The scooter rides at four in the morning, out past the reservoir, alone, in the cold, the only real freedom she had left in a year that had steadily narrowed almost everything else about her down to a schedule. All of it sitting downstream of a single signature on a single form, and the signature had just been withheld by someone with every reasonable justification in the world for withholding it.

She thought about the ledger, ruled straight in careful black ink, built specifically to survive an audit nobody had ever actually run, a balance she still checked twice before trusting it, months after she’d first drawn its careful columns at a bench not unlike the one this exact folding table now reminded her of. She thought about the reclassification form, filed just inside a threshold that spared her a written justification. She thought about the physical, and the reservoir field meter, and every single document she had ever handled with the specific, deliberate care of someone who understood exactly how much weight a single piece of paper could carry when the person behind it had nothing else standing between her and a system that would otherwise swallow her whole. She had built an entire architecture out of paper this year, careful, load-bearing, tested at every point she could think to test it.

She had never once considered that the weakest point in that architecture wouldn’t be a form she filled out herself, but a signature she needed from someone else, someone with a conscience and a professional obligation and every reason in the world to eventually decide, on her own terms, that she couldn’t keep signing it.

You built the whole system to survive an audit, Pip said, quiet, as though it had been tracking the exact same thought all along. You never once built it to survive someone simply, honestly changing their mind.

“No,” Nadia said. “I didn’t.”

* * *

“Can I ask what specifically you need to see,” Nadia said, working hard to keep her voice level, the same careful neutrality she used for anything she needed to think through clearly rather than feel through messily. “On the clearance itself. What would actually satisfy it, specifically.”

“A physician’s note,” Val said. “Addressing the hemoglobin, the LDH, the haptoglobin, the CK. Confirming there’s a benign explanation, or that whatever’s causing it has been evaluated and doesn’t pose a risk to continued training. I’m not asking for a miracle. I’m asking for someone with a medical license to look at the same numbers I looked at and put their name on a conclusion, the way I’m not willing to put mine on one I don’t actually believe.”

Nadia thought carefully about what a genuine, thorough physician would actually find, given the same panel, given the same questions, given considerably more time and considerably more equipment than a volunteer nurse and a single folding table had ever offered her at the season physical. A real workup would ask the questions Val hadn’t had the authority to ask. It would order the follow-up tests Val had only been able to flag rather than perform. It would, almost certainly, find something Val hadn’t found, because Val had been looking at a single sheet of numbers and a doctor with real resources would be looking at the actual body those numbers came from.

She ran through the likely sequence the way she ran through any diagnostic path she expected to be forced down, an intake questionnaire she couldn’t answer honestly, a physical exam that would catch a density no ordinary sixteen-year-old carried, a repeat panel that would show the exact same anomalies Val had already flagged, and, somewhere past all of that, further along a road she had no real desire to walk any further down, a referral to a specialist with considerably more time and considerably less reason to let an unexplained result simply sit unexamined. Every path she traced ended somewhere worse than where she was currently standing, and she traced four more before she let herself stop, because a fifth path wasn’t going to produce a different ending than the first four had already shown her.

Whatever she eventually decided to do, she understood, sitting very still under the fluorescent light while Val waited patiently for some kind of answer, it couldn’t possibly be the honest version of what was actually being asked of her. She couldn’t walk into a real workup and come out the other side with a clean bill of health, because there wasn’t a clean bill of health that actually described what her body was doing. Whatever clearance she eventually produced would have to come from somewhere that didn’t look that closely, or it would have to be something she built herself, the way she built everything else this year that the world had never once been willing to hand her honestly.

She kept every single word of that reasoning carefully behind her teeth, because saying any part of it out loud, here, to Val specifically, would have undone in one sentence everything she’d spent the last four difficult weeks quietly trying to hold together.

* * *

“I need to think about how to get that,” she said, finally, choosing the sentence carefully, true enough to satisfy the room without promising anything she didn’t yet know how to deliver.

“I know it’s not simple,” Val said. “I know you don’t have a family doctor sitting on call, the way most of the kids on this team do. I looked into what’s available through your coverage. There’s a walk-in clinic two SkyTrain stops from Kelso House that bulk-bills, and a youth health centre downtown that specialises in exactly your situation, unaccompanied minors without a regular provider. I wrote both down for you.”

She slid a small piece of paper across the folding table, two addresses and a phone number in Val’s neat, economical handwriting, the specific care of someone who had clearly spent real time on this beyond the minimum the conversation required.

Nadia looked at the two addresses without immediately picking the paper up, reading the careful printing the way she read anything she needed to trust, cataloguing the handwriting itself for what it told her, no rush in the letters, no shortcuts, the specific unhurried penmanship of someone who’d taken the trouble to write it out properly rather than scrawl it fast on a scrap just to be done with the conversation and move on to whatever came next in her own evening. Val had clearly researched both places, not simply pulled a name from memory, and that fact alone told Nadia more about how seriously her coach had taken this than anything either of them had said out loud so far.

“The youth centre downtown is the better option, if I had to pick one,” Val added, watching her study the paper. “They see kids in situations like yours regularly. I don’t think you’d be the strangest file that clinic has ever handled.”

“I doubt that very much,” Nadia said, and something almost like a smile crossed her face before she caught it and put it away again, tucked back where the rest of tonight’s harder feelings were already waiting to be dealt with properly, later, once she had the privacy to actually let them land.

“Thank you,” Nadia said, and meant it, taking the paper carefully, the same careful handling she gave anything she needed to trust would survive the trip home intact.

* * *

“I want to say one more thing,” Val said, as Nadia stood to leave, “and then I’ll let you go. I’ve coached eleven years. I’ve seen kids push through things they shouldn’t have pushed through, and I’ve watched some of them pay for it later, in ways that had nothing to do with wrestling by the time the bill came due. I’m not withholding this to punish you. I’m withholding it because I’d rather lose a season than watch that happen to you specifically, and I don’t know how else to say that without it sounding like a threat, which it isn’t.”

“I understand,” Nadia said, and found, saying it, that she genuinely did, the specific ache of understanding someone’s reasoning completely while simultaneously knowing it was about to cost her everything she’d spent a year quietly protecting.

She thought, standing there with her bag over one shoulder, about every other adult who’d ever held real power over some piece of her year, Bhandari, the trainer’s clearance form itself, the review officer whose name she still didn’t know. None of them had ever once explained themselves this carefully, this honestly, before exercising the power they held. It was, she thought, a strange kind of comfort to be denied something so completely and still feel, underneath the refusal, that the person doing the denying genuinely wished there had been another way.

“For what it’s worth,” she added, quiet, “I don’t think you’re wrong to ask for this. I think you’re doing exactly what a good coach is supposed to do. I just also think it’s going to cost me more than you probably realise.”

Val held her gaze for a long moment, something unreadable passing behind her eyes, the specific weight of a person who’d clearly braced herself for a harder reaction than the quiet one she was actually getting. “I believe you,” she said, finally. “I wish I could tell you that changes my answer. It doesn’t.”

“I know,” Nadia said. “I wasn’t asking it to.”

* * *

She didn’t speak on the walk to the bus stop, the cold sharp enough now to sting in her lungs with every breath, the small slip of paper folded carefully into her jacket pocket where she could feel its edge against her ribs the whole way.

Eleven whole days, Pip said, once they were finally far enough from the school that talking felt private again.

“Eleven days,” Nadia agreed, low, still turning the number over.

What are you actually going to do about all of this, Pip asked.

“I don’t know yet,” she said, and the admission cost her something to make out loud, the specific discomfort of a problem she couldn’t immediately see a clean path through, the first one all year that had refused to resolve itself the moment she sat down and actually looked at it properly. “I know what I can’t do. I can’t walk into a real clinic and let a real doctor run a real workup on this body and expect the result to say fine. I’ve seen what my own panel looks like. A stranger with better equipment than Val had is going to see considerably more than Val saw.”

And if you genuinely can’t get the clearance at all, in time, Pip said, quiet.

“Then the category goes unmet,” Nadia said, “and Bhandari’s next review flags short, and I’m looking at exactly the reassessment she warned me about back in September, except this time I don’t have six weeks to fix it. I have whatever’s left after this cycle closes.”

* * *

The bus was nearly empty this late in the evening, a handful of other passengers scattered across the seats with the specific tired stillness of people heading home after a long day, and she found a seat near the back, away from the small cluster near the front, glad for the distance it gave her from having to hold her face together for anyone else’s benefit.

She sat with the whole shape of it on the bus, the cold glass of the window fogging slightly under her breath, and made herself do the thing she always made herself do with a problem this large, break it into pieces small enough to actually hold.

Piece one: she needed a clearance, in writing, from someone with a medical license. Piece two: whatever that clearance said had to survive a doctor actually looking at her, which meant the doctor either had to be satisfied by something less than a full workup, or the workup itself had to somehow come back clean despite everything her body was actually doing. Piece three: eleven days wasn’t enough time to solve either of those pieces the way she solved most problems, carefully, tested, built from the ground up with margin to spare.

She wrote the three pieces down in her notebook once she had them properly ordered, the same discipline she applied to any problem too large to hold entirely in her head, numbering each one carefully in the margin, and stared at the finished list for a long moment afterward, hoping a fourth piece might reveal itself the way a fault sometimes did once she’d finally gotten the symptoms written out in front of her instead of just turning them over silently in the dark of a moving bus. Nothing new arrived. Three pieces, no obvious fourth, and a deadline that had already shrunk by however many hours the walk and the bus ride had cost her since Val had first said the word clearance.

“I don’t have a clean solution,” she said, out loud, watching the city slide past in the dark. “I don’t think there is one. Every version of this I can see costs me something I don’t want to pay.”

I know, Pip said. I don’t have a clean solution either. I want you to know I’m going to spend tonight looking anyway, even without one, because doing nothing feels considerably worse than looking and coming up empty.

I keep thinking, over and over, about what a clinic would actually need to see, specifically, in order to sign off on something like this, it added, after a long pause. Not a fully clean panel. Just enough of an explanation to satisfy Val’s specific requirement, which is narrower than a genuine full medical clearance would need to be. Val needs a letter saying you’ve been evaluated and cleared to continue. That’s a considerably smaller target than actually curing whatever’s in your blood.

Nadia turned that particular distinction over carefully, more than once, the difference landing somewhere genuinely useful for the first time since Val had first said the actual word clearance. “A smaller target,” she repeated. “Not fine. Just evaluated. Those aren’t the same claim.”

They are genuinely not the same claim at all, Pip said. I don’t know yet how to close that specific gap honestly. I just wanted you to know clearly that the actual target is smaller than it felt an hour ago, even if I still can’t tell you exactly how to actually hit it.

“Smaller genuinely helps,” Nadia said, quiet. “Smaller is actually something real to work with. I’ll take smaller over nothing at all tonight.”

She let the thought settle for a while, watching the last few blocks toward home slide past the bus window, and found, turning it over one final time, that a smaller target was the first genuinely useful thing either of them had said since Val had first handed her the two folded addresses across that quiet folding table.

* * *

She let herself into the bay a while later, the ordinary stillness of the room offering none of its usual comfort tonight, and stood for a long moment in the doorway before she finally moved, the small slip of paper still in her jacket pocket, two addresses she hadn’t yet decided whether she could actually use.

She pinned it to the corkboard above the bench anyway, next to the wrestling schedule and the ledger and the reclassification form, one more document in a year that had never once run short of them, and stood back to look at the whole board the way she’d looked at it once before, months ago, four separate attempts to impose order on a year that had never offered any of its own. Five now, counting tonight’s addition, each one a small fortification built against a different piece of the same larger siege, and she wondered, standing there in the quiet, how many more the board would eventually need to hold before the season finally let her go.

Eleven days. She didn’t have a plan yet. She had, for the first time all year, only the number itself, sitting there on the corkboard, waiting for whatever she was eventually going to have to do about it.

She got ready for bed slowly, the day’s exhaustion finally catching up with her now that there was nothing left immediately demanding her attention, and lay in the dark a long while before sleep found her, running the whole evening back one more time the way she ran anything before letting it close. Val’s careful, rehearsed sentences. The small slip of paper with two addresses. The specific ache of being understood completely by someone who still had to say no anyway.

You should really try to sleep now, Pip said, gentle, once the silence had stretched on long enough that it seemed to finally notice she wasn’t actually resting at all.

“I know,” Nadia said, quiet. “I’m trying. Eleven days doesn’t get any longer just because I stay awake worrying about it.”

No, Pip agreed, quiet. It genuinely doesn’t, not really. I just wanted to say it out loud anyway, regardless of whether it actually helped.

She closed her eyes properly then, the corkboard’s new addition sitting somewhere just out of sight in the dark, a fifth document now governing a life that had never once, in her whole time keeping this bench, felt quite this thoroughly out of her own hands.

Sleep took longer than usual to actually arrive, her mind circling the same three pieces over and over even after her body had stopped fighting the exhaustion.
