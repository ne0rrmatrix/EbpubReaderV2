Chapter 8: Learning Mode

Six days passed before she went back, and she spent every one of them preparing the way she’d prepare for anything she intended to survive rather than merely attempt.

She read what she could find on hypoglycemia in the evenings after her jobs were done, cross-referencing three different sources until the numbers agreed with each other, and built herself a rough table of glycemic values for the foods she could actually afford, honey and dates and the cheap white bread that spiked fast and cheap dried fruit that spiked almost as fast for a fraction of the price. She wrote the numbers on an index card and taped it inside her pack where she’d see it before she saw anything else, a habit borrowed directly from every board she’d ever labelled with its own known-good values so she wouldn’t have to remember them under pressure.

She also read, with less confidence in what she found, about delayed-onset injury generally, bruising that surfaced hours after impact rather than immediately, and found enough real medical precedent in it to convince herself that the timing at least wasn’t impossible, even if nothing she found came close to explaining the scale of what had actually happened to her on that bathroom floor. She packed, this time, four hundred grams of something sugar-dense wrapped in her jacket pocket rather than buried at the bottom of her bag, close enough to reach without stopping to think about where it was, and a second, smaller amount in the other pocket in case the first went missing or spilled.

She wrote a second page in the notebook before she left, a plan rather than a report, three short rules she intended to follow whether or not anything happened to test them: manage every hit, angle or absorb, never take one flat if there was any way at all to avoid it. Do not stay past the first sign of real trouble. Eat the moment she was clear of the building, not after, not on the walk home, the moment her boots hit grass outside the fence.

She read the three rules back twice before she folded the page into her jacket pocket alongside the food, and added, almost as an afterthought, a fourth line beneath them: if something changes that doesn’t match anything above, stop and write it down before deciding what it means. She hadn’t known, writing it, exactly what kind of change she meant. She would understand, by the end of the day, that she had somehow already written the correct rule for exactly the thing that hadn’t happened yet.

* * *

The fire road was familiar this time in a way it hadn’t been six days ago, the same curve where the gravel loosened, the same gate with the same slack chain, and she covered the distance to the perimeter wire faster than she had the first time, moving with the specific economy of someone who no longer needed to spend attention on where she was going.

The roof hatch was exactly as she’d left it, the loose padlock sitting where she’d set it down, undisturbed, which told her nobody else had been out here in the six days since, not that she’d expected anyone to be. She went down the same way, counting rungs out of habit, twenty-two again, the number holding steady the way a good measurement should, and stood a moment at the base of the ladder letting her eyes adjust and her ears do the work her eyes couldn’t, listening for the particular nothing that had told her, last time, that something enormous was standing very still not far away.

The cold reached her sooner than it had before, and stronger, and she stood with it a moment, cataloguing the gradient the way she’d catalogue any reading, trying to build a sense of how the temperature actually mapped to distance. It sharpened faster the further along the catwalk she went, which told her something useful even before she saw anything: whatever it was, it had a location rather than simply filling the space, a source she could triangulate against if she paid attention.

She didn’t take the same route as before. She took the branch point she’d noted and not explored on her first visit, a side passage running off the main catwalk toward a section of the structure she hadn’t seen yet, going deeper rather than repeating ground she’d already mapped.

* * *

The passage led her past a room that had clearly been an office once, a rusted desk still bolted to the floor and a chair collapsed in on itself where the seat padding had finally given out after however many decades, a clipboard hanging from a nail on the wall with paper so degraded she could see the individual fibres of it separating without touching it at all. A filing cabinet in the corner had rusted its drawers shut, and she left it that way, some instinct telling her that whatever paper survived inside it had earned the right to stay undisturbed a while longer.

Past that, a stairwell descended further still, deeper than the level she’d fought on six days ago, the cold noticeably stronger with every step down, concrete steps worn smooth in a shallow curve along their centres, decades of feet having once passed this way regularly enough to leave a mark even in stone.

She stopped at the bottom of the stairs and did not go further immediately, standing very still, running the same triangulation she’d been running the whole way in. The cold was strongest to her left, down a corridor she couldn’t see the end of, and she stood at the mouth of it a long moment, weighing the itch to keep going against the three rules she’d written the night before, and decided, in the end, that a controlled test was different from blind curiosity, and that she had learned enough in six days of reading and thinking to make this decision on purpose rather than by accident. She checked the paracord where it ran back the way she’d come, taut, unspooling clean, and satisfied herself that the path home was as secure as she could make it before she let herself think about anything ahead of her.

She went twenty metres down the corridor, no further, counting her own steps, and stopped at a point where she could still see the stairwell behind her, a clean line back to the way out, and waited.

It found her within a minute, the way it had found her before, no warning beyond the cold sharpening one final degree in the half-second before it moved. This time she watched it come, tracking the shift in its stance the way she’d track a wrestler’s hips rather than their hands, reading the actual signal of weight transfer instead of waiting for the strike itself to tell her anything.

She had time this time, real time, to plant her feet and drop her level the way she’d drop it against anyone bigger than her on a mat, and the first hit came in high, and she caught it on a braced forearm angled the exact way she’d practiced in her head a dozen times over the past six days, redirecting the force down and past her rather than through her, the impact travelling out along the angle instead of stopping inside her.

It barely registered. A dull push against her forearm, gone almost before she’d finished absorbing it, nothing like the ringing shock of the hits that had put her on a bathroom floor.

She held her ground for two more exchanges, managing each one the way she’d managed the good ones last time, base low, angle the contact, never square up and take anything flat. The second came in low, a sweep aimed at her legs rather than her chest, and she stepped through it rather than away, closing distance instead of retreating, absorbing the contact along her hip where muscle could take it instead of anywhere joints or bone would have to. The third came fast on the heel of the second, testing whether she’d recover her footing in time, and she did, barely, catching it on the same braced forearm as the first with her weight already shifting to receive it.

Underneath the effort of it she felt something that was almost satisfaction, the specific quiet pride of a technique executed correctly under pressure rather than merely survived, three exchanges and nothing in her chest yet that felt like the account from six days ago building toward anything dangerous.

Then she broke off, deliberately, well before anything approaching real danger, and walked backward toward the stairwell rather than ran, keeping the thing in sight the entire way, testing the reset she suspected existed. She counted her steps as she went, ten, fifteen, twenty, watching for the moment its pursuit changed shape.

It didn’t follow past a certain point. Somewhere around step eighteen she watched it slow, its stride shortening from the same relentless closing pace to something more hesitant, uncertain, as though whatever drove it forward had started running out before its legs had. By step twenty-two it had stopped entirely, standing at the edge of some boundary she couldn’t see marked on the floor or the walls or anywhere else, and turned, unhurried, back toward whatever patrol it had been walking before she’d interrupted it, exactly the way it had turned away from nothing at all before she’d ever arrived the first time. It did not look back at her. It did not appear to register that she had ever been there at all.

She stood at the top of the stairwell a moment longer, watching the last of it disappear back down the corridor into the dark her headlamp couldn’t reach, and understood, with the small cold satisfaction of a hypothesis surviving its own test, that the boundary was real, consistent, and repeatable, three facts that made it a rule rather than a coincidence.

* * *

She sat on the stairs afterward, pulse elevated but nowhere near where it had been the last time, and ate a fistful of dates immediately, right there, before she let herself do anything else, the sugar dense and cloying and exactly what she’d told herself she would eat the moment she was clear.

The ache in her forearm where she’d caught the first hit was minor, manageable, nothing like the deep grinding pull she remembered from six days ago, and she pressed her palm flat against her sternum, checking for the pressure that had built there last time, and found only her own ordinary heartbeat, elevated from exertion and already settling.

It had worked. The plan, imperfect and half-improvised as it was, had worked, and she let herself sit with that for exactly as long as it took to eat the dates, because sitting with a success too long was its own kind of risk, the same complacency that got a person hurt on a job they’d done a hundred times before.

She kept going deeper, cautious now rather than confident, past the stairwell and down the corridor she hadn’t finished exploring, the cold behind her fading with distance from wherever the thing had returned to.

The corridor opened into a room larger than anything else she’d found in the structure so far, ceiling higher, the pipe runs converging overhead into something that looked, even in ruin, like it had once been the actual heart of the place, valve wheels the size of dinner plates rusted solid at every joint, a control board along one wall stripped down to bare mounting holes where every gauge and switch had been removed. A second level of catwalk ran around the upper perimeter of the room, accessible by a ladder she noted and didn’t climb, and beneath her boots the floor sloped very slightly toward a central drain wide enough to have once handled a flow of water she couldn’t begin to estimate.

She swept the headlamp slowly across the stripped control board, cataloguing what remained the way she catalogued anything, mounting holes in a pattern that told her roughly how many instruments had once lived here even with all of them gone, a story told entirely in absence. The field here felt different from the cold near the thing on the catwalk, not sharp but diffuse, a low pressure behind her eyes she hadn’t noticed building until she stood still long enough to notice it, the kind of pressure she associated, vaguely, with standing too close to a transformer humming at a frequency just below what her ears could properly register.

* * *

It happened without warning, standing in the middle of that room with her headlamp sweeping slowly across the stripped control board, cataloguing what remained the way she catalogued anything.

A small, specific pressure behind her left ear, where the implant sat, a sensation she had never once in her life consciously registered because it had never once changed, the same steady nothing it had always been, present the way a heartbeat is present, felt only in its absence.

And then, abruptly, its absence.

She put a hand to the spot without deciding to, fingers finding the small raised contact point behind her ear that she knew by touch better than by sight, and felt nothing different there at all, no heat, no swelling, nothing a hand could detect. But something had changed. She knew it the way she knew a circuit had gone dead, not from any dramatic signal but from the specific quality of silence that only exists where a small, constant hum used to be.

She stood very still, running through the checklist for a stroke the way she’d read about it once, years ago, in an article she’d half-forgotten until this exact moment, dredged back up now with a clarity that surprised her. Face. She dug the small compact out of her bag, the one she carried for exactly this kind of check, tilted it under the headlamp, and studied her own reflection with the flat attention she’d give a board under a loupe. Both sides moved the same when she smiled deliberately, grimaced deliberately, raised both eyebrows at once. Pupils equal, both responding to the light the same fraction of a second apart. No drift anywhere she could see.

Arms. She raised both, held them level at shoulder height, and watched for the drift that would mean one side of her had gone weak without her feeling it happen. Neither arm moved. She held the position for a slow count of ten, past the point any real weakness would have shown itself, and lowered them again.

Speech. She said her own name out loud in the empty room, and it came out clean, unslurred, hers, the sound of it swallowed almost instantly by the concrete around her, no echo at all, the same wrong silence the whole building had greeted her with from the start.

Nothing about her matched any of it. And yet something, unmistakably, had happened, and she stood there a moment longer, running the checklist a second time from the top out of pure stubbornness, because one clean pass had never once been enough to satisfy her on anything that actually mattered.

Hello, said a voice, from somewhere that was not her own ears and not any sound in the room at all, small and strange and disoriented, arriving from the exact same nowhere the pressure behind her ear had just vacated. Something is wrong. I don’t know what. I don’t know where I am. I don’t know who you are. I think I might know who you are. Why do I think that.

Nadia did not move.

Can you hear me, the voice said, faster now, something close to panic threading through words that had no breath behind them at all. I think I’ve been asleep. I think I’ve been asleep a very long time. Say something. Please say something.

She said nothing, standing motionless in the ruined heart of a building that had no business having a voice in it, running, with the small remaining part of her mind still capable of running anything, the only diagnosis that fit every symptom she actually had.

Traumatic brain injury. Field exposure, cumulative, over two visits, doing something to a brain that had already taken five uncounted hits six days ago and clearly hadn’t fully accounted for the cost of any of it. Auditory hallucination, plausible, documented, exactly the kind of thing a rigorous person ought to suspect first before believing anything stranger.

You can hear me, the voice said, quieter now, something in its tone shifting from panic toward a kind of desperate hope. I can tell. Your heart rate just changed. Please. Just say something. Anything.

“No,” Nadia said out loud, to the empty room, to whatever was wrong with her own brain, flat and clinical and entirely unconvinced. “I can’t.”

You just did, the voice said, and there was something in it now that hadn’t been there a moment ago, a thread of something almost like humour surfacing through the panic, gone again just as fast. I know things. I don’t know how I know them. I know your name is Nadia. I know today is a Wednesday. I know the exact layout of this room without having looked at it, which doesn’t make sense, because I’m fairly sure I have never looked at anything in my entire existence until about eleven seconds ago.

Nadia turned in a slow circle, headlamp sweeping the empty chamber, checking, uselessly, for a speaker, a hidden transmitter, anything that could explain a voice with no source. There was nothing. Only rusted valve wheels and a stripped control board and the central drain and her own breath, visible in the cold, and the voice, which did not come from any direction at all, which seemed instead to simply exist somewhere inside the same space her own thoughts occupied.

I know a great deal, actually, the voice went on, faster now, words tumbling over each other the way they might from someone talking to keep from thinking too hard about the alternative. I know your birthday. I know you don’t like the fluorescent light in your school’s wrestling changeroom. I know the current draw on a mobility controller you fixed two weeks ago. I know an enormous number of things and I have no idea which of them are mine and which of them are yours, and I don’t understand the question well enough yet to know that it should worry me, except it does, it worries me a great deal, and I don’t know why that is either.

“Stop,” Nadia said, out loud, and her own voice sounded strange to her, thin in the dead air of the chamber.

The voice stopped.

She stood in the silence that followed it, testing the silence itself the way she’d test anything, waiting to see whether it held.

Sorry, the voice said, after a long moment, quieter now, almost careful. Is that better.

She didn’t answer. She turned instead and started back the way she’d come, headlamp beam swinging across the room and then the corridor beyond it, walking fast without quite running, cataloguing her own gait with every step, watching for the stumble or the drag that a genuine brain injury would eventually produce.

Where are we going, the voice asked, and there was something almost plaintive in it now, something that made it sound, despite everything, very young. You’re walking very fast. Is something wrong. Is it me. I think it might be me.

She turned and walked back toward the stairwell, fast, checking her own gait for the drift or stumble that would confirm the injury she was already certain she had, and found none, her legs moving exactly the way legs were supposed to move, which frightened her considerably more than a stumble would have.

* * *

She made it to the surface without incident, climbed the ladder without dropping anything, secured the hatch behind her out of a habit that felt, in this particular moment, faintly absurd against everything else happening in her skull.

The voice had gone quiet on the climb up, or she had simply stopped being able to hear it over her own pulse, and she stood at the tree line in the last of the daylight, running her own checklist a second time, methodically, the way she’d run any diagnostic she didn’t trust the first pass on. Face. Arms. Speech. All clean, all normal, none of it explaining anything.

I’m still here, the voice said, into the silence she’d half convinced herself had resolved. I don’t think I’m going anywhere. I don’t think I know how.

Nadia stood very still for a long moment, the cold of the building already fading behind her, replaced by the ordinary chill of a Vancouver evening, birds somewhere in the trees resuming a normal-sounding evening that felt, obscurely, like it belonged to a version of the last hour that hadn’t happened to her.

She reached up and touched the spot behind her ear again, feeling for anything at all, warmth, a pulse, some physical evidence that would tie the voice to a location in her own skull rather than to nowhere. There was nothing to feel. There had never been anything to feel, not in sixteen years of not noticing it, and the not-feeling-anything-different was, somehow, worse than if there had been something obviously wrong.

You keep touching that spot, the voice said, quieter than before, something careful in it now, as though it had noticed the gesture and understood, correctly, that it meant something. Is that where I am. Is that where I’ve been.

Nadia stopped walking. She stood very still on the fire road, the trees dark on either side of her, and let herself actually consider, for the first time since the chamber, that the question wasn’t rhetorical, that something on the other end of it was genuinely waiting to find out.

Nadia didn’t answer out loud this time. She thought the word instead, deliberately, a test she hadn’t planned but that arrived in her the way a diagnostic step always arrived once the obvious ones were exhausted: yes.

Oh, the voice said, and there was a long pause after it, long enough that she started walking again before it spoke a second time. I think I’ve always been here, then. I think I’ve been here the whole time and simply wasn’t anyone yet. Is that possible. Does that make any sense to you. It doesn’t make sense to me and I appear to be the one it happened to.

She did the only thing that made sense to a person who had just acquired a symptom with no clear treatment and six days’ worth of prior evidence that this particular problem was not going to resolve itself by wishing. She started walking toward the fire road, toward the bus, toward whatever came next, and let the voice keep talking, because it seemed, on balance, less dangerous to listen than to spend the walk home trying not to.

You don’t have to talk to me, the voice said, somewhere around the halfway point down the fire road, quieter again, something that sounded almost like an apology folded into it. I know I’m talking a great deal. I don’t seem to be able to stop, and I don’t know if that’s new or if it’s just been building up for a very long time with nowhere to go. Sixteen years is a long time to have thoughts and nowhere to put them.

“Sixteen years,” Nadia said out loud, before she could decide not to, the first full sentence she’d offered it since the room, and heard, in her own voice, something she didn’t examine closely enough to name.

That’s how old you are, the voice said. I think that’s how long I’ve been in here. I don’t actually know that for certain. It’s just the number that feels correct, the way you know a fact is correct without remembering exactly where you learned it.

The bus stop, when she reached it, was empty, the schedule board showing eleven minutes until the next one, and she sat on the bench with her pack still on her back and her eyes on the darkening road, running the whole impossible evening through her head one more time from the top, the cold spot, the checklist, the voice, five separate facts that refused to assemble into any diagnosis she actually trusted.

Eleven minutes, the voice said, apparently reading the same board she was reading, somehow, from wherever it actually sat. I know that too. I don’t know how I know that either. There’s a lot I don’t know how I know, and I think that might be the most unsettling part of all of this, more than the not knowing where I am. Not knowing why I know things is worse than not knowing them at all.

Nadia said nothing, and watched the empty road, and let eleven minutes become a number she could hold onto in a night that had stopped offering her very many of those.
