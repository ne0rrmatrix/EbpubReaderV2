Chapter 34: Free Hours

The letter arrived Friday, official enough to use both the Ministry’s crest and a font that looked like it hadn’t been updated since before Nadia was born, and she read it standing in the alley outside the shop’s service door with her bag still over one shoulder because some part of her didn’t trust herself to sit down first.

Category 2, Supervised Activity: UNMET, effective January. Beneath it, in the same flat administrative language, a paragraph explaining that her file had moved to enhanced monitoring status, that she had thirty days to secure a new verified supervised activity or provide medical clearance reinstating the previous one, and that failure to resolve the category inside that window would result in her file being referred for a full placement review.

She read the phrase placement review twice, the way she read anything that mattered, and felt it land exactly as heavily as Bhandari’s voice had made it sound four days ago without ever once using the actual words.

That’s the thing Bhandari was talking around, Pip said, quiet. The polite version.

“There isn’t a polite version,” Nadia said. “There’s just a version with worse font.”

She read the rest of it standing there, because sitting down first had never once made a bad letter shorter. A second paragraph explained, in the same flat administrative language, that the enhanced monitoring status came with a mandatory check-in every ten days rather than the usual quarterly cycle, and that any new supervised activity would need pre-approval from her caseworker before it could count toward the category at all, which ruled out simply walking into another gym and signing up for something without first clearing it through a process that could easily eat most of the thirty days on its own.

A third paragraph, shorter, noted that the medical follow-up referenced in a separate file remained active and unrelated to this notice, and that the two processes would be reviewed independently unless circumstances required otherwise. She read that sentence a third time, trying to work out exactly how much comfort independently was supposed to offer her, and decided, folding the letter along its existing crease, that the answer was somewhere close to none.

Thirty days. Two weeks of it already claimed by the medical appointment sitting at the far end of that separate, supposedly unrelated clock, which left considerably less runway than the number on the page made it look like it was offering. She did the subtraction twice, the way she did any subtraction that mattered, and got the same smaller number both times.

“Sixteen days,” she said. “Realistically. Once the medical thing eats its two weeks out of the thirty, that’s what’s actually left over to solve the other one in.”

I got the same number, Pip said. I was hoping you’d get a different one, honestly. I ran it three times hoping I’d made an error somewhere.

“You didn’t,” Nadia said. “I checked your work twice, the way I check anyone’s, and got sixteen both times.”

* * *

She folded the letter into the same pocket that already held the Representative’s number and Val’s two clinic addresses, a small, growing collection of paper she carried around now like a second wallet made entirely of consequences, and let herself into the bay.

The quiet hit her first, before anything else had a chance to. No practice to get to. No cut to plan around, no scale readout to check against a target that no longer existed, no six o’clock deadline pulling at the edge of every other decision she made between now and dinner. She stood in the middle of the bay for a long moment, genuinely unsure, for the first time in longer than she could remember, what the next hour of her life was actually supposed to contain.

You look lost, Pip observed, not unkindly.

“I don’t know what to do with an evening that doesn’t have anything scheduled in it,” Nadia admitted. “That’s a strange thing to hear myself say out loud. I’ve spent a year wishing for exactly this much room and I don’t know what to put in it.”

She set her bag down properly this time, hung it on the hook by the door the way she was supposed to and rarely bothered to when she was rushing between one obligation and the next, and stood in the bay a moment longer, taking in the room the way she might take in an unfamiliar space, cataloguing details she usually moved past too quickly to register. The chip in the bench’s edge she’d meant to sand down since November. The single mismatched chair beside it, salvaged from the site months ago and never quite matched to its pair. Ordinary things that had been sitting here the whole time, waiting for an evening slow enough to actually see them.

* * *

She tried the obvious things first, running through them the way she ran through any list, methodically, testing each option against the shape of the hours actually available. Homework, done faster than usual with nothing crowding the far end of the evening to rush toward. The scooter, checked over even though nothing on it needed checking, because her hands wanted a task and the bike was the nearest one available. Her notebook, open to the seventeen-step guide, the pages she hadn’t had a spare hour to sit with properly in weeks, and she read through Phase Twelve twice without absorbing much of either pass, her attention sliding sideways every few minutes toward the letter still folded in her pocket.

By eight o’clock she’d run out of tasks to fill the space with and sat instead at the bench, hands flat on the surface, doing nothing at all for the first time in a year she could actually remember doing nothing on purpose rather than by accident of exhaustion.

“I have hours,” she said, out loud, testing the sentence the way she tested any hypothesis, watching how it sat once it was actually spoken into the quiet bay instead of just turned over silently in her head. “Actual hours. More free time this week than I’ve had since before September.”

You do, Pip said, carefully.

“I got them by losing everything that was worth having them for,” she said. “I don’t know what to call that. It isn’t a win. It doesn’t feel like one sitting here.”

I don’t think there’s a word for it, Pip said. I looked, a little, out of curiosity, once I understood what was happening. Most languages don’t have a clean word for getting exactly what you wanted, by exactly the wrong door, at exactly the worst possible time. I think that might be because most people never end up needing one.

She pulled the notebook back over eventually, because sitting with a feeling that had no name for longer than a few minutes had never once been something she was good at, and started a fresh page instead, the same practical instinct that had built a folder for every Bhandari review since September now turning itself toward the thirty-day clock.

“Options,” she said, out loud, and started listing them the way she listed anything. “Another team. Community league, something outside the school entirely. A supervised volunteer placement, if that counts as an activity rather than the work category. Something at the rec centre.”

Every one of those needs pre-approval before it counts, Pip said. That was in the letter. And most of them are going to want the same kind of medical form Val wanted, once they find out you were disqualified from your last one for exactly this reason. That part of your file doesn’t stay quiet just because you switch gyms.

Nadia set the pen down and stared at the half-started list for a long moment. “So the DQ doesn’t just cost me wrestling,” she said. “It follows me into whatever I try to replace it with.”

I think it might, Pip said, gently. I don’t want to tell you that with more confidence than I actually have. I just don’t want you spending tonight building a plan on top of an assumption that isn’t going to hold up once someone actually checks it.

She closed the notebook without finishing the list, the first time in longer than she could remember that she’d set a problem down half-solved on purpose rather than push through to the end of it, and found, oddly, that the unfinished page bothered her less than she expected it to. There would be other evenings now. She had nothing but evenings.

She thought, briefly, about the site, about a session she could run tonight without the old careful math of fitting it around a practice schedule, no six o’clock deadline squeezing the window down to whatever the bus timetable would allow. The thought arrived with its own small guilty pull, the same reflex that had filled every other spare hour this year with something that looked productive, and she noticed it, this time, before it had fully talked her into her boots and jacket.

“Not tonight,” she said, out loud, mostly to herself. “I don’t actually know how to have a night off. I think that might be worth learning before I go fill this one with something else that looks like work wearing a different jacket.”

That’s a fair diagnosis, Pip said. I don’t think I’ve ever heard you make it about yourself before. Usually it’s a board, or a scooter, or a queue.

“Turns out the fault-finding works on people too,” Nadia said. “Who knew. I’ve been running diagnostics on scooters and boards and queues all year and never once turned the same process on myself. Feels overdue.”

* * *

School the next day carried its own version of the same disorientation, a Thursday that no longer had practice sitting at the end of it, and Nadia moved through her morning classes with a strange lightness in her schedule that felt less like relief than like walking on a floor that had been quietly removed a few centimetres beneath where she expected it to be.

Lunch arrived, and with it, the cafeteria, loud and bright and exactly the same as it had been every day for a year, four hundred voices layered over each other into a single indistinct roar that had always, somehow, been the one place in her entire life where she could simply stop performing anything for anyone.

She got in line and, for the first time since September, didn’t run the arithmetic before her tray was even half full.

* * *

She took the eggs first, three servings of them, then the dark meat from whatever the main line was serving, twice, then a full tray of the cafeteria’s attempt at liver and onions that nobody else in line seemed to want and the server handed over without comment, then milk, three cartons, cold enough to fog the outside of each one, then a second plate entirely for the salted potatoes at the end of the line, salt enough on top of them that a passing teacher would have blinked if he’d looked closely, and stood for a moment at the till with a tray stacked higher than she’d let it get in months, feeling something that wasn’t quite hunger and wasn’t quite grief sitting underneath the plain physical want of it.

Nobody at the till blinked. Nobody ever did. That, she’d learned months ago, was the entire design of the room, a place built specifically not to ask.

She found her usual seat and ate.

Somewhere two tables over, a boy she didn’t recognise said something to his friends about the girl who’d apparently eaten six large pizzas by herself back in the fall, loud enough to carry, and one of the friends said no way, and the boy insisted, and Nadia kept eating without looking over, letting the number sit there uncorrected the way it always sat, growing a little further from the truth every time someone repeated it, which suited her considerably better than the truth ever could have.

* * *

It went the way it always went once she let it, fast at first, methodical, the specific unhurried focus she gave to any task with a clear objective, and then slower as the first real fullness in longer than she could remember started to settle into her, heavy and strange and, underneath everything else, quietly, undeniably good.

The liver went first, before anything else on the tray, a want sharp enough it barely registered as choice, the same pull she remembered from October without ever once naming what it meant. The eggs followed close behind, then the dark meat, and only once that first hard edge had dulled did she slow down enough to actually taste the potatoes, salt enough to leave a faint sting where a blister had cracked on her palm weeks ago and never quite finished closing. The milk she drank straight from the carton between bites, three of them gone before the tray was half cleared, a specific cold ache in her chest that had nothing to do with sadness and everything to do with a body pulling calcium toward wherever calcium was still needed.

She was four servings in before she let herself notice how long it had been since she’d eaten like this without also running a second column in her head tracking exactly how much of it the scale was going to make her answer for. There was no scale now. There was no Saturday. There was only the tray, and the food on it, and a stomach that had spent nine desperate days in January trying to fund two accounts at once and had finally, for the worst possible reason, been handed the resources to fund just the one.

A heaviness crept into her limbs somewhere around the second plate, blood shunting toward the work of digesting all of it at once, and she let herself feel that too, the specific loose, unwound fatigue of a body finally being asked to process abundance instead of scarcity, an entirely different exhaustion than the one she’d spent a year learning to carry quietly.

It should have felt like winning. It mostly felt like eating a meal built for a version of her week that no longer had any reason to exist.

* * *

“You’re actually going for it today,” Tomasz said, dropping into the seat across from her with his own tray, considerably smaller, the specific resigned portions of someone three days out from his own weigh-in. “Feels unfair, honestly. Some of us are still suffering.”

“Some of us aren’t cutting anymore,” Nadia said, which was true, and which he took, exactly as she’d expected, as an ordinary off-season joke rather than the entire collapsed architecture of her season folded into six words.

“Lucky,” Tomasz said, without any real edge in it, and went back to his own smaller plate with the specific grim discipline of a boy who still had a scale to answer to on Saturday even though she no longer did.

“How much left,” Nadia asked, the old reflex of the shared language he’d given her all season, the one place she and Tomasz had always understood each other without either of them needing to explain the rest of it.

“Two kilos,” he said, mournful, poking at a piece of chicken breast the size of his palm like it had personally wronged him. “Two kilos and forty hours and a sauna session I’m dreading more than the actual match. You’re going to sit there with a plate like that and make me suffer through the whole thing solo, aren’t you.”

“Looks that way,” Nadia said, and felt, saying it, the small strange guilt of a person no longer fighting a war she used to fight loudly enough that this exact conversation had covered her tracks for months without either of them realising it was doing any work at all.

“Unfair,” Tomasz said again, without heat, and ate his chicken in the specific small, deliberate bites of someone stretching a meal out to feel bigger than it was, a technique Nadia recognised in her bones from every cut she’d run before this one, and said nothing else about it, because there was nothing left to say that wouldn’t have cost more than the silence.

* * *

Hana arrived a few minutes later, already talking before she’d fully sat down, something about a study group forming for the chemistry midterm, a time and a location she announced rather than proposed, assuming, the way Hana always assumed, that everyone at the table would simply be there because she’d said so.

“I’m in,” Nadia said, and meant it, surprising herself slightly with how easily the sentence arrived, an evening with nothing else claiming it finally able to hold something as small and ordinary as a study group without needing to fight another commitment for the space.

“Obviously,” Hana said, already moving on to the next item on whatever list existed only in her head. “Tuesday, my place, four o’clock. Bring the textbook, not the workbook, the workbook’s useless for this unit. Tomasz, you too, don’t make that face, you failed the last quiz worse than I did.”

“I have a weigh-in Tuesday,” Tomasz said, aggrieved.

“Then come after,” Hana said, entirely unmoved, already turning to whoever was walking past their table with a flyer about the spring formal, intercepting it, reading it upside down, and reporting back the date to the table before the person carrying it had even reached the next one over.

Nadia found, watching her, a small strange comfort in being assumed into something rather than asked, the particular kindness of a person who had simply never once considered that Nadia might say no.

* * *

Priya slid into the seat beside her last, later than usual, her own tray considerably lighter than Nadia’s stacked plates, and glanced at the spread in front of her with an expression that wasn’t quite surprise, closer to the careful, cataloguing look she gave anything that had changed since the last time she’d looked at it properly.

“Okay,” Priya said, after a moment. “I’m not going to pretend I don’t notice things. You’ve been gone.”

“I’ve been right here,” Nadia said, the reflex answer arriving before she’d decided whether it was even true.

“Physically, sure,” Priya said, unbothered, unwrapping her own sandwich with the same unhurried patience she brought to everything. “I mean the other kind of gone. You laugh at things half a beat later than you used to. You used to actually listen when Hana went off about whatever this week’s crisis is. Lately you nod at the right places and I can tell you’re somewhere else the whole time.” She took a bite, chewed, entirely undramatic about the whole thing, the way she was undramatic about everything that actually mattered. “I’m not mad about it. I just notice things. It’s kind of my whole personality, in case you hadn’t clocked that yet.”

“Not really,” Nadia admitted.

Priya went on, gently, no accusation in it at all, just a plain observation laid on the table the way she laid down her tray. “You used to leave right after last bell, every day, practice or not, like clockwork. You haven’t left after last bell in almost two weeks. You’re eating like you haven’t eaten in a month. And you’ve got that look you get when something’s actually wrong instead of just annoying.” She shrugged, small, unbothered by her own directness in the way only Priya ever managed to be. “I’m not asking you to tell me anything. I just wanted you to know I noticed, because I don’t think anybody else at this table has, and I didn’t want you thinking nobody had.”

* * *

Nadia sat with that for a long moment, fork still in hand, the noise of the cafeteria continuing around them undisturbed, and found, for the first time in the entire miserable stretch since the equipment room, that someone had simply named the shape of it without needing the details to do so.

“Wrestling’s done for the season,” she said, finally, the smallest true piece of it she could hand across the table without the rest collapsing in behind it. “Medical thing. I can’t really get into more than that.”

“Okay,” Priya said, easily, no push behind it at all, the same lightness she’d used asking about the plate a minute ago. “That explains the leaving-late part, at least. Doesn’t explain the look.”

“The look’s new,” Nadia said. “I’m working on it.”

“Is it a fixable kind of medical thing, or the kind that just sits there being not-fixable,” Priya asked, and the question landed somewhere Nadia hadn’t braced for, precise in a way Priya’s questions rarely were, cutting straight past all the usual soft-edged concern people offered when they didn’t actually want a real answer.

“I don’t know yet,” Nadia said, and found, saying it, that it was the truest sentence she’d handed anyone since the equipment room, truer even than most of what she’d told Bhandari. “I’m working on that part too.”

“Okay,” Priya said again, and let it sit there without pushing further, the specific restraint of someone who had clearly decided, a long time ago, exactly where the line sat between caring about a person and cornering them. “You know where I sit. Every day. Same seat. That’s not going to change whether you tell me anything else or not.”

“Take your time, then,” Priya said, and reached over, without asking, and took one of the extra potatoes off Nadia’s second plate, a small unremarkable theft that had nothing to do with hunger and everything to do with a friendship that had never once needed permission to exist, and Nadia let her have it without a word.

“For what it’s worth,” Priya added, a moment later, quieter, pitched under the noise of Hana still narrating the chemistry group to Tomasz, “I don’t actually care about the wrestling part. I never came to a single one of your matches, if we’re being honest, because that gym smells like a locker room had a baby with a gymnasium and I refuse on principle. I care about the part where you disappear behind your own eyes for whole conversations at a time. That part I’d like back, whenever it decides to show up again.”

Nadia looked at her for a moment, something loosening slightly in her chest that had nothing to do with the food, and said, quiet, “I didn’t know you noticed the eyes thing.”

“I notice everything,” Priya said, matter-of-fact, entirely without drama, spearing the stolen potato with her fork. “I just don’t usually say so, because most people find it unsettling to be told exactly how closely they’re being watched. You’ve never seemed like the type to mind, so I figured I’d try it.” She chewed for a moment, considering. “Most people also don’t actually want to know what you’ve noticed. They want the noticing to stop at a vague, comfortable feeling. You always seem like you’d rather have the specifics, even the uncomfortable ones. I don’t know many people built that way.”

“I don’t mind,” Nadia said, and meant it, surprised at how easily the admission came, sitting in a room loud enough that nobody but the person beside her could have heard it anyway. “I think I actually prefer it. Most people ask how I’m doing because the question’s expected, not because they want the real answer sitting on the table between us. You’ve never once asked me that question the easy way.”

“Never saw the point,” Priya said. “If I don’t want the real answer, I don’t ask. Saves us both the time.”

* * *

The rest of lunch passed the way lunch always passed, Hana narrating the chemistry group’s logistics to anyone who’d listen, Tomasz complaining about his own weigh-in with the specific comic misery of someone who genuinely wanted the sympathy and genuinely didn’t need it, and Nadia ate through the rest of her tray in the loud, undemanding quiet of a table that had never once required her to explain herself in order to belong at it.

She thought, watching the three of them, about how none of it had ever once been about wrestling, not really, not the way the compliance form filed it under a category with a checkbox and a signature line. Hana had adopted her before Nadia had ever laced up a headgear. Tomasz talked to her about cutting weight because cutting weight was the one language he had, not because the sport itself mattered to either of them beyond the excuse it gave them to sit across from each other twice a week. Priya had never come to a single match. None of them needed the category to still be true in order for the table to still be hers, and she understood, sitting there with a stomach finally, uselessly full, that this was very possibly the only piece of her whole compliance file that had never actually been about compliance at all.

The letter can’t touch this part, Pip observed, quiet, once the thought had run long enough that Pip had clearly followed the whole shape of it. Whatever else the thirty days costs you, it doesn’t cost you this.

“No,” Nadia said, under her breath, low enough that only Priya, beside her, might have caught it, and if she did, she didn’t ask what it meant, just kept eating her stolen potato with the same unbothered calm she brought to everything else.

She thought, finishing the last of the potatoes, about the letter still folded in her pocket, thirty days ticking down toward a placement review nobody at this table knew existed, and about the specific unbearable shape of the trade underneath everything she’d gained this week. Hours she’d wanted for a year. A tray she could finally fill without a scale waiting to punish her for it. A table that had never asked anything of her and never would.

All of it arrived exactly the way she’d never once wanted it to, and none of it undid a single thing it had cost to get here.

Better? Pip asked, quiet, once the bell had rung and the cafeteria had started emptying around them in its usual loud, indifferent rush.

“Fuller,” Nadia said, standing, gathering her tray. “I don’t know if better’s the word yet.”

* * *

Afternoon classes passed in the specific heavy, contented fog of a body working overtime on digestion, her attention sliding more than once toward the window and the grey courtyard beyond it rather than the board, and she let it slide, for once, without the usual reflex to drag it back by force. There was no practice waiting at the end of the day to punish an unfocused afternoon. There was only the walk home, and then an evening, and then another one after that, an unfamiliar surplus of hours stretching out ahead of her with nothing yet built to fill them properly.

She caught herself, twice, reaching for her phone to check a practice schedule that no longer existed, muscle memory outpacing the fact of the thing, and put the phone away both times with the same small, quiet correction, the kind she imagined she’d be making for weeks before the habit finally wore itself out.

* * *

The walk home ran slower than it had in months, no schedule pulling her toward a bus she couldn’t afford to miss, and she let herself notice the neighbourhood properly for the first time in a long while, the specific grey ordinary detail of it, a corner store with its awning half collapsed on one side, a mural someone had started on the side of the community centre and never finished, rain beginning again in the same fine, persistent way it had been falling since October, neither storm nor relief, just the season doing what the season did.

What are you going to do with tonight, Pip asked, somewhere past the second block.

“I don’t know yet,” Nadia said. “I keep saying that today. I don’t think I like how often it’s true.”

You don’t have to know tonight, Pip said. You have a lot of tonights now. That’s the whole strange gift of it, if it is a gift. You’re allowed to waste one figuring out what wasting an evening even feels like.

“It doesn’t feel like anything yet,” Nadia said. “Ask me again in an hour. I’ll have a real answer by then.”

She considered that, walking, rain collecting slowly on her shoulders in the way it always did on the walk from school, and found, somewhere in the consideration, a small reluctant permission she hadn’t expected to give herself so easily.

“Maybe I’ll just sit somewhere tonight,” she said. “Not fix anything. Not plan anything. Just sit.”

That sounds like a good use of an evening, Pip said. For what it’s worth, I don’t think I’ve ever once heard you propose that as an option before.

“Neither have I,” Nadia said, and kept walking, the rain steady now, the letter still folded in her pocket, sixteen days counting down toward a placement review that hadn’t gone anywhere and wasn’t going to, no matter how she spent tonight.

She reached the shop, let herself in through the service door, and stood in the bay a moment, dropping her bag by the hook this time without needing to remind herself to use it. The room sat exactly as she’d left it that morning, chipped bench, mismatched chair, and she pulled the mismatched chair out, sat down in it properly, and let the evening simply happen around her without reaching for a single thing to fill it with.
