Chapter 18: Barely Participated

The letter came printed on Ministry letterhead, folded into thirds inside an envelope with a window that showed her own name through the paper before she’d even opened it, and said, in language that had clearly been drafted once and reused several thousand times since, that as a newly registered minor with an active Guild classification she was required to attend a Youth Orientation Clear, one session, mandatory, non-transferable, at a facility to be assigned.

She read it twice at the bench, turning it over once to check the back was blank, the way she checked anything the Ministry sent her for a second page she might have missed, some clause buried in fine print that would explain what a Youth Orientation Clear actually was beyond the name it had been given.

“I already have a supervised activity,” she said, out loud, to the empty bay, because Pip was somewhere in its inversion and the sentence needed saying to someone even if nobody heard it properly. “Wrestling covers that category.”

It’s not the same category, Pip said, arriving late and thin, its attention still mostly elsewhere the way it always was above ground in daylight hours. I looked while you were reading it the second time. This is a registration requirement, not an activity requirement. Every minor with an active class gets one, once, regardless of what else they’ve already got logged. It’s a box that has nothing to do with the other boxes.

“Of course it is,” Nadia said, and folded the letter back along its original creases, and put it in the drawer with the voucher paperwork and the review date Bhandari had written on a slip months ago, another piece of the machine that had never once asked whether she had room in her week for it, filed now beside every other form that governed a life it had never actually bothered to look at closely.

* * *

The facility turned out to be a converted community centre forty minutes by bus from the bay, a squat brick building with a Guild crest bolted above the door that had gone green at the edges from weather nobody had cleaned it, and a gymnasium inside that had been retrofitted, at some point in the building’s life, with a shallow instance access point along the far wall, the kind of installation that had clearly been expensive once and was now simply old.

Eleven of them showed up for the nine o’clock slot, all somewhere between fourteen and seventeen by the look of it, none of them people Nadia recognised from Kitsilano Secondary or anywhere else, a cross-section of the district pulled together by the same administrative logic that had pulled her here, a shared classification and nothing else in common. A few of them knew each other already and stood in a loose cluster near the gym’s far wall, talking with the specific over-loud confidence of people who had decided, individually and in advance, that today was going to be boring and were determined to make that boredom entertaining for each other. The rest stood alone the way she stood alone, checking phones, reading the single laminated instruction sheet taped to the wall twice as though a third reading might produce new information.

The contractor running the session sat behind a folding table near the entrance with a clipboard, a thermos, and the specific posture of a man who had run this exact session more times than he could easily count and had stopped, a long time ago, expecting any version of it to surprise him. He took attendance by reading names off a printed roster in a flat monotone, checked a box next to each one as it was confirmed, and gave a safety briefing that ran four minutes and covered, in order, where the exit was, what the panic phrase was for calling an early abort, and the fact that the instance had been swept and certified inert that morning by a Guild technician whose signature was on a form he held up briefly without actually letting anyone read it.

He’s said this speech enough times that he’s stopped looking at the room while he says it, Pip observed, quiet, present enough in the gymnasium’s echo to notice. I don’t think that’s laziness exactly. I think it’s what happens to any safety briefing repeated past a certain number.

“It’s what happens to any procedure,” Nadia said, low, mostly to herself, watching the contractor’s eyes drift somewhere over the group’s heads rather than across any individual face in it. “Doesn’t make the briefing wrong. Just means the person giving it stopped needing to think about it, which isn’t the same as the room stopping needing him to think about it.”

She catalogued him anyway, the way she catalogued anything she was about to trust her safety to, however lightly. Mid-fifties, a knee he favoured slightly walking from the table to the instance wall, a clipboard he checked against more out of habit than genuine attention, a thermos he refilled twice in the first twenty minutes from a machine near the door. Not unkind. Not careless in any way that would show up on a form. Simply a man doing a job the budget had shrunk around him, the same slow attrition Vancouver’s whole administrative apparatus seemed to be quietly running on, one field officer doing the work that used to need three.

His name tag read Osei, the plastic edges worn soft and pale from years of the same lanyard, and he called out each group number in the same flat register he’d used for the safety briefing, never once raising his voice above what the room strictly required. She watched him check the monitor at the wall between groups, a habit that looked thorough from a distance and, up close, amounted to a glance and a nod, his attention already halfway back to the thermos before the previous group had fully cleared the doorway. It wasn’t neglect exactly. It was a man rationing an attention span that had been asked to cover four times what it used to, the same arithmetic Bhandari ran on her own caseload, the same arithmetic the whole province seemed to run on lately, and she filed the pattern next to every other version of it she’d already catalogued this year without feeling the need to remark on it out loud.

A girl near the folding table asked him, while they waited for their group number to be called, whether the constructs in there could actually hurt anyone, and Osei said no, not in any way that mattered, and then, almost as an afterthought, that the facility hadn’t logged an injury requiring more than a bandage in the four years he’d been running the program, which he seemed to offer as reassurance and which Nadia filed instead as a number that told her more about how low the ceiling on this instance had been built than about how carefully anyone was actually watching it.

* * *

The instance itself matched every low-stakes description on the laminated sheet, a shallow training clear built and maintained specifically for exactly this purpose, cosmetically dressed as a collapsed parking structure with a scattering of low-tier constructs that reset on a fixed timer and had never, according to the contractor’s introductory speech, produced an injury requiring more than a bandage in the facility’s operating history.

They went in six at a time, the contractor watching from a monitor bolted to the outside wall that showed a flattened overhead view with none of the detail Nadia was used to reading off her own meter, blinking dots representing each participant and each construct, a system built for headcount rather than tactics. Nadia’s group went in second, and she found the instance exactly as advertised, low ceilings, dim emergency lighting, constructs that shuffled rather than charged and telegraphed every swing a full second before it landed, an environment built by someone who had clearly understood that most of the kids walking through it had never taken damage in their lives and needed the space to survive them making every possible mistake.

The other five in her group made most of those mistakes cheerfully and without consequence, one boy swinging wide at a construct and overbalancing into a support pillar, a girl freezing entirely at the first shuffling approach and having to be walked backward by a boy who’d apparently decided, unprompted, that he was in charge of her for the day. Nadia moved through it the way she moved through anything with a floor plan and a threat pattern, reading the room automatically, cataloguing sightlines and chokepoints out of a habit six weeks of a real Elite had built into her so completely she couldn’t have turned it off if she’d wanted to, and found herself, within the first few minutes, standing slightly to one side of wherever the group’s attention was pointed, watching the angles nobody else in the room had thought to watch.

She introduced herself to no one and nobody introduced themselves to her, which suited the morning exactly as well as it suited most mornings, and spent the opening minutes simply reading the room the way she’d read any unfamiliar floor, cataloguing who moved with some prior training in their legs and who was entirely new to having a body that could take a hit at all. The girl who froze, Jamie, moved like someone who had spent her whole life being told to be careful and had believed it completely, shoulders drawn in, weight back on her heels in a way that made every approach look worse to her than it actually was. The boy who’d appointed himself her minder moved with more confidence than skill, throwing his shoulder into swings that would have unbalanced him against anything with real weight behind it, the specific overconfidence of someone who had never once been made to answer for a mistake in a room like this.

The first construct rounded the near pillar within a minute of the group stepping off the entry platform, a slow grey shape built along vaguely humanoid lines with a single glowing seam down its chest marking, according to the laminated sheet, the point a strike needed to land to trigger its reset. It moved the way the sheet had promised, telegraphing a full second before any swing connected, patient in a way nothing at the site had ever once been patient, and the boy went at it immediately, shouting something to the rest of the group about taking point, and connected on his third swing to a small chorus of unearned cheering from two others nearby.

Nadia hung back from that exchange entirely, letting the boy have his moment, watching instead the pillar the construct had come from and the darker gap behind it where the overhead lighting hadn’t quite reached, filing it as a second entry point before anyone else in the group had thought to look for one.

You’re doing the thing you do at the site, Pip said, quiet, underground enough in the instance’s shielded structure to be nearly as present as it ever was in the tunnel. Positioning. I don’t think you’re doing it on purpose.

“I’m not doing anything,” Nadia said, low, watching a construct’s slow telegraphed swing land harmlessly against a raised shield one of the other kids had thrown up a half second too late to actually need it. “I’m just standing where I’m standing.”

You’re standing exactly where the third construct would need you not to be standing, if it came around that pillar the way the pattern suggests it might in about forty seconds, Pip said. That’s not nothing. I just want you to know I’m watching you do it.

She didn’t answer that, and simply kept standing where she was already standing.

* * *

It happened forty seconds later, close enough to Pip’s estimate that she filed the accuracy of it away for later even in the moment it was happening. The girl who’d frozen at the first construct froze again at the third, a slower shuffling thing rounding the pillar exactly on the line Nadia had already quietly claimed, and the boy who’d appointed himself her minder had drifted six feet away chasing a different fight, close enough to help and not close enough, and the construct’s telegraphed swing was going to land somewhere it genuinely shouldn’t, not dangerous by the site’s standards, not remotely close to anything the Arrears queue would even register, but enough, on an unlevelled fourteen-year-old with zero clearance and zero mitigation technique, to put her on the ground hard and end her orientation early with a story attached to it that would follow her through the rest of the program.

Nadia didn’t decide to move so much as she was already moving, the same way she moved underground when a hit was coming and there wasn’t time left to think it through first, a half step and a shoulder that put her exactly between the girl and the arc the construct’s swing was going to travel, absorbing a contact she’d already read as low-threat before it landed, the impact barely registering against a body that had spent six weeks learning to read hits three hundred points harder than this one.

The construct staggered back on its own reset logic, the girl stumbled a step and caught herself against the pillar, and the boy who’d drifted arrived a full second too late to have done anything at all, already saying he’d had it, already reaching for the girl’s arm to check she was okay, and neither of them, in the noise and low light and the general chaos of six kids and one lumbering construct in a room built for exactly this kind of confusion, seemed to register that Nadia had been anywhere near either of them a moment before.

She stepped back to where she’d been standing, resumed watching the angle the fourth construct would eventually come from, and said nothing.

The whole exchange had taken perhaps a second and a half from the moment she’d shifted her weight to the moment she’d resettled it, and she ran it back once, the way she ran back anything at the site, checking the physics of it against what she already knew. The contact had registered, distantly, as a number she didn’t bother converting, somewhere well under the forty-point threshold that counted as a well-absorbed hit against a real Elite, a fraction of a fraction of anything her mitigation training had ever needed to account for. Her shoulder would carry a faint bruise by evening, the kind she’d stopped noticing months ago, invisible under a sleeve and entirely beneath the threshold of anything she’d think to mention to anyone.

Jamie straightened up against the pillar, drew one long breath, and looked around the low-lit room with the specific relief of someone who believed, completely and without any reason to doubt it, that she had simply overbalanced on her own and caught herself in time. The boy reached her a half-second later, already saying he’d had it the whole time, and Jamie nodded along, already reconstructing the last two seconds into a story where his hand on her arm had been the thing that mattered, and neither of them so much as glanced toward the spot six feet away where Nadia had already gone back to watching the room.

Nobody saw that, Pip said, quiet, something in its voice that might have been the closest thing it had to satisfaction, or possibly just accurate reporting. Not the contractor, watching his flattened dots on a monitor outside. Not the boy, already three feet in the wrong direction. Not the girl, whose eyes were closed for most of it.

“Good,” Nadia said, quietly.

Can I ask why that’s good.

She considered the question for a moment while resetting her weight, watching the fourth construct begin its slow circuit around the far wall. “Because if anyone saw it, someone asks how a first-timer with a junk class read that angle before it happened,” she said. “And I don’t have an answer for that question that doesn’t open a door I need closed.”

That’s rule two, Pip said. It costs you at exactly the moment you can’t afford it. You just spent effort and attention you didn’t officially have to spend, on a session that was never yours to begin with, to save someone who will never know it happened.

“I know how the rule works,” Nadia said, quiet but firm, and moved on to the next construct before Pip could say anything else about any of it.

* * *

The rest of the clear ran out its allotted forty minutes without further incident, the group herding itself, gradually and without much grace, toward the exit marker at the far end of the instance, the contractor’s voice occasionally crackling through a speaker mounted near the ceiling with a timer update nobody was listening to closely. Nadia positioned twice more before the session ended, small adjustments nobody would have been able to name as adjustments even if they’d been watching for them, a half step that put her between a swinging construct and a boy reloading his stance at the worst possible angle, a shoulder turned just enough to absorb a glancing contact that would otherwise have caught a different girl square in the ribs.

None of it registered to anyone in the room as anything other than an ordinary session running its ordinary, mildly chaotic course. The contractor logged the group out at the forty-minute mark with the same flat efficiency he’d logged them in, checked boxes on his clipboard, and moved on to the next group waiting by the folding table without a single comment about how the previous one had gone, because as far as his monitor’s flattened view had shown him, nothing worth commenting on had happened at all.

Osei did glance up once as her group filed past the table, running his eyes over the six of them with the brief, practiced sweep of a man confirming nobody was limping, and his gaze passed over Nadia without pausing a fraction longer than it paused over anyone else, exactly as it should have, exactly as the whole architecture of the morning required. She caught the sweep happening and let it happen, standing at an ordinary angle with an ordinary expression, indistinguishable in every way that mattered from the five people she’d spent the last forty minutes quietly managing.

“Good group,” he said, mostly to himself, checking a final box, and waved them toward the exit corridor without looking up again.

Nadia sat on the gymnasium bleachers afterward, working through the specific bone-deep tiredness of a session that shouldn’t have cost her anything and had cost her more than the laminated safety sheet’s promises would ever have predicted, three small positioning saves stacked on top of a week that had already run her Arrears budget tighter than she liked, a level two body still learning what its own new weight actually demanded of it, and a calorie deficit that had been calculated around wrestling practice and a scavenged bench and absolutely nothing else.

You spent real resources on that, Pip said, gentler now, the gymnasium’s noise thinning as kids filed out toward the exit in loose clusters. Not much. But real. On three people who will go home tonight and describe this as an uneventful morning.

“That’s the point,” Nadia said, low, watching the girl who’d frozen at the third construct laughing about something with a friend near the door, no idea at all what the morning had actually cost either of them, no idea there’d been anything to cost at all. “If it was eventful, I did it wrong.”

I know, Pip said. I still wanted to say it out loud. Somebody should know what it actually took, even if it can’t be her.

Nadia didn’t answer that right away, and let the sentence sit, because it was, she thought, turning it over the way she turned over anything Pip said that landed somewhere she didn’t have a ready response for, probably the nearest thing to gratitude she was ever going to collect for the morning, and it came from the one witness in the building who’d actually understood what it had cost her to spend.

* * *

They went for food afterward, the way groups of teenagers apparently always went for food afterward regardless of what the morning had actually involved, a cluster of them drifting toward the strip mall two blocks from the community centre with the loose, unplanned momentum of people who hadn’t discussed it so much as simply started walking in the same direction at the same time. Nadia went along because leaving alone would have drawn more attention than staying, the same calculation she’d made at Hana’s dinner weeks earlier, and found a booth near the back of a burger place with three other kids from her group, the boy who’d appointed himself the freezing girl’s minder loudest among them, already turning the morning into a story before they’d finished ordering.

“That was so boring,” he said, to general agreement around the table, sliding into the booth with the specific relief of someone who’d survived an ordeal considerably smaller than he was making it out to be. “Like, actually boring. I don’t think anything even happened in there.”

“The one construct almost got Jamie,” someone else said, gesturing vaguely toward the girl who’d frozen twice, sitting at the end of the booth with a milkshake she hadn’t started drinking.

“It didn’t though,” the boy said. “I had her.”

Jamie didn’t correct him, and Nadia, sitting across from both of them with a burger she was eating steadily and without much appetite left after the morning she’d actually had, didn’t correct him either, because correcting him would have meant explaining a version of the morning nobody at this table had any framework to receive, and because the whole point, the entire architecture of what she’d done in that instance, was that nobody would ever need to know it had happened at all.

“You were pretty quiet in there,” the boy said, turning to her, the first time he’d addressed her directly all morning, in a tone that carried no real interest, just the reflexive social gesture of including a stranger in a booth once the conversation had run long enough to notice the gap. He said it the way he’d said everything else that morning, already half distracted by his own phone lighting up on the table between them. “Like, I don’t think I saw you do anything.”

“Mostly just watched, honestly,” Nadia said, which was true in the specific narrow sense that let her say it without lying, and reached for a fry rather than elaborating.

“Yeah,” the boy said, already turning back to the rest of the table, already moving on to a different part of the story where he was, in his own telling, considerably more central than the flattened overhead monitor would have supported. “She barely participated.”

Nobody at the table disputed it. Nobody had any reason to. It was, as far as any of them had actually seen, an entirely accurate description of her morning, a girl who’d stood near the edges and watched and occasionally moved out of the way of something, contributing nothing to a session that had, by every account any of them could give, gone by without a single genuine problem in it.

He’s wrong, Pip said, quiet, present enough in the daylight of a strip mall booth to say it at all, which she noticed, distantly, the way she’d started noticing when Pip made the effort. For what it’s worth. I know the record.

“I know he’s wrong too,” Nadia said, low, under the table noise, reaching for another fry. “That’s the whole design. It only works if he stays wrong.”

I understand the design, Pip said. I’ve watched you run it for months now. I’m just not always sure it’s fair to you, even when I agree it’s the correct one to follow.

“Fair’s not really on the table,” Nadia said. “It’s just what works. I stopped asking the other question a while ago.”

I know, Pip said. I’m just not going to let you sit here and let the record actually be wrong, even if nobody else in this booth ever gets to read it.

She let that sit for a moment, the burger cooling in front of her, the boy three seats down already telling a different, unrelated story to a different part of the table, Jamie still working on her milkshake in the small, quiet, unbothered way of someone who genuinely believed nothing had come close to happening to her at all.

It was, Nadia thought, watching the whole booth carry on around her without the smallest idea of what the morning had actually contained, exactly the trade she’d have chosen if anyone had ever offered it to her honestly. A girl safe and unaware she’d needed to be. A story that made her invisible rather than remarkable. A cost paid quietly out of a budget nobody but Pip would ever audit, and a record that existed, complete and accurate, in exactly one place in the entire building, a voice only she could hear, keeping count of a version of the morning that had actually happened.

Jamie caught her eye once, near the end of the meal, over the rim of a milkshake glass scraped nearly clean, and said, unprompted, that she was glad Nadia had been in her group, that some of the others seemed kind of intense about it and Nadia had at least seemed calm the whole time, which wasn’t gratitude exactly, wasn’t anything close to recognition of what had actually happened by that pillar, but landed anyway as a small, specific kindness that cost Jamie nothing to say and cost Nadia, briefly, the effort of not letting her face show how strange it felt to be thanked for the wrong thing entirely.

“You were calm too,” Nadia said, which was generous and not quite true and cost her nothing to offer, and Jamie smiled like she meant it, and went back to her glass, and the moment closed itself the way most moments at this table closed themselves, without either of them examining it any further.

The boy paid for his own burger with a card his mother had apparently given him for exactly this kind of outing, made a joke about the Ministry owing him a better lunch for a morning this boring, and the table laughed, and Nadia laughed too, a small practiced sound in the right place at the right volume, cheap to produce and entirely sufficient for what the moment required of her.

She finished the fries, said little else for the rest of the meal, and let the boy’s verdict stand uncontested, because uncontested was, in the end, the whole point.

* * *

The bus ride home ran long, a transfer delayed by a schedule that had been thin even before the district started cutting routes, and she sat near the back with her bag on her lap, running the calorie math for the rest of the day against a deficit the morning had widened more than she’d budgeted for, the specific arithmetic that had become as automatic to her as breathing, protein against a session she couldn’t log anywhere, a placement hour the letter had technically satisfied and would never once appear on any form describing what she’d actually done to earn it.

The cold had a bite to it now that hadn’t been there this morning, and she pulled her jacket tighter, watching the strip malls slide past, thinking about the letter still folded in the drawer at the bay, one session, mandatory, non-transferable, a box the Ministry would mark checked without ever once knowing what checking it had actually required of her. It would sit in her file next to the wrestling hours and the placement hours logged faithfully every week at Pearce’s, one more line of paper that told a version of her life with all the actual weight sanded off it, and she found, turning that thought over against the window, that she’d stopped expecting the paper to ever tell the truth about any of it.

You’re going to be hungrier than usual tonight, Pip said, quiet, the bus mostly empty this late in the afternoon, its attention drifting the way it always drifted this far from anything underground.

“I know,” Nadia said, watching the strip malls slide past the window, one after another, indistinguishable from each other in the flat grey afternoon light already thinning toward evening. “I already know what I’m going to want. Iron again. It’s always iron, lately.”

I noticed that too, Pip said. I’m still logging it. I still don’t have anywhere to put it either. Someday one of us will.

The bus hissed to a stop two blocks short of the bay, the driver calling the transfer number in a bored monotone nobody but Nadia seemed to be listening for, and she stepped down into a cold that had sharpened considerably since morning, her breath showing for the first time that week, the season tipping over some invisible line the way it did every year without any particular ceremony attached to the crossing.

She didn’t answer Pip, and walked the last stretch home in silence, a placement hour logged somewhere in a Ministry database that would never reflect what the hour had actually cost her, a construct’s glancing swing absorbed by a shoulder nobody had watched closely enough to credit, and a verdict standing uncorrected in a booth two bus transfers behind her, exactly as designed, exactly as it needed to be.
