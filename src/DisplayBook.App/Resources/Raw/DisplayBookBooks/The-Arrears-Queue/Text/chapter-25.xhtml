Chapter 25: What They Saw

Practice ran long that Tuesday, Val holding the team an extra twenty minutes to drill a takedown sequence nobody had gotten clean on the first dozen attempts, and Nadia left the mat with exactly the kind of tight window she’d built her whole week around protecting, forty minutes to shower, change, and catch the last bus that would get her to the site with enough evening left to run Phase Eleven properly. It was a light day. She’d marked it on the schedule pinned above the bench three weeks ago, in the same careful hand she used for anything she meant to hold herself to, and she’d been counting down to it since Sunday.

The locker room was already thinning out by the time she got there, most of the team having showered and gone while the drill ran late, and she found the quiet almost restful, the particular decompression of a room that had spent the last two hours full of noise finally allowed to empty. She peeled off her practice gear methodically, the same way she did everything, and did the mental arithmetic one more time as she worked, forty minutes to the bus, the bus itself twenty-five, the walk from the stop another eight, leaving her enough of the evening to run the full protocol without rushing any part of it, which mattered more than usual tonight because a rushed session was a session she couldn’t fully trust the results of.

She was three lockers into changing when she smelled it.

Not smoke exactly, not yet. Something hotter and sharper than smoke, the specific acrid tang of insulation starting to cook rather than burn, a smell she’d have recognised anywhere after six years of opening dead equipment and finding exactly this smell waiting for her inside the case.

* * *

The equipment room sat off the back of the locker area, a narrow space crowded with mats and spare gear and, in the far corner, an old space heater the district had never replaced, running on an outlet that had clearly been wired for something considerably lighter decades ago and never once upgraded since. Nadia was through the door before she’d consciously decided to move, the smell pulling her the way a fault always pulled her, faster than thought, faster than any decision she’d have described afterward as a decision at all.

The heater’s cord sat plugged into an outlet that had gone visibly wrong, a faint orange glow showing at the seam of the plate where no glow should ever be visible, the plastic around it already softening, and a thin curl of smoke lifting off the wall itself rather than off the heater.

She didn’t reach for the plug. Six years of repair work had taught her exactly what not to touch on a fault presenting like this one, a connection that had been arcing long enough to heat the wiring behind the wall rather than simply the cord in front of it, and pulling a hot plug out of a failing socket by hand was as likely to make things worse as better.

Her mind ran through the possibilities the way it ran through any fault with more than one plausible cause, discarding each option as fast as it arrived. A shorted heating element inside the space heater itself would have shown differently, heat concentrated at the unit rather than radiating out from the wall plate the way this one clearly was. A simple overloaded circuit would have tripped its own breaker long before the insulation started cooking, which meant whatever had failed here had failed in a way that bypassed the protection meant to catch exactly this kind of fault, a loose connection generating resistance heat in a spot the circuit’s own safety margin had never been designed to notice. The wall itself was the problem, not the heater plugged into it, and that distinction mattered enormously for what she did in the next ten seconds.

She glanced once toward the equipment room’s far corner, confirming the heater’s own casing hadn’t started smoking independently, and filed that as a second data point supporting the same conclusion. Whatever was failing was failing behind the plate, in wiring she couldn’t see and had no way to assess safely without cutting power first. There was no version of this where touching anything before the breaker came first was the correct sequence.

“Breaker,” she said, out loud, to nobody, already moving toward the panel she’d clocked on her first week in this building and filed away without ever once expecting to need it.

* * *

Tomasz was still in the locker room, sitting on the bench in his own unhurried post-practice slouch, complaining to nobody in particular about the two kilos he still had to find before Saturday, when Nadia came through at a dead run, yanked open the panel cover on the wall, and killed a breaker without pausing to check the label first, her hand finding the right switch the way her hand found anything it had already mapped.

“Whoa,” Tomasz said, sitting up properly. “What’s going on?”

“Outlet in the equipment room,” Nadia said, already moving back toward the smoke, the panel door left hanging open behind her. “It’s fine now. Power’s off. Don’t touch the wall by the heater, it’s still hot.”

He followed her as far as the equipment room doorway and stopped there, watching, while Nadia crouched in front of the dead outlet and examined it without touching it, close enough to read the damage but careful, the same careful distance she kept from anything she hadn’t yet confirmed was safe to handle.

The smoke had already thinned considerably with the power cut, the acrid smell settling into something more manageable, and she waited a moment longer before leaning any closer, giving the dead circuit time to actually cool rather than trusting that killing the breaker had made everything instantly safe. Heat lingered in a failed connection long after the current stopped flowing through it, sometimes for several minutes, and touching a plate too soon was a mistake she’d made exactly once, years ago, on a board that had looked cold and hadn’t been, and had never once repeated since.

“You just going to stand there and stare at it,” Tomasz asked, from the doorway, not unkindly, more curious than impatient.

“I’m letting it cool,” Nadia said, without looking away from the wall. “The current’s off but the heat isn’t gone yet. Touching it now is how people get burned on things that already stopped being dangerous in every way except temperature.”

He didn’t say anything else for a while, and she let the silence sit, counting the seconds the way she counted anything, until enough time had genuinely passed that she trusted the plate to be safe to approach properly.

“The plate’s basically melted,” Tomasz said, low, from the doorway. “How did you know it was that outlet and not, like, the whole room?”

“The smell told me where,” Nadia said, not looking away from the wall. “Insulation cooking smells different depending on how close you are to it. And the glow was only at this one plate. If the wiring further back had gone too, I’d have seen it through the wall vents by now. It hasn’t spread yet.”

* * *

She stayed crouched there a moment longer, reading the specifics of the damage the way she’d read a schematic, cataloguing what she was looking at without yet touching anything. The outlet itself had clearly been failing for a while, the specific browning pattern around the screw terminals telling a story she’d seen inside a hundred dead appliances, a connection that had loosened just enough to arc under load, heating slowly, cooking the insulation around it a little more every time the heater ran, until tonight it had finally gone far enough to show itself.

“This has probably been doing that for months,” she said, half to Tomasz, half just working the thought out loud. “A loose lug arcing under load doesn’t fail all at once. It builds. Every time that heater kicked on, it got a little worse, and nobody would’ve seen anything until exactly the point it started smoking, because there’s nothing to see before that.”

“That’s genuinely terrifying,” Tomasz said. “How do you even know that.”

Nadia didn’t answer right away, still working the outlet over with her eyes, and Tomasz didn’t push, watching her instead with an expression she didn’t register at the time and would have had no framework to interpret even if she had, the specific look of someone recalculating, quietly and in real time, exactly how much they’d actually understood about a person they’d known for months.

He’d wrestled across from her a dozen times this season, complained to her about his own cut more honestly than he’d complained to anyone else on the team, and had built, somewhere along the way, a working picture of her that fit neatly into the categories the rest of the roster already used, quiet, strong, kept to herself, good at fixing things, the one person on the team who never once seemed to mind how bad the cut got because she never once complained about it out loud. None of those categories had a slot in them for what he’d just watched, a person who’d read a wall panel the way most adults couldn’t read a car engine, correctly, instantly, with no visible hesitation between noticing the smoke and knowing exactly what to do about it. The picture didn’t break so much as it developed a gap, a piece of her that the rest of the categories hadn’t accounted for, and he stood in the doorway turning that gap over without yet knowing what to do with it either.

“You didn’t even flinch,” he said, finally, half to himself.

“There wasn’t time to flinch,” Nadia said, which was true and also, she recognised distantly, not really an answer to what he was actually asking.

* * *

The custodian arrived twelve minutes later, summoned by a call from the front office once Val had come to investigate the commotion, and confirmed everything Nadia had already worked out for herself, a failing outlet on an ancient circuit the district had been meaning to replace for years, the kind of deferred maintenance that sat quietly failing in a hundred buildings across the city until the exact night it finally announced itself.

He was an older man, unhurried in the specific way of someone who had seen a great many small building failures over a long career and had stopped finding any of them alarming, and he ran his own inspection of the outlet with a flashlight and a voltage tester, nodding along to findings that matched, almost exactly, word for word, what Nadia had already worked out from smell and colour and a glance through the panel vents. He glanced at her once during the inspection, a brief, appraising look she caught and filed without reacting to, the specific look of a tradesman recognising, however briefly, a competence he hadn’t expected to find in the room.

“Whoever pulled that breaker did you a favour,” he said, to the room generally, packing his tester away. “Another ten, fifteen minutes and that would’ve been a real fire instead of a scare. District’s had this whole wing on the replacement list for three years. Budget never quite stretches to it.”

“Attrition,” Nadia said, quietly, mostly to herself, and if anyone heard the word they didn’t ask what she meant by it.

The custodian taped a hazard sign over the dead outlet, promised the wing’s wiring would finally get bumped up the priority list after tonight, and left with the specific weary satisfaction of a man who’d filed this exact kind of report more times than he could easily count and expected to file it again somewhere else before the year was out, in a different wing of a different building, the same story with a different address attached to it.

Val asked, once the custodian had the outlet properly isolated and the room declared safe, how Nadia had known to go for the breaker instead of just pulling the plug, the question arriving with none of the alarm it might have carried from someone less used to watching her handle equipment other people found baffling.

“You don’t pull a hot plug out of a socket that’s already arcing,” Nadia said, the answer arriving without any particular weight attached to it, the same flat certainty she’d have given any technical question with an obvious correct answer. “You could get a worse arc right at the moment of disconnect, or take some of the melted plastic with it. Kill the source first. That’s just how you deal with a live fault.”

“That’s not something most sixteen-year-olds know,” Val said, not quite a question, watching her with the same particular attention she remembered from the wrestling room scale months ago.

“I fix electronics,” Nadia said. “It comes up.”

Val let it go, the way most people let things go when she gave them exactly enough of an answer to close the question without inviting a follow-up, and turned to reassure the rest of the team drifting back in from the hallway that everything was under control, the crisis already folding itself into the evening’s ordinary business.

She lingered a beat longer than she needed to, watching Val move through the small crowd with the same practical calm she brought to everything, and thought, not for the first time, about how easily a question that size had closed once she’d given it a technically true answer instead of a complete one. Val hadn’t pushed because Val had no framework for suspecting there was anything left to push on. A wrestler who also fixed electronics was, to a coach with forty other things on her plate tonight, a perfectly adequate explanation for a perfectly adequate outcome, and the gap between adequate and actually impossible simply never registered as a gap worth examining.

A few of the returning wrestlers clustered near the equipment room doorway, trading half-formed versions of what had happened, someone smelled smoke, someone pulled a breaker, could’ve been way worse, the story already smoothing itself into something simpler than what had actually occurred, the specific way any story simplified once it passed through more than two mouths. Nobody in that cluster looked twice at Nadia. She was, as far as any of them were concerned, simply part of the room the crisis had happened in, not the reason it had ended as quickly as it had.

She listened to the story reshape itself in real time, someone becoming a generic pronoun rather than a name, the details flattening with each retelling until the version circulating by the time the last few stragglers left the building barely resembled what she’d actually done at all. It was, she thought, exactly the outcome she’d have chosen if anyone had ever offered her the choice, a version of tonight where the fault had simply gotten fixed, by someone, somehow, and nobody felt any particular need to ask who.

* * *

Tomasz caught her by the door twenty minutes later, once the custodian had finished and the room had emptied back down to its usual handful of stragglers gathering their bags, and said nothing at first, just looked at her for a moment in a way that made her check, briefly, whether something was actually still wrong with the outlet.

“That wasn’t just fixing electronics,” he said, finally, quiet enough that nobody else caught it. “The way you found it. The way you knew exactly what not to touch, and exactly which breaker, before you’d even really looked at it properly. That’s not the same thing as knowing how to solder a wire back together.”

“It’s the same skill set,” Nadia said, which was true and also not really an answer, and she knew it wasn’t really an answer even as she said it.

Tomasz studied her for another moment, and something in his face settled into a decision she didn’t catch him making, the specific quiet resolve of a person choosing, deliberately, not to ask the next question even though the next question was sitting right there, plainly visible to both of them, unasked and unanswered and left that way on purpose. “Okay,” he said, simply. “I’m not going to make a thing of it. I just wanted you to know I noticed. That’s all.”

“There’s nothing to notice,” Nadia said, which came out more defensive than she’d meant it to, and she heard the defensiveness land and didn’t know how to take it back gracefully.

She considered, briefly, saying more, offering some fuller version of the truth that might satisfy him enough to actually close the subject rather than merely set it aside for tonight, and discarded the idea almost as fast as it arrived. There wasn’t a fuller version that stopped at a safe distance from everything underneath it. The moment she explained why she’d known exactly which breaker to pull without checking a label, she’d be explaining a great deal more than an outlet, and Tomasz, whatever else he was, hadn’t earned that particular door being opened for him just by standing in the right hallway at the right time.

“You don’t have to explain anything to me,” Tomasz said, watching her work through it, and something in the observation suggested he’d read at least part of what had just crossed her face. “I’m not trying to corner you. I just don’t love pretending I didn’t see what I saw. That’s all this is.”

“Sure,” Tomasz said, in a tone that plainly didn’t believe her and equally plainly wasn’t going to argue about it, and shouldered his bag, and left it there, the whole exchange closing itself the way most of his conversations with her closed, without either of them needing to say anything further about what had actually just happened. He paused once more at the door, glanced back at her, and said nothing else at all, which somehow felt like the more honest ending than any further sentence would have.

* * *

The walk to the bus stop ran colder than it had going in, the evening having slid well past the point where the day held any warmth left in it at all, and she covered the distance quickly, checking her phone twice against the posted schedule to confirm exactly how badly the twenty minutes had actually cost her. The math came back the way math like that always came back, precise and unkind, the last bus with any hope of getting her to the site with a usable window already gone by the time she’d finished with the custodian, leaving only the second-to-last, which would land her at the site with barely enough evening left to justify the trip at all.

She caught the second-to-last bus rather than the last one, the twenty extra minutes the equipment room had cost her collapsing the window she’d built for tonight’s session down to nothing survivable, and sat near the back watching the city slide past in the dark, running the numbers the way she ran anything that had just been taken from her without her consent.

You’re not going to make Phase Eleven tonight, Pip said, quiet.

“I know,” Nadia said, low. “The outlet cost me the window. Twenty minutes doesn’t sound like much until you’re counting a schedule that already didn’t have any room in it.”

I watched what you did back there, Pip said. I want to say, clearly, that it was the correct call. You could have let someone else deal with it and made the bus. You didn’t, and I don’t think that decision cost you as much thought as it probably should have.

“It would’ve been on fire in another ten minutes,” Nadia said. “I wasn’t going to just leave and hope somebody else noticed the smell in time.”

I know, Pip said. I’m not arguing with the decision. I just want it on the record that it cost you something real, on a night you genuinely couldn’t afford to lose anything.

“Everything costs something,” Nadia said, tired, watching a streetlight slide past the window. “I’ll make the window up somewhere else. I always do.”

She thought about the schedule pinned to the corkboard above the bench, three thin columns already stretched past what they could comfortably hold, and calculated, without much enthusiasm, where the lost twenty minutes might actually get repaid, running the same tired arithmetic she’d already run twice tonight. Sunday, possibly, if nothing else claimed it first. Or she simply accepted a shorter Phase Eleven session next light day, running the protocol’s minimum rather than its full duration, which risked muddying data she’d worked hard to keep clean. Neither option pleased her. Both were, at least, options, which was more than the schedule had offered her most weeks this season.

Which one are you actually going to choose, Pip asked, watching her work through it.

“I don’t know yet,” Nadia admitted. “I’ll know once I see how Sunday actually shakes out. I’m not going to decide now and lock myself into a plan I might have to break again in three days anyway.”

That seems reasonable, Pip said. I just don’t like how often reasonable, lately, means you not getting something you actually needed.

“Reasonable is what I’ve got,” Nadia said, and left it there, watching the bus carry her the rest of the way through streets she’d long since stopped really seeing, the whole familiar route reduced to a rhythm rather than a set of individual sights.

* * *

She thought, somewhere past the third stop, about Tomasz’s face in the equipment room doorway, the specific way he’d looked at her before he’d settled into whatever decision he’d finally made, and found the memory sitting somewhere she didn’t have an immediate use for, filed the way she filed most things that happened around her without quite being aimed at her, a data point about tonight rather than about herself, no different in kind from the outlet itself.

He’s not going to say anything, Pip observed, gently. I don’t think that’s in question. I just want to note that he clearly worked something out tonight, even if he never says a word about it to anyone.

“He works out a lot of things,” Nadia said. “He’s not stupid. He just also doesn’t push, which is more than I can say for most people who ever notice anything unusual about me at all.”

She thought about the specific shape of what he’d said in the doorway, I don’t love pretending I didn’t see what I saw, a sentence that had asked nothing of her at all beyond simple acknowledgement, no explanation demanded, no promise extracted, only a small, quiet insistence on his own honesty rather than hers, delivered without a single trace of the leverage such a sentence could so easily have carried instead. It was, she realised, turning the phrase over again, a considerably gentler way to handle a genuine discovery than she’d have expected from most people, and she wasn’t entirely sure what to do with the fact that it had come from Tomasz specifically, a boy she’d mostly known through shared complaints about a cut neither of them particularly enjoyed.

You keep replaying that sentence, Pip said. The one about not pretending.

“I don’t know why it’s sticking,” Nadia admitted. “It should have been the part of tonight I worried about most. Instead it’s just sitting there, and it doesn’t feel like a threat. I don’t entirely understand why it doesn’t feel like a threat.”

Maybe because it wasn’t offered as one, Pip said, quiet. People don’t always ask a question because they want the answer. Sometimes they just want you to know they saw it, and that’s the whole request.

That’s two now, Pip said. Pearce, and now Tomasz. Two people who’ve each seen one piece of something and decided, on their own, not to ask the next question.

I keep a mental list, it added, after a moment. I’m not sure why I started. It seemed important, once, and I’ve never found a good reason to stop.

“What’s on it,” Nadia asked, more curious than concerned, watching the streetlights slide past in their steady rhythm.

Not much yet, Pip said. Pearce, watching you fix his till faster than he thought possible. The wrestling scale, and what Val saw in it. Tomasz, tonight. Small things, individually. I just notice that the list has never once gotten shorter.

“Lists don’t get shorter,” Nadia said. “That’s not really what lists are for.”

Nadia turned that over for a moment, the bus rattling through a stretch of road with more potholes than pavement, and found she didn’t have much of a reaction to it beyond mild interest, the same mild interest she’d have given any fact that was true and didn’t currently require anything of her. “I hadn’t thought about it that way,” she said, finally. “I don’t really think about who’s seen what. I just think about whether the fault got fixed.”

I think that’s exactly the problem, Pip said, careful. Not a moral failing. Just a blind spot. You track queues and ledgers and hours down to the last unit, and you don’t track this at all, even though it might matter more, eventually, than any of the numbers you do track.

“What would tracking it even look like,” Nadia asked, genuinely curious rather than dismissive, turning the question over the way she turned over any problem she hadn’t previously thought to frame as a problem at all. “A column for who’s seen what. A running tally of how much each person actually understands. I don’t know how I’d even begin to estimate the numbers.”

Neither do I, honestly, Pip said. I don’t think it’s the kind of thing that reduces to a column the way everything else in your life does, however much I suspect you’d like it to. I just think it’s worth knowing the count is climbing, even without a clean way to actually measure it.

“Then I’ll know the count is climbing,” Nadia said, “and I still won’t be able to do anything about tonight specifically, because the outlet was going to catch fire whether or not Tomasz happened to be sitting in the locker room when it did. I can’t control who’s in the room. I can only control whether the fault gets fixed once I’m already in it too.”

I know, Pip said. I don’t think you’re ever going to think about it the other way, honestly, even though I suspect you probably should start.

She didn’t answer that, watching the last few blocks toward home slide past in the dark, the equipment room’s outlet already filed and closed in her own head, one more solved problem in a week that had already handed her several, and the twenty minutes it had cost her sitting in a different column entirely, a debt she’d find a way to repay somewhere before the schedule noticed it was missing.

The bay greeted her the way it always greeted her, quiet, unlit, exactly as she’d left it that morning, and she stood in the doorway a moment before turning on the light, letting the ordinary stillness of the room settle something in her chest that the whole evening had left unsettled without her fully noticing until now, the specific relief of a space that never once asked anything of her that she hadn’t already chosen to give it. No smoke here. No breaker panels. No coach watching her a beat too long, no teammate working something out in a doorway. Just a bench, a bed, and a schedule pinned above both, waiting patiently for whatever tomorrow decided to ask of it.

She pulled the schedule down long enough to mark tonight’s missed session in the margin, a small honest notation rather than an erasure, because a record that hid its own failures wasn’t a record she could trust the next time she needed to plan around one, and trusting the record was the entire reason the schedule existed at all. Missed. Equipment room fire, contained. Reschedule for Sunday if available. She pinned it back up exactly where it had been, the gap now visible and accounted for rather than quietly absorbed into a schedule that would otherwise have looked cleaner than the week had actually been, and stepped back to look at the whole corkboard one final time before switching off the light for good.

You always write the failures down too, Pip observed, watching her work. Not just the successes. I don’t think I’ve ever seen you skip logging a bad result just because it was inconvenient to admit.

“A schedule that only records the good weeks isn’t a schedule,” Nadia said, pinning the last corner flat. “It’s a story I’m telling myself. I’d rather have the honest version, even when the honest version says tonight went sideways.”

She got ready for bed slowly after that, the day’s accumulated fatigue finally catching up with her now that there was nothing left demanding her attention, and lay in the dark for a while before sleep actually arrived, running back through the evening one final time the way she ran through anything before letting it close, checking each piece against the others for anything she might have missed the first time through. The outlet. The breaker. Tomasz in the doorway, quiet, deciding something she still didn’t have a name for. None of it kept her awake for long. By the time sleep finally took her, the whole sequence had already settled into the same flat category as everything else that week, solved, filed, and done, one more entry in a year that had never once run short of them.
