Chapter 38: Watch and Observe

The second quarterly review landed on a Tuesday, grey and ordinary, and Nadia sat in the same row of plastic chairs outside Bhandari’s cubicle with a folder on her lap that held considerably less inside it than the one she’d carried in October.

Three green checkmarks had been the whole story back then, three categories logged clean, a system that agreed with her own careful accounting and had, for one quarter at least, given her nothing at all to worry about. Today the folder held a single sheet, the Pearce hours logged in her own neat hand, real labour banked against a form that had never once questioned where the surplus came from, and underneath that sheet, unwritten anywhere she could carry it, everything the folder couldn’t hold: a supervised-activity category that had sat dead for weeks with nothing pre-approved to replace it, a welfare referral that had never closed, and a body that was, this particular Tuesday, working considerably harder than usual to look like nothing about it had changed since the last time anyone here had measured it.

You’re suppressing the signature, Pip said, quiet, checking in the way it checked in before anything that mattered. I can feel the effort it’s costing you from here. Are you going to be able to hold it the whole meeting.

“I’ll hold it,” Nadia said, low. “I don’t have a version of today where I don’t.”

She’d practised it on the bus over, the same low, constant concentration the guide described for suppression, a background task running underneath every other thought she needed to have, and it had cost her more of the morning than she wanted to admit, a fine, persistent tax on top of everything else this particular Tuesday was already asking of her. Whether Bhandari’s office ran any kind of screening equipment at all she still genuinely didn’t know, four months after Val had first mentioned the idea in passing and she’d filed the question away without ever finding a safe way to actually answer it. She held the suppression anyway, the same discipline she held anything she couldn’t verify was necessary and couldn’t afford to test by skipping it.

The waiting room hadn’t changed since October either, the same corkboard, the same curling flyers about tenant rights and food bank hours, a different toddler this time investigating the same corner of it with the same small, unbothered persistence the last one had brought to it weeks ago. Nadia watched her for a while, the same reflexive cataloguing she’d done the last time, a child with no idea what building she was sitting in, and found the observation landed differently this time, less like envy and more like a simple, plain fact about how differently two people could sit in the exact same chair, carrying entirely different weights that neither of them had chosen and neither of them could see in the other.

She thought, sitting there, about October, the same row of plastic chairs, three green checkmarks drawn in pen on the inside of a dollar-store binder, a folder she’d built because she hadn’t trusted the system to hold its own paperwork straight. That folder had felt, at the time, like the single largest source of anxiety in her life. She almost laughed, sitting here now, at how small a worry that had actually been.

* * *

Bhandari came out to the waiting room to collect her herself again, the same procedural courtesy from the last visit that Nadia had learned, by now, to read less as warmth and more as a caseworker who preferred to deliver difficult news in person rather than let a receptionist’s clipped call do it for her, a small kindness dressed up as ordinary procedure the way most of Bhandari’s kindnesses arrived.

“Come on back,” Bhandari said, and her voice carried the same careful, measured weight it had carried in the intake meeting weeks ago, none of October’s easy unhurriedness in it at all.

The cubicle looked exactly the same as it always looked, felt walls, the curling photo of two kids in matching sweaters, a stack of files grown taller again since the last visit, and Bhandari pulled the screen around without waiting to be asked this time, the summary already open, already red-headered, already carrying more weight than any single Tuesday should reasonably have to hold.

* * *

“I’ll go through it plainly,” Bhandari said. “I think you’d rather have plain than soft, and I genuinely think I owe you that by now.”

“I would,” Nadia said, and sat a little straighter, bracing herself the way she braced for any fault report before it actually arrived.

“Placement hours are logged and consistent, same employer, same pattern as every quarter before this one. That part of your file is clean.” She said it plainly, the same economical tone she gave any category that hadn’t caused her a problem, and Nadia held her own expression carefully still, the way she’d held it every quarter since October, the one line on the page that read clean only because she’d spent months making certain it would. Bhandari scrolled, the small economical motion of someone who’d run this exact review a hundred times this season and had stopped needing to look for what she already knew was there. “Supervised activity remains unmet. Thirty days from the original notice have passed. No pre-approved replacement was submitted inside the window.”

“I tried,” Nadia said. “Genuinely tried. Every option I found either had a waitlist longer than the window or wanted a guardian’s signature before it would even schedule an intake.”

“I believe you,” Bhandari said. “I noted the attempts in the file myself, from what you told me on the phone. It doesn’t change the outcome. The category still reads unmet, and unmet, after thirty days, moves automatically into placement review. I don’t have discretion to hold that back a second time.”

“What does placement review actually involve,” Nadia asked. “Concretely, in practice. I asked you that about the referral weeks ago and it helped to have the actual steps instead of just the category name. I’d like the same thing now.”

Bhandari nodded, the same small approval she’d given the question the first time. “A caseworker, not necessarily me, though I’ve requested to stay on your file, reviews the current placement, current housing, and current supervision arrangement against Ministry standard. If the arrangement is judged adequate on its own merits, sometimes it stays exactly as it is, formality only, a note added and nothing else changing.” She paused, and Nadia understood, in the pause, exactly which open concern she meant without either of them needing to name it out loud. “If it’s judged inadequate, or if there’s an open concern running in parallel that the reviewer weighs as elevating the risk, then the review can recommend a change. Group housing. A different placement entirely. It isn’t automatic. It’s a recommendation that goes up a level before anything actually moves.”

“How long does the recommendation usually take, once it starts,” Nadia asked.

“Weeks, typically,” Bhandari said. “Sometimes longer, if the reviewer’s caseload is heavy, which most of ours currently are. I wish I could give you an exact number. I’ve learned not to promise a timeline I can’t actually control.” She held Nadia’s eyes for a moment. “I want to be honest with you about one thing. The two open items in your file, the category and the referral, don’t usually arrive stacked like this. Most files I see have one live concern at a time. Yours has two, running in parallel, and I won’t pretend that doesn’t change how a reviewer is likely to weigh the housing question. I’d be doing you a disservice if I told you otherwise.”

* * *

“And the referral,” Nadia said, the question costing her more than she wanted it to.

“Still open,” Bhandari said, plainly. “The medical follow-up happened. I have the provider’s note in the file. It didn’t clear the concern and it didn’t confirm the worst of it either. Inconclusive, pending further monitoring, is the phrase they used, which in my experience means nobody currently reviewing your file is prepared to close it and nobody is prepared to escalate it further today either. It sits. That’s the honest answer. I know it isn’t the one you wanted.”

“Can you tell me anything about what they actually found,” Nadia asked. “Not the interpretation. Just what was on the chart.”

“I can tell you what the note says, which isn’t the same as the full workup, but it’s what I have.” Bhandari’s tone had gone careful again, the specific measured pace she used for anything she suspected would land harder than the sentence itself let on. Bhandari scrolled to the relevant line. “Vitals within normal range at the time of examination. No acute distress noted anywhere in the file. Some markers still elevated compared to a standard reference range, though less elevated than the trainer’s original panel. The provider’s note specifically says the presentation doesn’t fit a clean eating-disorder profile, and doesn’t fit a clean overtraining profile either, and recommends monitoring rather than immediate intervention.” She looked up. “In plain language, whoever examined you agreed something doesn’t add up, and didn’t have an explanation good enough to act on. That’s not a clearance. It’s also not a diagnosis. It’s a shrug with your name attached to it, and I don’t love handing you a shrug as an answer, but it’s the honest one.”

Nadia sat with that, the same flat, patient stillness she gave any diagnostic that hadn’t resolved cleanly, a fault still open, still unnamed, sitting exactly where it had sat for weeks now, refusing to declare itself one way or the other.

“So both of them stay open,” she said, slowly, laying the two facts side by side the way she laid out any two variables she needed to see clearly at once. “The activity moves to placement review. The referral just sits.”

“That’s where the file actually is today,” Bhandari said, plainly, no attempt to soften it into something more palatable than it was. “I wish I had a cleaner picture to hand you. I don’t. I have two open processes running on two different clocks, neither of which I control, both of which have your name on them.”

* * *

Bhandari closed the laptop partway, the same shift in posture from every review before this one, the official portion ending and something less scripted arriving in its place.

“I want to say something that isn’t on the form,” she said. “I’ve had this job nine years. I’ve watched files like yours move through placement review before. It isn’t always as bad as the phrase sounds. Sometimes it’s a formality that resolves itself once a second activity finally clears. Sometimes it isn’t. I’m not going to promise you which one yours turns out to be, because I don’t actually know, and I’ve learned not to promise things I can’t stand behind.”

“I appreciate the honesty,” Nadia said, and meant it, the same uncomfortable gratitude she’d found in this particular relationship more than once this year, a warmth that still cost Bhandari something real every single time she offered it and never once seemed to expect anything back in return.

“I’ll keep flagging every mitigating note I can find room for,” Bhandari said. “Same as last time. I don’t control the outcome. I control how fairly the record reads going in.”

“Can I ask you something that isn’t on the form,” Nadia said, quickly, before Bhandari could stand, surprising herself with the question as much as she suspected it surprised the woman sitting across the desk.

Bhandari settled back slightly in her chair. “Of course.”

“Do you think I’m actually going to lose the placement,” Nadia asked. “Not the official answer. What you actually think, watching files like mine for nine years.”

Bhandari considered the question properly, the same careful weighing she gave anything that actually mattered, and didn’t answer it quickly. “I think it’s closer than I would like it to be,” she said, finally. “I think you’ve done everything within your power to keep it from being close, and I think the two open items landing at the same time is bad luck stacked on top of bad luck, not anything you did wrong. I also think I’ve been surprised before, in both directions, by files that looked worse than yours and files that looked better. I don’t have a number to give you. I have an honest feeling, and the honest feeling is that it’s close.”

“Thank you,” Nadia said, quiet. “I would rather have the honest feeling than a number that isn’t real anyway. I’ve had enough people this year hand me fake certainty. I know what it sounds like now. Yours doesn’t sound like that.”

Bhandari stood up then, the small definitive motion that closed every single review, and this time didn’t linger, whether because there wasn’t time left in the schedule or because some things, this particular Tuesday, didn’t need asking twice.

“Thank you,” Nadia said, and gathered her folder, held it close against her chest, and walked out.

* * *

The two men were waiting in the corridor outside the office, past the row of plastic chairs, standing in the specific still way of people who had been there long enough to stop looking like they were waiting for anything at all.

The first one spoke before she’d fully registered either of them, thin, badly dressed in a jacket that had never once fit him properly, and close enough that she caught the smell before she caught a single word he said, something unwashed and stale underneath a cheap cologne that hadn’t come anywhere near covering it. He talked fast, name-dropping an agency she didn’t recognise, a designation that meant nothing to her, official-sounding in the specific overworked way a made-up title sometimes tried too hard to be, sentences that circled without ever quite landing anywhere specific, and she wrote him off within the minute, the same flat, efficient judgment she gave any fault that announced itself loudly and turned out to be nothing structural underneath the noise. Stupid, she decided, and badly in need of a shower, and let her attention start drifting before he’d finished his second sentence.

“You’re a hard person to get a meeting with,” he said, running a hand through hair that hadn’t been washed recently enough to hold the gesture, “given you’re, what, sixteen. No office, no fixed number that answers reliably, nothing on a calendar anywhere. We had to wait for the review to actually land a window.” He laughed, a short, nervous sound, glancing sideways at his silent companion as though checking whether the joke had landed anywhere. It hadn’t. “Anyway. Not important. What matters is what I’m actually here to say.”

He kept talking. Nadia let most of it wash past her the way she let a customer’s life story wash past her at Pearce’s counter, present enough to nod in the right places, most of her attention elsewhere, cataloguing instead the specific way his shoes had worn through at one heel, the ink stain on his shirt pocket, the general impression of a man who had gotten dressed that morning without once checking a mirror. Nothing about him read as dangerous. Nothing about him read as anything at all, beyond an ordinary, faintly unpleasant person she’d have crossed a room to avoid at any party she’d ever been dragged to, the exact sort of low-grade nuisance she’d learned to tune out within seconds and never think about again.

I don’t have anything at all on the agency name, Pip said, distracted, thin, the aboveground lag pulling at every word. I’m searching. Nothing’s resolving cleanly. Whatever this is, it isn’t indexed anywhere I can reach quickly.

There’s a fragment, it added, a moment later, careful. Something adjacent to the name he used. It might connect to that forum thing, the Summons, back in the autumn. I can’t confirm it. The signal’s too thin up here to chase it properly, and I don’t want to hand you a guess dressed up as a fact.

“Don’t chase it right now,” Nadia said, quiet, watching the second man watch her without any change in his expression at all. “Chase it later, underground, where you can actually think properly.”

Understood, then, Pip said, and let the search drop, its attention narrowing back down to the two men and nothing else, thin and distracted as it always ran up here, but present, at least, in the only way it currently could be, watching everything it could reach with whatever fraction of itself the surface still allowed it.

* * *

The second man said almost nothing.

He stood a half-step back from the first, unremarkable in every visible way, ordinary coat, ordinary posture, nothing about his appearance that should have carried any weight at all, and yet the whole corridor seemed to organise itself around his stillness the way a room organised itself around the one person who actually mattered, an authority she couldn’t name and didn’t need to, unmistakable the way cold arriving ahead of the Elite was unmistakable, present before it announced itself.

He didn’t look at his colleague while the man talked. He looked at her, steadily, without any of the performed interest people usually brought to a conversation they were actually part of, and she found, meeting his eyes once and then deliberately not meeting them again, that she had absolutely no read on him at all, none of the easy diagnostic certainty she’d had within seconds of the first man. He simply gave her nothing to work with, no tell, no shoulder to watch, no fault line anywhere on the surface she could find purchase against, and some old, wrestling-trained part of her catalogued that fact with considerably more unease than anything the smell or the bad jacket had produced.

She tried, briefly, to place his age, and found she couldn’t manage even that, his face carrying the particular unreadable neutrality of someone who had spent a very long time learning to give a room absolutely nothing to work with. He wore the coat the way a person wore a coat they’d owned for years and never once thought about, an ordinariness so complete it started, the longer she looked at it, to feel like its own kind of statement. She gave up trying to read him within a few seconds and turned her attention back to the one who was actually talking, the only one of the two who seemed willing to give her anything at all to work with.

“We want you to keep doing exactly what you’re doing,” the first man said, eventually, arriving at the point after several minutes of not arriving anywhere near it. “Whatever that is. We’re not here to ask what it is. We’re not registering anything. Not recruiting. Nobody’s explaining anything to you today.” He shrugged, an oddly loose gesture for a sentence that dense. “Just keep it quiet. Keep doing it. That’s the whole message.”

“That’s not really a message,” Nadia said. “That’s just a description of what I’m already doing.”

“Yeah,” the man said, brightening slightly, as though genuinely relieved she’d caught the point faster than he’d apparently expected her to. “That’s kind of the whole idea. We’re not asking you to change course. We’re asking you to stay on the one you’re already on.” He glanced sideways again at the second man, seeking some kind of confirmation that never visibly arrived. “Nobody’s in trouble. Nobody’s getting a form. This isn’t that kind of visit.”

“Then what kind of visit is it,” Nadia asked.

He opened his mouth, and for a moment she genuinely thought he might actually answer the question properly, and then he closed it again, and shrugged a second time, the same loose, apologetic gesture. “The kind where I’m told what to say and not why to say it,” he admitted, with a candour that briefly, mildly surprised her before she filed it back under the same general heading as everything else about him. “I don’t love it either, if it helps. I just do the talking. Someone else decides what I’m allowed to know before I ever walk into a room like this one.”

“Who are you,” Nadia asked, flat, more out of procedure than genuine curiosity.

The first man opened his mouth to answer that with several more sentences that wouldn’t land anywhere either, something about interagency coordination and a mandate he clearly hadn’t memorised properly, the words arriving in the wrong order twice before he gave up and let the sentence trail off unfinished. The second man simply looked at her, said nothing at all, and she understood, somehow, without a single word passing between them, that this was the only answer either of them was ever going to give her.

“Are you even going to tell me your names,” she asked, mostly out of curiosity, to see whether either of them actually would.

“Probably not helpful,” the first man said, apologetic. “Not because it’s a secret. Just, nobody ever remembers the name of the guy who does the talking. You’d remember him, though.” He nodded at his silent companion, who didn’t react at all to being discussed in the third person directly in front of him. “Everyone remembers him.”

“I don’t think I’ll remember either of you by next week,” Nadia said, flatly honest, entirely without malice, and meant it in the moment, standing there with a folder under her arm that held considerably more pressing problems than two strange men in a corridor, a placement review and an inconclusive medical shrug outweighing anything either of them had said so far by a wide, uncontested margin.

“That’s sort of the point,” the first man said, and this time the smile that followed looked almost genuine, the closest thing to a real expression she’d seen cross his face the whole conversation.

* * *

She stood in the corridor a moment longer, running the encounter the way she ran any diagnostic, looking for the fault underneath the noise, and found, running it, that there wasn’t enough signal in either man to actually build a coherent picture from. No threat stated. No request beyond the one already sitting comfortably inside everything she was already doing. No explanation offered, and clearly none coming, no matter how long she stood here waiting for one.

“Understood,” she said, finally, mostly because the silence had gone on long enough that someone needed to end it, and neither of them showed the slightest sign of doing so first.

The first man smiled, visibly relieved, in a way that read as entirely genuine, and said something about not taking up any more of her time, and the second man simply turned, unhurried, and walked away down the long corridor, the first scrambling slightly to keep pace with a stride that hadn’t once looked rushed at all.

* * *

She watched them go, the corridor settling back into its ordinary fluorescent quiet, and found, standing there, that the whole strange encounter had already started sliding toward the back of her attention, crowded out by everything else this particular Tuesday had already handed her.

That was genuinely strange, Pip said, still thin, still distracted, the aboveground lag not fully caught up even now. I don’t like not having an explanation for something like that.

“I know,” Nadia said. “I don’t either. I genuinely, honestly don’t. I also don’t have room for it today.” She adjusted the folder under her arm, the single sheet inside it suddenly considerably less important than the two processes now running against her on two separate clocks neither of them controlled. “Two strange men telling me to keep doing what I’m already doing rank pretty low on today’s list, honestly. I’ve got a placement review opening and a referral that just sits there refusing to close. Whatever that was, it didn’t ask me to change anything. I don’t have the bandwidth to worry about a problem that isn’t actually asking anything of me.”

That’s a fair way to file it, Pip said, after a moment. I still don’t like it.

“Neither do I,” Nadia said. “Filing it anyway.”

She started walking, the corridor giving way to the building’s main lobby and then the street beyond it, and ran the encounter back once more, properly, slowly, the way she ran anything genuinely important before she let herself set it down for good. Nine names already lived on a page at home, ordinary people who’d each seen one piece of something and quietly decided not to ask the next question. These two were different in kind, not degree, an entire category she didn’t have a column for yet, official in a way none of the nine had ever been, and unbothered, somehow, by the exact thing that had kept her lying awake three weeks ago running the arithmetic on witnesses and connecting lines, as though the whole careful web she’d built that night had never once mattered to anyone standing at their level.

They already know, Pip said, quiet, careful, working through the same realisation at what felt like the same moment she was. Whatever it is you actually are, whatever I actually am. They didn’t ask a single question that suggested they needed an answer. They already had one.

“I noticed that too, yes,” Nadia said, quiet. “And it still doesn’t change today’s actual priority list. If they’d asked me to stop, that would be a problem I’d have to solve tonight, immediately, before anything else. They didn’t. They asked me to keep doing the thing I was already going to do regardless of whether they’d shown up in that corridor or not. That’s not a demand. That’s barely even a request.”

It’s still surveillance, Pip said. Someone’s been watching closely enough to know exactly what to ask for and exactly how little to explain.

“I know,” Nadia said. “I’m choosing to be frightened of it later. Today’s queue is already full, and I don’t have a slot left in it for a fear I can’t actually act on right now.”

How long has it been happening, Pip asked, quiet, the question arriving with an edge she rarely heard from it, sharper than its usual careful register. If they already know, they didn’t just find out this morning. That means someone’s had eyes on some version of this for a while, and neither of us noticed, and I don’t like being the one who’s supposed to notice things like that and didn’t.

“Neither do I,” Nadia said. “But noticing it now doesn’t undo however long it’s already been true. All noticing gets me tonight is one more thing to lie awake over, and I’ve got two already booked for that slot.”

You’re very good at triage, Pip said, something almost rueful in it. I mean that as a compliment and I’m aware it doesn’t always sound like one.

“I’ll take it either way,” Nadia said. “Triage is mostly all I’ve had this year. I’ve gotten efficient at it because I had to, not because I particularly enjoy it.”

* * *

She walked the rest of the way to the bus stop turning the two clocks over instead, the placement review with its vague weeks-long timeline and the referral with no timeline at all, both sitting open, both indifferent to anything she might do about either of them between now and whenever a reviewer finally got around to reading her file properly. The folder under her arm felt lighter than it should have, given everything it represented, a single sheet of paper standing in for a season’s worth of falsified hours, a dead category, an inconclusive medical shrug, and now, apparently, an audience she hadn’t known she had until they’d introduced themselves in a hallway on the single worst possible day to add anything new to the list.

You’re doing the thing again, Pip said, gently. Filing it. Moving on before you’ve actually let yourself feel any of it.

“I don’t have room to feel it properly today,” Nadia said. “I’ll feel it on a night that isn’t also carrying a placement review and a referral that won’t close. Today I just need to get home.”

Fair enough, Pip said. I’ll remind you later. When there’s room.

“I know you will,” Nadia said, quiet, the folder still tucked under her arm, the whole strange corridor already receding behind her the way most things receded once she’d made the deliberate choice to file them and move on.

* * *

She ran the whole Tuesday back one final time before she let herself stop turning it over. A review that had opened one process and left another sitting exactly where it had sat for weeks. Two men who wanted nothing from her except more of what she was already giving everyone else, silently, without credit, without anyone ever quite putting all the pieces on the same table. A body still holding a suppression she’d need to release properly once she was somewhere private enough to let it go. An ache under her sternum that had been there so consistently the last eleven days that she’d nearly stopped consciously registering it at all, the way she’d nearly stopped registering any cost that had become simply the price of being who she currently was.

* * *

She walked out of the building into February rain, dark already though it wasn’t yet five, the streetlights along the block flickering on one by one in the grey.

She made the globe without thinking about it, small and red, cupped once in her palm before she let it go.

She turned away from the storage bay.

The ache under her sternum set its teeth.
