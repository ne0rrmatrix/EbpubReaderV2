Chapter 12: Making Weight

The scale in the wrestling room read almost exactly the same number three days running, which Val said, checking the log without any particular emotion at all in her voice, meant the easy part of the season was now officially over.

Preseason had been conditioning and technique, baseline numbers and nobody thinking too hard yet about the specific figure printed on a roster next to their name. The gym itself had shifted with it, the same mats and the same wall bar and the same trophy case with its gaps still unfilled, but the air in the room had changed somehow, a tightening she felt more than saw, twenty-odd people all quietly doing the same arithmetic in their own heads at the same time. The actual season, the one with a schedule of meets stretching out from November to February, ran on a different logic entirely, and that logic started here, six weeks out from the first competition, with a number on a scale that either matched a weight class or didn’t.

Val ran the weigh-ins the same way she ran everything, briskly, without ceremony, a line of wrestlers filing past the same balance-beam scale Nadia had fixed six weeks ago, each name checked off a clipboard the moment the beam settled. Nobody complained about the process itself, whatever they felt about the number it produced, because Val’s flat efficiency about it somehow made the whole ritual easier to bear than a coach who made a show of it would have.

The gym smelled different this time of year too, a colder edge under the usual rubber and sweat, the door propped open between rounds to let heat escape a room that ran too hot the moment twenty bodies started moving through drills together. Somebody had taped a printed schedule to the wall beside the trophy case, dates running from the first November meet clear through to the very end of February, a physical timeline Nadia found herself reading more carefully than she expected to, working out, without quite meaning to, how many two-week gaps the calendar actually contained between now and the season’s eventual end.

Nadia’s matched, for now, close enough that Val didn’t say anything to her directly, the way she said things to two of the heavier wrestlers whose numbers had drifted further over the summer than they wanted anyone to notice, a quiet word to each of them after practice that Nadia caught only the shape of from across the room, not the content. But close enough was not the same as settled, and Nadia understood, standing on that scale for the third day running, watching the beam settle at almost exactly the same reading it had settled at the day before, that the number was about to become the single most contested figure in her entire week, competing against a second number that had nothing to do with wrestling at all and mattered considerably more.

She thought, stepping off the scale, about the density problem she hadn’t quite named yet, a faint suspicion sitting at the edge of her attention that the number on the beam had been climbing by fractions she couldn’t entirely explain against how little she’d actually changed about her training. She set the thought aside for now, filed rather than dismissed, because a cut that was about to demand every ounce of her discipline was not the week to go chasing a suspicion she had no real evidence for yet beyond a feeling and a handful of fractions of a kilo.

* * *

She’d run the collision properly for the first time two nights earlier, sitting at the bench with both sets of figures laid out side by side on the same page for the first time instead of in two separate parts of the notebook she’d somehow kept them apart in for weeks, as though some part of her had been avoiding the comparison on purpose for as long as it could get away with it.

On one side: fifty-two kilograms, the number she needed to weigh in at, or under, six weeks from now, and every six weeks after that for the rest of the season. A cut, if her body ran anything like the average for someone her size and sport, of somewhere between two and four kilograms depending on how much water and glycogen she was carrying going in, achieved through a caloric deficit sustained across the days leading into a weigh-in, tightening in the final forty-eight hours the way every cut she’d ever watched other wrestlers run had tightened.

On the other side: the five thousand calories a single properly run visit to the site required to process cleanly, a surplus so large it dwarfed anything a normal training diet asked of her, arriving at exactly the moment the wrestling side of the ledger was asking for the opposite. She underlined the two figures separately, five thousand in one colour of ink and the cut’s deficit in another, a habit she’d never needed before this particular comparison, because no two numbers in her life had ever before pointed in such flatly opposite directions at the same time.

She tried, for a while, to imagine a version of the week where both could coexist without either one bending, a session timed for the one day the deficit happened to be smallest, a cut structured loosely enough around the edges to leave room for an occasional surplus day buried inside it. The arithmetic refused every version she tried. A surplus day inside a cutting week didn’t merely pause the deficit, it reversed the whole week’s progress in a single sitting, and a cutting week structured loosely enough to survive that reversal stopped being a cut in any meaningful sense at all, just an ordinary diet with an ambition it would never actually reach in time for a weigh-in.

She tried a longer view instead, spreading the site’s cost across two weeks rather than cramming it into one, banking a surplus in the days immediately after a weigh-in when the scale no longer mattered until the next cycle began, and that version came closer to working than any of the others, though it meant the site could only realistically be visited once every two weeks rather than the weekly rhythm she’d been building toward before the season proper had ever started. Once every two weeks was slower than she wanted, considerably slower than the pace she’d been building toward before the season proper started. It was, by every measure she could actually run, the only version of the week that didn’t break something else important in the process.

She stared at the two columns for a long time, the way she stared at any two facts that refused to occupy the same reality peacefully, and understood, before Pip had said a single word about it, that there was no clever rearrangement available here, no fifth column hiding somewhere she hadn’t looked. A deficit and a surplus could not both be true of the same body on the same day. One of them was going to have to give way to the other, repeatedly, for months, and she had no idea yet which one it would be on any given week, only that the collision itself was now permanent rather than occasional, built into the shape of her life for as long as both halves of it stayed true.

I’ve run this a few different ways, Pip said, into the silence after she’d finished writing it out plainly, cause and effect, no speculation past what the numbers actually supported. I keep hoping I’ll find an error in my own math that makes it less bad. I haven’t found one yet.

“Neither have I,” Nadia said, and closed the notebook on it, because there was nothing left to do with the fact except live inside it.

* * *

Tomasz found her by the water fountain after the weigh-in, drinking exactly as little as everyone drank in the first real week of a cut, and told her, without her having to ask, exactly how bad it was going to get.

He was three weeks further into his own cut than the rest of the team by his own choice, said he preferred to bleed the weight off slow rather than panic in the last few days the way half the roster always did, and described the whole arc of it to her with the specific weary authority of somebody who had lived through it four times already and had stopped pretending any part of it was going to surprise him.

The first week was mostly discipline, cutting the easy calories, the soda and the seconds and the late-night anything, the kind of restriction that mostly just meant saying no to things that were never strictly necessary in the first place. The middle stretch was the water manipulation, learning exactly how much to load and how much to cut and when, a science half the coaches he’d had over the years treated like folklore rather than physiology, passed down through half-remembered advice from wrestlers a decade older than any of them. The final two days before an actual weigh-in were, in his words, not really eating so much as suffering with intention, sauna suits and spitting into cups and every trick short of the ones that would actually put a person in danger, and he said this like it was a fact of life rather than a complaint, the specific flat acceptance of someone who had decided, a long time ago, that this was simply what the sport cost.

He told her about a tournament two years back where he’d cut too hard too fast, weighed in fine and then spent the two hours before his first match lying on a gym floor with his coach checking his pulse every ten minutes, water and electrolyte packets pushed on him by every adult in the building until his hands stopped shaking enough to compete at all, and said it the way a person tells a story they’ve told enough times that the fear has mostly worn off it, leaving only the shape of the lesson behind. “Slow is better,” he said. “Everybody learns that the hard way exactly once, if they’re lucky.”

Nadia listened to all of it with real attention, filing each detail the way she filed anything with a practical use, water loading ratios, the specific timing windows he swore by, none of it academic to her the way it might have been to somebody without her particular second reason for needing every gram of margin she could find, a reason he would never once know existed no matter how many stories he told her.

“You’re quiet about it,” he said, glancing sideways at her. “Most people complain more their first real cut.”

“I’m thinking,” Nadia said, which was true, and left it at that, because the actual thought, that her cut was going to run headlong into an obligation he knew nothing about and never would, wasn’t one she had any way to share with him.

He shrugged, apparently satisfied with the answer regardless of how little of the actual truth it contained, and went back to sipping water in the small, careful amounts that had already become habit for him, three full weeks into a routine he could probably now run without thinking about it at all.

Priya, further down the hallway waiting for her by the lockers, asked a version of the same question later that afternoon, not about the cut specifically but about why Nadia seemed quieter than usual lately, and Nadia gave her the same kind of answer she gave everyone who asked that particular question, true in its smallest possible sense, false in everything it left out. Tired. Busy. The season starting. Priya accepted it the way Priya accepted most things Nadia said, without pushing, and moved on to a story about a group project that had nothing to do with any of it, and Nadia let herself be carried along by the story rather than the question, grateful, in the flat way she was grateful for most things, that neither Priya nor Tomasz nor anyone else at that school had any reason to ask the second question underneath the first one.

* * *

She built her first real cut week the way she built anything, methodically, on paper, with numbers she trusted more than instinct. A modest deficit against her ordinary intake, timed to land her a comfortable margin under fifty-two by the actual weigh-in rather than scraping the number at the last possible hour the way half the roster apparently did, because she had learned, over the last two months, exactly how badly her body reacted to being pushed right up against any kind of edge. Water managed rather than eliminated, because dehydrating herself blind seemed, on every piece of research she could find, like exactly the kind of shortcut that caused more problems than it solved, and she cross-referenced Tomasz’s half-remembered advice against three separate sports-medicine sources before she trusted any of it enough to build a plan around it.

She wrote the week out the same way she’d written her placement search and her caloric collision, hour by hour, meal by meal, a schedule specific enough that she wouldn’t have to make a single decision under pressure once the actual cutting started. Breakfast shrunk by a fixed, modest margin rather than skipped entirely, because a skipped meal early in a cut only made the later ones harder to manage. Dinner moved earlier, giving her body more overnight hours to work through whatever she’d eaten before the next day’s deficit began stacking on top of it. Water, tracked in actual millilitres on the same page as the food, tapering only in the final two days rather than the whole week, exactly the way Tomasz had described it and exactly the way three separate sources agreed was the safer approach.

She priced the whole week out in grocery terms too, the same conversion she ran on everything now, rice and eggs and the cheaper cuts of chicken stretched carefully against a deficit that still needed to hit its protein targets even while cutting calories everywhere else it safely could, because a cut built on starving the wrong nutrient was, according to everything she’d read, a cut that cost more muscle than it needed to and made the whole season harder rather than easier. The total came in low enough that money, for once, wasn’t the binding constraint on this particular problem, which felt, after two months of nearly everything else being exactly that, like a small strange mercy she hadn’t expected to receive.

This is a remarkably thorough document for something you plan to abandon the first time it collides with a dungeon visit, Pip observed, reading over the finished page.

“It won’t collide,” Nadia said. “That’s the whole point of writing it out. I already know where the collision would happen. I’m planning around it.”

You say that with a great deal of confidence for someone who has never actually run this specific experiment before.

“It’s the same method I use for everything else,” Nadia said. “I don’t need to have run it before. I just need the plan to survive contact with the first real week, and if it doesn’t, I’ll know exactly where it broke and fix that specific part.”

That’s a very reasonable way to approach a body, Pip said, after a pause. I don’t know if bodies always cooperate with reasonable approaches. I suspect we’re about to find out together, and I’ll admit I’m curious in a way that probably isn’t entirely appropriate given what’s actually at stake for you.

What she did not build into the week, deliberately, on purpose, with her jaw set the way it set when she made a decision she didn’t love, was a visit to the site.

You’re not going this week, Pip observed, somewhere around Thursday, when the pattern of the last five days had made itself obvious even without her saying anything about it directly, no gear packed, no headlamp charging on the bench, none of the small preparatory rituals that usually announced a visit days before she actually took one.

“I can’t afford it,” Nadia said. “Not this week. Maybe not most weeks, once the cut’s properly underway.”

That’s going to slow the whole project down considerably.

“I know.”

I don’t say that as a criticism of you, Pip added, quickly, something careful threading through it. I say it because I think it’s worth naming plainly rather than letting it sit there unsaid. You’re choosing the cut over the site this week. I’d like to understand why, if you’re willing to walk me through it, because from where I sit, the site seems like the thing that matters more in the long run.

Nadia considered the question while she measured out a portion of rice she wouldn’t have bothered measuring three months ago, back when a portion had simply been whatever fit comfortably in the bowl, the kitchen scale she’d bought specifically for this cut sitting on the bench where the honey jar’s ghost still lived in her memory more than the bench itself.

“The cut’s not optional right now,” she said, finally. “Missing a weigh-in loses me the supervised activity category, and losing that reopens the file, and I can’t afford that this month any more than I could two months ago. The site can wait a week. The file can’t.”

That’s a very rational answer.

“It’s the only kind I’ve got.”

I don’t doubt that it’s rational. I’m just noticing that every decision this week has run through the same filter, what does the Ministry need, what does the file need, what happens if I fail this particular test, and I don’t think I’ve heard you once this week ask what you actually want, separate from any of that.

Nadia set the measuring cup down and considered the observation properly, the way she considered anything Pip offered that landed somewhere she hadn’t expected it to.

“I don’t know if that question’s useful to me,” she said, eventually. “I don’t think I’ve ever really asked it. Wanting things and getting things have always run on different tracks for me. Asking what I want just tells me what I can’t have yet.”

That’s a genuinely sad thing to say so calmly, Pip said, quiet now, something careful in it.

“It’s not sad,” Nadia said. “It’s just how the tracks run.”

* * *

The week ran the way she’d planned it, mostly, which was its own small victory against a body that had spent the last two months learning, in the worst possible way, exactly how much it could demand when it wanted to. She was hungrier than an ordinary deficit should have made her, a low persistent pull she recognised now as the same signal she’d felt in the cafeteria weeks ago, except running in the opposite direction this time, her body asking for reserves she was actively declining to give it.

The cafeteria itself became a genuine test of will in a way it never had been before. She still went, still sat at the same table with Priya and Hana and whoever else drifted through, but the tray in front of her stayed smaller now, portioned to the ounce rather than filled the way it used to be filled without a second thought, and she found herself watching the serving line the way she’d watch a board she wasn’t allowed to touch, cataloguing what she wasn’t having rather than what she was.

Priya noticed on the third day, glancing at the modest plate with a flicker of the same surprise she’d shown weeks ago in the opposite direction. “Wrestling,” Priya said, not quite a question, already reaching for her own tray as an answer to a question nobody had asked, and Nadia said yes, which was true, and left it exactly there, because the true answer had two parts and only one of them belonged to a conversation held over a cafeteria tray.

By Wednesday the hunger had settled into something closer to background noise than crisis, her body apparently accepting, grudgingly, that the deficit was here to stay for the week, and she managed the rest of the days on discipline alone, tracking every gram against the plan she’d written, adjusting nothing, trusting the numbers the way she trusted a schematic she’d already checked carefully twice and had no real reason to doubt on the third pass.

She ate on schedule anyway, disciplined about it in a way that cost her more effort than she let herself show, and by Friday’s weigh-in she came in comfortably under fifty-two, enough margin that Val glanced at the number, then at her, and said nothing beyond a short nod that Nadia read, correctly, as approval. It was a small thing, a nod, barely anything at all, and it landed with more weight than she expected it to, a confirmation that the plan had worked exactly the way she’d built it to work, one clean data point in a week that had otherwise felt like nothing but restraint.

Tomasz made his own number by a narrower margin, having pushed his own cut closer to the wire than he’d planned to, and complained about it loudly and at length on the walk out of the gym, a running commentary on the specific misery of the last six hours that Nadia listened to with the same easy attention she gave most of what he said, half of her actually following the story and half of her already thinking three moves ahead to the following week, when the cut would tighten and the site would still be waiting, unpaid, patient, exactly where she’d left it.

He asked, somewhere in the middle of the walk home, how she’d found her first real week, and she said it was easier than she’d expected, which was true in the narrow sense that the wrestling half of it had gone exactly to plan, and he nodded like that matched his own experience of a first cut, easier than the stories made it sound, right up until the season actually tightened around everyone properly.

* * *

She didn’t go back to the pumping station until the second Saturday after that, a full ten days between visits, longer than any gap she’d managed since she’d found the place, and she felt the absence of it the whole time in a way that surprised her a little, an itch she hadn’t expected to develop this fast for a building full of cold and a thing with four wrong-jointed legs that had nearly killed her once already.

The ten days had a rhythm to them she hadn’t anticipated when she’d first written the cut plan. School, shifts at Pearce’s, practice, the careful arithmetic of every meal, and underneath all of it a low background awareness of the site sitting out past the reservoir, unvisited, patient, the way a repair she’d deliberately set aside sat in a corner of the bay reminding her, every time she passed it, that it hadn’t gone anywhere. She caught herself, twice that week, pulling up the registry app just to look at her own profile page, the threshold number unchanged since her last visit, a small confirmation that nothing had moved without her and nothing was going to.

She kept busy in every other direction instead, taking on an extra job through the parts board on a night she’d normally have had free, restocking Pearce’s back shelf without being asked simply to have something to do with her hands, and none of it quite filled the specific shape of the absence, because none of it was actually the thing she was missing, only a reasonable substitute for the hours it would have occupied. Pip noticed the substitution too, remarking once, mildly, that she seemed to be finding an unusual number of small tasks to fill an unusually specific gap, and she didn’t argue the observation, because arguing with an accurate one had never once been worth the effort.

When she finally went back, on a Saturday that had no wrestling and no shift and, for once, a caloric surplus banked carefully over the previous two days rather than borrowed against the week ahead, she went carefully. She’d eaten well ahead of it, banking whatever margin the cut had left her room to bank, protein and complex carbohydrate stacked across breakfast and lunch in quantities that would have alarmed her three months ago and now simply felt like preparation. She kept the session itself short, cautious, closer to her second visit than her third, because a body running a deficit five days out of seven had no business testing the outer edge of anything at all, no matter how badly some part of her wanted to push further.

Welcome back, Pip said, once the cold of the building had settled properly around them both, something almost fond in it, the particular warmth of a voice that had, in its own way, missed the place too.

“It’s good to be back,” Nadia admitted, out loud, to the empty catwalk, and meant it more than she’d expected to when she said it.

She found the thing exactly where the patrol always put it, patient, incurious, moving along the same worn groove in the concrete it had walked for longer than she’d been alive, and held it for a controlled stretch of contact rather than the extended tests she’d run three weeks ago, base low, angle everything, nothing left to prove tonight that she hadn’t already proven. The chamber looked the same as it always did, valve wheels rusted solid, the stripped control board still holding its neat rows of empty mounting holes, ten days having changed nothing about the room even as they’d changed a great deal about her. The exchanges came easier than she expected, her body apparently remembering the rhythm of it even after ten days away, and she disengaged well before anything approached the edge of what a deficit-weakened body could actually absorb, walking the reset backward the way she always did, watching until the thing lost interest and returned to its own patient work.

The visit itself confirmed nothing genuinely new, added no fresh data to the notebook, cost her a manageable fraction of what a full session would have. It was, by every measure she actually cared about, a maintenance trip rather than a research one, a way of keeping the thread from going entirely cold while the rest of her week belonged to a scale and a diet and a number that had nothing to do with any of this at all.

The bleed, when it came, sat well within what she’d banked for it, a mild ache rather than a crisis, resolved with a fraction of the food she’d carried out here specifically for that purpose, and she sat on the cold grass at the tree line afterward, eating anyway, methodically, because a pattern that had nearly killed her twice was not a pattern she intended to gamble with simply because tonight’s version of it felt manageable. The sky above the reservoir had gone the flat grey it went most weekend afternoons this time of year, no rain yet but the promise of it sitting heavy in the air, and she ate through the last of what she’d packed while it held.

You’re being very careful, Pip observed, watching her eat the way it always seemed to watch, in whatever sense it watched anything.

“I don’t have room for careless right now,” Nadia said. “Not with the cut running underneath everything else. One mistake here costs me two separate things at once instead of one.”

That genuinely sounds exhausting to me.

“It is,” Nadia admitted, chewing slowly through the last of the dried fruit she’d packed, and didn’t pretend otherwise, because pretending had never once made an exhausting thing lighter, only made it lonelier to carry.

She walked out afterward into an ordinary grey Saturday afternoon, the cut still running underneath everything, the site still waiting behind her, both of them permanent now, both of them hers, and started the long walk back toward the fire road already doing the arithmetic for next week, because next week would need its own version of this same impossible balance, and the week after that, and the one after that, for as long as both halves of her life insisted on staying true at the same time.

Tomasz texted that evening, a single line asking how her first real cut week had gone, and she typed back that it had gone fine, which was true in the way that mattered to him, and left out the second half of her week entirely, the half that had nothing to do with wrestling and everything to do with a decision she made, over and over, that she had no way of explaining to anyone who hadn’t stood on that catwalk beside her, watching it come at her the way she had, and understood, in the exact way it had felt at the time.

She got into bed that night with the two schedules still sitting side by side in her head the way they’d sat on the page two nights earlier, a cut and a site, a weigh-in and a threshold, both of them entirely and completely hers now for as long as either one lasted, and fell asleep somewhere in the middle of counting forward through the calendar Val had taped to the gym wall, working out, without ever quite finishing the count, exactly how many of these careful, balanced weekends the season actually had room for before something, somewhere along the line, was finally going to have to give.
