Chapter 9: The Storage Bay

You’re doing the thing again, Pip said, somewhere behind her left ear, quiet enough that it took her a second to register it over the noise of the hallway. Where you go somewhere else while your face stays exactly the same. I’ve started keeping track. This is the fourth time today.

Nadia didn’t answer out loud, because answering out loud in a crowded hallway between second and third period was the kind of thing that got noticed, and she had spent three days now working out the shape of talking to something nobody else could hear without ever moving her mouth, a habit that came slower than she expected from someone who solved most problems by simply doing the obvious thing until it worked. The trick, she was learning, was less about controlling her face and more about controlling the half-second where her attention split, the small internal flinch that happened whenever she started constructing a reply and had to catch it before it reached her mouth.

She’d gotten better at it over three days, from a standing start of nearly answering out loud twice on the first afternoon, once nearly in front of Priya, to something closer now to a habit she trusted, though not one she trusted completely, which was why she still preferred, when she had the choice, to have these conversations somewhere with fewer witnesses.

You’re thinking about money again, Pip said. You always think about money right before lunch. I don’t know if that’s a coincidence or a pattern yet. I’m keeping a list.

I have a great many lists, actually, it added, a beat later, unprompted, the way it tended to add things unprompted whenever a silence ran longer than it apparently found comfortable. I don’t know if that’s useful information or just something I felt like telling you. I’m still working out the difference.

She was, in fact, thinking about money, and about Szabo, who had said something two days ago on her way out of his shop that she hadn’t been able to put down since.

* * *

He’d mentioned it almost sideways, the way he mentioned most things that turned out to matter, counting out her total for a tote of stripped connectors without looking up from the till. Said a fellow he knew from years back, ran a mechanic’s shop off Kingsway, had scaled the business down to half what it used to be after his knees gave out on him, and had a storage bay behind the main shop floor sitting mostly empty now, used for nothing but a stack of old tires and a workbench nobody touched. Said the man wasn’t interested in going through a proper rental agency for a space that small, the paperwork costing him more in time than the bay would ever earn him in rent, and that he’d rather have somebody reliable in it paying cash than have it sit there collecting dust and liability.

Szabo hadn’t said any of it like an offer. He’d said it the way he said most things, a fact passed along because it happened to be true and happened to be relevant, sliding the connectors into a bin without once looking up to see how she was taking it, and left her to do whatever she wanted with it. She’d thanked him and gone home and hadn’t said anything else about it, and he hadn’t brought it up again either, ringing up her next order two days later with exactly the same flat attention he gave everything, as though he’d already forgotten he’d said it at all, or had simply never expected it to matter to anyone the way it had started mattering to her.

For two days she’d let the fact sit in the same part of her mind she kept everything else that hadn’t finished being useful yet, alongside the pumping station and the shape of whatever had happened behind her ear, one more open problem in a week that had produced an unusual number of them. She ran the numbers on it twice without meaning to, walking to school, waiting for a bus, standing at her own bench with a board half forgotten in front of her, the way she ran any number that hadn’t finished convincing her it was real: three hundred against what she actually made in an average month, three hundred against the four hundred she was paying nobody for nothing every month she stayed unhoused, three hundred against a version of her life that had a door in it she could close behind herself.

By the third day it had stopped sitting quietly. She caught herself, standing at the parts board on her phone during a spare period, typing Fitch Automotive into a search bar before she’d consciously decided to look it up at all, as though some part of her had already made the decision and was simply waiting for the rest of her to catch up. The listing that came back was three lines long, an address, a phone number, and a single review from four years ago praising a fair price on a brake job, which told her almost nothing and told her, at the same time, that the business was real, current, and exactly as unremarkable as Szabo had made it sound.

* * *

The shop was called Fitch Automotive on a sign that had lost most of one letter to weather, a two-bay garage with a single working lift and a smell of old oil baked into the concrete floor so thoroughly it would probably outlast the building itself. A row of tires leaned against the outside wall, tread worn smooth, waiting on a scrap pickup that had clearly been promised and not yet delivered, and a bell wired above the door announced her the same way the one at Duarte’s had, an actual bell rather than anything electronic, the kind of shop that had never once considered replacing a thing that already worked.

Fitch himself was in his sixties, moved like his knees genuinely had given out on him the way Szabo had said, one hand braced against the doorframe every time he shifted his weight, and looked her up and down once when she introduced herself, the specific frank assessment of somebody deciding, in under three seconds, whether a person was going to be trouble. He asked how she knew Szabo, and she told him, and something in his face settled slightly once she had, as though the introduction itself carried more weight with him than anything she could have said about herself directly.

Whatever he saw, it seemed to satisfy him. He led her through the shop floor to a door at the back she hadn’t noticed from outside, past the single working lift with a sedan up on it mid-repair, a radio somewhere playing the same low background hum she’d heard at Duarte’s, and the bay behind it was smaller than she’d pictured and better than she’d hoped. Concrete floor, swept clean recently enough that she could see the broom marks still in it. A single window high up on one wall too small to be much use for anything but light, glass grimed with years of shop dust on the outside but intact, no cracks she could see. A service door to the alley that had its own separate lock, older than the one on the main shop but solid when she tested the handle. Enough space, once the old tires and the ruined workbench were hauled out, for a bed against one wall and her own bench against the other with room left to actually move between them without turning sideways.

Fitch stood in the doorway while she looked it over, not hovering exactly, just present, the way a person stands in a doorway when they want you to take your time without feeling like you’re being watched too closely doing it, one shoulder braced against the frame and his weight kept mostly off the knee that was clearly giving him the most trouble. He mentioned, almost as an afterthought, that the wall behind where the workbench had stood ran warmer than the rest of the room in winter, something to do with a flue from the shop’s own heater running through it, which he seemed to consider a selling point worth naming even though nothing about how he said it suggested he was trying particularly hard to sell her on anything.

There was a bathroom, shared with the shop, accessible through an interior door that Fitch said he’d put a proper lock on for her if she took the place, small but clean, a toilet and a sink and a shower stall that looked like it hadn’t seen much use beyond whatever the last mechanic who’d worked here had needed it for. A single outlet on the near wall that he tested for her himself, plugging in a work light and watching it come on without so much as a flicker, and a second outlet by the door he hadn’t mentioned until she pointed at it, which turned out to work just as cleanly once he checked it too.

He wanted three hundred a month, cash, first and last up front, no lease, no application, no questions about her age beyond the one he’d already silently answered for himself in the first three seconds of meeting her. He said, when she asked what happened if something went wrong, wiping his hands on a rag that had clearly given up being clean sometime last decade, that he’d give her a week’s notice if he ever needed the space back for something, and expected the same courtesy in return, and that as far as anyone else was concerned, if anyone ever asked, she was helping him out around the shop in exchange for a place to stay, which was a version of the truth close enough that neither of them would ever have to remember it as a lie.

He said this last part plainly, in the same unhurried tone he’d used for everything else, without any particular weight attached to it, the way a person states a working arrangement rather than issues an instruction, and Nadia understood, without either of them saying it directly, that he’d done some version of this before, that a sixteen-year-old with cash and no questions asked was not the first stranger he’d ever quietly let a room to, and that whatever had taught him the wisdom of that particular script had probably cost him something to learn. She didn’t ask what, and he didn’t offer it, and the not-asking felt, standing there in the doorway of a bay that was rapidly starting to feel like it might actually be hers, like the correct and only acceptable form the conversation could take.

He asked, almost as an afterthought while she was still looking around the bay a second time, whether she needed the shop’s address for anything official, school or otherwise, and she said she might, eventually, and he said that was fine, that mail addressed to the shop got sorted same as anything else, no trouble at all, and moved on to the next thing without making anything more of it. He mentioned, once, that he’d had a nephew about her age who used to help out around the shop on weekends, years back, before the family moved out to the Interior, and that the bay had sat empty since without anyone much minding, which was as close as he came to explaining, unprompted, why he was doing any of this at all.

Nadia did the arithmetic in the time it took him to finish the sentence. Three hundred a month left her more than enough from a normal week’s work, more than the phantom placement category the Ministry still hadn’t found her, more, by a comfortable margin, than the number that had been failing her on every listing she’d checked for two months straight, and against all of it sat a room with a lockable door and a landlord who had already, in the space of ten minutes, told her more useful truths than most adults managed in a month.

She said yes before he’d finished describing the second month’s terms, and he seemed, if anything, mildly amused by how little negotiating she’d felt the need to do, and told her first and last could wait until Friday if she needed the days, which she didn’t, because she had it, folded into the tin she’d carried out here specifically in case the answer was yes.

* * *

This is a very good development, Pip said, that evening, while she carried the first box up from the bus stop, her own tools wrapped in a towel inside it because she trusted nothing else to survive the walk. I did some reading while you were signing nothing, since there was nothing to sign. Three hundred a month is, by every metric I can find, an exceptional rate for a private space of this size in this city. I don’t think Fitch entirely understands what he’s charging you. I don’t think you should tell him.

She didn’t answer out loud, walking the last block with the box balanced against her hip, but she thought, clearly enough that she suspected it counted as answering anyway, that she had no intention of telling him anything of the sort.

Moving in took most of two evenings and cost her nothing beyond the labour of it, since she owned almost nothing that needed moving, a fact she registered without any particular feeling attached to it, the way she registered most facts about her own life that would have looked, from the outside, like they ought to hurt more than they did.

The bed came secondhand off a listing three blocks from Fitch’s shop, forty dollars and a promise to come back for it with a hand truck, which she borrowed from Fitch himself without him asking a single question about why she needed it. The seller, an older woman clearing out a spare room, threw in a set of sheets that still had the fold lines from the packaging, unused, a gift she offered so plainly that Nadia accepted it without the usual internal debate about whether accepting things from strangers was a habit worth keeping.

The bench she built the same way she’d built the one at Kelso House, a door laid flat, this time across two proper sawhorses instead of filing cabinets, because a proper hardware store had proper sawhorses for twelve dollars each and she had, for the first time in longer than she could remember, twelve dollars to spend on something that wasn’t strictly necessary. She levelled it with a folded cardboard shim under one leg, checked it with a marble she kept in her tool roll for exactly that purpose, watched it sit dead still in the centre of the door, and moved on satisfied.

Ferreira, when Nadia told her, two days after the fact rather than before, took the news the way she took most things these days, with a tired kind of relief that had genuine warmth underneath the tiredness. She asked for the address, wrote it in the file without making a production of it, and said, almost too quietly to be sure she’d meant it to be heard, that she was glad, that this was one fewer thing she had to worry about on a caseload that gave her precious little to be glad about most weeks.

She asked, too, in the same practical tone she asked everything, whether the arrangement was in writing anywhere, and Nadia said no, and Ferreira said that was probably for the best, in the same breath, which told Nadia more about how the system actually worked than any of the official paperwork ever had. A written lease would have needed a guardian’s signature nobody could provide. A handshake needed nothing but two people willing to trust each other, and apparently that was still, in this city, in this particular corner of it, sometimes enough.

She set the bench up first. Before the bed, before anything else in the room found a place, she had the bench against the wall under the one window, the hot-air station at the back out of any draft, the totes stacked and labelled exactly the way they’d always been labelled, each one set down in the same relative position it had held at Kelso House and, before that, in whatever room she’d had before Kelso House, an order she’d apparently been carrying with her for years without ever once questioning why it had to be that specific order.

She ran a cable to the outlet Fitch had tested for her and brought the bench supply up cold, watching the display climb through its startup sequence and settle clean at zero, no fault, no flicker, a small unremarkable confirmation that felt, tonight, like more than it should have. She plugged in the hot-air station next and let it run through its own warm-up cycle, standing there with her hand near the nozzle the way she always did, checking the airflow against habit rather than against any actual doubt that it would work.

Then she stood in the middle of the empty concrete floor, in a room with a door that locked from the inside with a key that existed nowhere but on her own keyring, and let herself simply look at it for a while. The bare bulb overhead threw everything into a flat, even light, no shadows in the corners the way Kelso House had always had shadows in its corners, and the concrete under her boots was cold through the soles the way concrete always was, and none of it was much to look at by any ordinary measure, and she stood there anyway, in the quiet, for longer than the moment strictly required, and didn’t say anything out loud, and didn’t need Pip to tell her what her own face was doing, because for once she already knew.

You’re smiling, Pip said anyway, quietly, like it had noticed something worth noting and wasn’t sure whether pointing it out would ruin it. I’ve catalogued eleven of your facial expressions so far and this isn’t one of the ones I already had a name for.

“Don’t make it weird,” Nadia said out loud, to the empty room, and started unpacking the second box.

* * *

The cafeteria at Kitsilano ran a hot line and a cold line and a soup station that changed daily, all of it free, all of it unlimited, a hall large enough to seat half the school at once under a ceiling two storeys high that ate most of the noise before it could gather into anything unbearable, and Nadia had been eating there since the second week of September, most days, without once thinking very hard about why she liked it as much as she did.

She liked it today for the same reasons she liked it every day. It was loud in a way that asked nothing of her, four hundred people talking over each other in a room built to absorb exactly that much noise, and she could sit inside it with a full tray and not one person expecting her to contribute anything to the conversation running at her own table, because a person eating was doing a task, and a task excused her from every other task a lunch period usually demanded. The hour had a hard start and a hard stop, the bell at the beginning and the bell at the end, and inside those two fixed points she never once had to guess at what was expected of her, which was more than she could say for almost any other hour of her school day.

She sat where she usually sat, at the end of the table nearest the window, with Priya on one side already deep into a story about a group chat disagreement that had apparently consumed most of her morning, and Hana across from her scrolling through something on her phone while somehow still tracking every word Priya said closely enough to interject at exactly the right moments. Tomasz dropped his tray down two seats over, nodded at Nadia in the particular way wrestlers nodded at each other in public, an acknowledgement that required nothing further, and started in on his own lunch with the grim, methodical focus of somebody already three weeks into thinking about a cut that hadn’t technically started yet.

She went back for seconds without counting how many times, the way she always did, plate after plate disappearing in front of her with the specific efficient hunger of somebody whose body had apparently decided, over the last four days, that food was suddenly the single most important input available to it. Chicken, twice, cut off the bone with more attention than the cafeteria fork really warranted. A second bowl of the soup, which had beans in it, which she ate slower than everything else because something about it seemed to matter more than the rest, though she couldn’t have said why if anyone had asked her, a specific pull toward it that felt less like appetite and more like a component finally getting the voltage it had been starved of.

She took an extra carton of milk on the second pass through the line, and then a third on the way back for the chicken, drinking them cold and fast between bites of everything else, and noticed, distantly, that none of it was landing the way a full stomach usually landed, that the sense of having eaten enough kept arriving and then quietly withdrawing itself again a few minutes later, replaced by the same low insistent pull toward the serving line she’d been ignoring all through the first two plates. It wasn’t unpleasant. It wasn’t even particularly noticeable unless she stopped to look directly at it, the way most of her own body’s quieter signals stayed invisible until she went looking for them on purpose, and today, for whatever reason, she simply hadn’t felt the need to go looking.

You’ve eaten more today than I have data for from any previous day, Pip said, quiet enough that it barely registered over the noise of the room. I don’t have a great deal of data, admittedly. I’ve technically only been keeping records for four days. But this is a notable spike even against that very small sample.

Nadia didn’t answer, working through the second bowl of soup with the flat unhurried focus she gave anything that felt, in some way she hadn’t bothered examining, necessary rather than optional.

Priya noticed before Hana did, glancing at Nadia’s tray with something between amusement and genuine surprise, setting down her fork mid-sentence to actually look. “Are you actually going back for thirds,” she said, not quite a question, already reaching for her own half-finished plate as though comparing portions might settle it.

“I’m hungry,” Nadia said, which was true, flatly true, truer than most things she said out loud in this cafeteria, and Priya laughed, the short easy laugh of someone who’d decided the whole thing was funny rather than worth a second thought, and went back to whatever she’d been saying before, the topic already moving on without waiting to see whether Nadia had anything to add to it.

Nobody at the table thought anything else of it. Wrestling season was coming, and half the table already knew, in the vague way people knew things about friends of friends, that wrestlers ate strangely around a cut, more or less, at odd hours, in odd quantities, and a girl going back for thirds read, to everyone at that table, as exactly that and nothing stranger. Tomasz, without looking up from his own tray, offered the unsolicited opinion that thirds this early in the season was either very smart or very stupid depending entirely on how she planned to feel about it come November, and went back to his food before anyone could ask him to clarify which he actually thought it was.

She finished the second bowl of soup, and went back, unhurried, for a third plate of the chicken, and ate that too, sitting in the loudest room in the building, entirely unbothered by any of it, for the one clean hour of the day that asked absolutely nothing of her at all.

You ate considerably more than you did yesterday, Pip observed, once the bell had rung and the table was breaking apart into its separate directions, everyone gathering trays and bags at once. I keep expecting the trend to level off and it hasn’t yet. I don’t know if that’s a pattern worth worrying about or just what growing up looks like from the inside. I don’t have anything to compare it to. I’ve never done this before.

Nadia carried her tray to the return window without answering, filing the observation the way she filed most of Pip’s observations lately, alongside everything else she didn’t yet have enough data to explain.

* * *

She walked back to Fitch’s that evening in the last of the daylight, past a stretch of the Drive she was starting to know by its specific pattern of what was open and what wasn’t at this hour, the bakery-that-wasn’t-a-bakery-any-more, the hardware store with its unreliable ten-minute sign, Duarte’s two doors down from the laundromat with its light already off for the night. She let herself in through the alley door with her own key, the lock turning smooth and clean under her hand, and stood a moment in the doorway before she flicked the light on, into a room that held nothing yet but a bed, a bench, and the particular quiet of a space that belonged to nobody but her.

The shop on the other side of the interior door had gone quiet too, Fitch closed up for the evening, and she could hear, faintly, the specific settling sounds of an old building at the end of a working day, a compressor ticking as it cooled, a door somewhere shifting on its hinge. It was a different quiet than Kelso House’s quiet had ever been, no upstairs neighbours, no radiator that wasn’t running, no laminated sign on a kitchen wall reminding her to clean up after herself in a kitchen that had never really been hers to begin with.

Can I ask you something, Pip said, once she’d closed the door and thrown the bolt Fitch had installed exactly where he’d promised he would.

“You’re going to anyway,” Nadia said, sitting down on the edge of the bed and pulling her boots off one at a time.

Why didn’t you tell anyone about any of this. The room. The eating. Any of it. You have people who would probably want to know.

Nadia considered the question the way she considered most questions that didn’t have an obvious right answer, turning it over without any particular urgency to resolve it, sitting on the edge of the bed with one boot off and the other still half-laced.

“It’s mine,” she said, finally, which wasn’t the whole answer and was, as far as she could tell, the only part of it that actually mattered.

That’s a very short answer to a very complicated question, Pip said, and there was something in the way it said this that sounded, for the first time since the tunnel, almost like it was smiling too. I’ll allow it. For now. I reserve the right to bring it up again once I understand you better, which, given the rate I appear to be learning things about you, should be sometime around Thursday.

“Don’t count on Thursday,” Nadia said, and pulled the second boot off, and set both of them by the door in the exact spot she intended to keep setting them from now on, because a room this size only worked if everything in it had a place, and she had every intention of making sure it did.

She turned off the light and lay back on a bed that was entirely hers, sheets still smelling faintly of the plastic they’d come wrapped in, in a room with a locked door and a bench she’d already half filled with tools, and let the quiet of the place settle over her the way nothing at Kelso House had ever quite managed to.

Somewhere on the other side of the wall a compressor cycled on, ran for a minute, and cycled off again, the building doing what old buildings did, and she found, listening to it in the dark, that the sound didn’t bother her at all, that it had already, in the space of one evening, become simply the sound this particular room made, the way every place she’d ever slept had eventually earned its own specific texture of quiet.

She slept better that night than she had in longer than she cared to count, and woke once, briefly, somewhere in the small hours, to nothing at all, no unfamiliar noise, no reason for it, only the ordinary fact of a body checking its surroundings out of habit and finding, for once, nothing wrong with any of them.
