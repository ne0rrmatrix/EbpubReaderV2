Chapter 15: No Result

The cold had come in properly overnight, the kind that found every gap in the bay’s old brick and turned the concrete floor into something she felt through two pairs of socks before her feet had even finished waking up, and Nadia lay a minute longer than she needed to, watching her own breath collect above the cot in a thin visible cloud, before she made herself get up and deal with it the way she dealt with anything a morning handed her, methodically, without complaint, because complaining had never once made a room warmer.

The scooter sat exactly where she’d left it, propped against the wall beside the bench, and she looked at it the way she’d looked at it every morning for six days running now, waiting for it to explain itself and getting nothing back but the same ordinary shape it had always had, cracked deck, mismatched paint, the small display glowing steady the moment she checked it, as though the whole episode on the fire road had simply never happened to it at all.

She’d gone over it twice more since that Saturday, multimeter out both times, and found precisely what she’d found the first night: a machine reporting perfect health in every measurable respect, which was, by now, its own kind of evidence rather than the absence of any. A fault that hid from every instrument she owned wasn’t a fault her instruments were built to see, and she’d said as much to Pip on the ride home and meant it, and six days of thinking about it since hadn’t moved her an inch off that conclusion.

You’re doing the thing again, Pip said, arriving mid thought the way it usually did at this hour, distracted by whatever else was competing for it, the words landing a beat later than they would have underground. Standing there looking at it like it owes you an apology.

“It does,” Nadia said, and pulled her jacket off the hook, and didn’t elaborate, because there wasn’t an elaboration that would have made the sentence any less true.

She ate standing at the bench before she let herself think about the day properly, oats from the tin, water rather than the milk she couldn’t currently afford to keep stocked, and ran the whole problem again while she ate, the same way she’d run it five or six times already that week, because a fault that refused to resolve deserved another pass rather than an early verdict.

Two things, now. Not one strange event she could file as an anomaly and set aside, but two, close enough together in her own year that she’d stopped being able to pretend they were unrelated. Pip, waking underground with no warning and no explanation she’d ever managed to extract from it beyond its own honest admission that it didn’t fully understand its own situation either. And now a scooter that had gone completely dead for the better part of half an hour, an hour’s walk from the nearest working diagnostic she owned, on a night that had otherwise looked exactly like every other night she’d ridden it out to the site and back.

She had spent six years learning not to trust coincidence in hardware, and she wasn’t going to start now, not with a sample of two events that both happened, once she actually laid the dates side by side, close enough to a session at the site that the pattern was hard to look away from once she’d noticed it at all.

She had wrestling that evening and Pearce’s the day after, and the site itself wasn’t due again for the better part of another week, the two-week rhythm she’d settled into around the cut leaving her nothing to test against down there for a while yet. Whatever she did about the scooter and about Pip, she was going to have to do it up here first, with whatever she already had on hand, before she got anywhere near the one place she actually suspected the answer lived.

Practice ran conditioning that evening, sprints and circuit stations Val timed off her phone rather than a whistle, and Nadia moved through it the way she moved through most conditioning now, present enough to hit every mark Val called and distant enough that Priya asked her twice, on the walk to the changeroom afterward, whether she’d actually heard a single thing she’d just said. She hadn’t, not properly, and said so honestly rather than pretending otherwise, and Priya laughed the way she laughed at most of Nadia’s flat admissions, the specific short laugh of someone who found the honesty funnier than an apology would have been, and didn’t ask a second time what had actually been occupying her instead.

Tomasz caught her stretching afterward and asked whether she was still riding that ridiculous scooter of hers, word of it having apparently reached him through whatever channel carried gossip through this particular gym, and she said she was, and he said something about wanting a look at it sometime, and she said maybe, in the specific tone that meant probably not, and he let it go the way he let most things go with her, without pushing past the first no.

* * *

She got the notebook out that night, the ordinary one she used for repair jobs rather than the one she kept for the site, and wrote the two dates down side by side the way she’d write two failure reports on the same model of board, looking for the thing they had in common rather than the thing that made them different.

Pip’s first real evidence of anything, so far as she’d ever been able to establish it, sat within a day of one of her earliest visits to the pumping station. The scooter had died within an hour of her walking back from a session that had ended, by her own log, entirely ordinary. Neither event had happened anywhere else. Neither had happened on an evening she hadn’t gone down through the roof hatch first.

She sat with that for a while, turning it over the way she turned over any correlation before she let herself call it a cause, because two data points made a line and a line could still be an accident of exactly two data points and nothing more. But a repair tech who ignored a pattern because the sample was small had never been much of a repair tech in her experience, only a lucky one, and luck ran out exactly when the pattern needed to have held.

She ran the ordinary explanations first, because a repair tech who skipped the boring diagnosis in favour of the interesting one was a repair tech who eventually got burned by something a five minute check would have caught. Cold could kill a battery temporarily, and she’d already ruled that out with the spare cell, tested in weather colder than the fire road had actually been that night. Vibration could work a connector loose over enough kilometres, and she’d checked every crimp herself, twice, with tools rather than her eyes. A firmware fault in a controller with no firmware to speak of wasn’t really a category of explanation at all, not in a machine she’d built entirely analogue on purpose, with her own hands, from parts she could still name in order. She crossed each one off the page as she worked through it, the same way she crossed a component off a suspect list once she’d confirmed it clean, and was left, at the end of the list, with exactly one variable she hadn’t yet found a way to measure.

Are we doing the whiteboard thing, Pip asked, more present now than it had been at breakfast, something in the question suggesting it had noticed the notebook come out and knew, by now, roughly what that meant. You’ve got the face on.

“There’s no whiteboard,” Nadia said.

You know what I mean.

“I’m listing what I actually know instead of what I assume,” Nadia said, which was, in fact, exactly the whiteboard thing, and she didn’t bother denying it a second time.

What she actually knew: both incidents involved equipment that was hers, that she had personally handled, at a location with a field density high enough to register on a scale she had no instrument to read properly. What she assumed, and could not currently distinguish from what she knew, was that the field itself was the mechanism, rather than some property of the equipment, or of the building, or of nothing at all beyond her own pattern matching working a little too hard on too few examples.

The difference mattered enormously and she couldn’t currently test it, because she owned no way to put a number on the one variable she actually suspected. She could weigh a board. She could read a voltage. She had never once, in six years, needed to measure a field, because nothing she’d fixed for money had ever required it, and the gap in her own toolkit sat there now looking exactly like every other gap she’d ever had to close before she could finish a job properly.

So you need a meter, Pip said, arriving at the same place she had, a little ahead of her for once, which happened rarely enough that she noticed it when it did.

“I need a meter,” she agreed, and underlined the word once, because a plan with one clear next step was worth more than a page of correct observations with nowhere to go.

Buying one outright, new, was not a version of the week that survived contact with the tin in her drawer, not against rent and a grocery budget already stretched thin by a cut that hadn’t even reached its hard weeks yet. That left the parts board, the same place she’d found the scooter’s frame, the same place she found most of what she owned, and she pulled it up before she let herself go to bed, scrolling the categories she rarely used tucked at the bottom of the listings under headings nobody bothered to sort properly.

* * *

She found it that same evening, a scatter of home security gear and a single ancient laptop somebody wanted forty dollars for despite a cracked hinge, and among it a listing that read consumer field meter, housing cracked, no display, ward installer downsizing, thirty obo.

She messaged the seller before she’d finished reading it twice, a habit that had served her well enough with the scooter that she’d stopped questioning it, and arranged to meet him the following afternoon at a coffee shop two blocks from Pearce’s, in the narrow window she’d gotten expert at filling efficiently between her shift ending and practice starting.

The coffee shop turned out to be the kind that had been renovated exactly once in the last twenty years, a run of exposed brick along one wall that had probably been fashionable when somebody first knocked the drywall off it and now just looked tired, mismatched chairs collected from wherever chairs got collected from, a chalkboard menu with the prices rewritten often enough that the ghost of at least three older numbers still showed faintly under each new one. Rain had started somewhere on her walk over, the fine persistent kind Vancouver ran through most of the winter rather than anything that ever properly committed to falling, and she shook it off her jacket just inside the door before she found him at a corner table, a canvas bag of what looked like half a garage’s worth of tools sitting on the chair beside him.

He turned out to be an older man, retired rather than downsizing in any way that suggested trouble, clearing out a garage full of tools from thirty years installing wards on buildings that mostly didn’t need them any more, he told her, unprompted, while she turned the meter over in her hands checking the obvious things first. The housing had a crack running the length of one side, old, the plastic gone slightly yellow around the edge of it the way plastic did after enough years near a window, and the display sat dark and unresponsive no matter which of the two buttons she pressed.

“Battery, probably,” he said, watching her check it the way a man who’d fixed his own equipment for three decades watched anyone else attempt the same thing, equal parts curious and faintly territorial about it. “Or the ribbon behind the display. Never got around to it. Didn’t seem worth the bother for a tool I wasn’t using any more.”

She didn’t correct him, mostly because she hadn’t opened the housing yet to know whether he was right, and paid the thirty dollars in cash without haggling, because the price already sat well under what a working one would cost her and there was no version of this negotiation that improved on that math.

“Ward work,” he said, as she put it away, something in his tone suggesting he’d clocked her more carefully than she’d expected in the last five minutes, the specific attention of a man who’d spent thirty years noticing which teenagers actually knew what they were holding and which ones were buying a prop. “You installing, or studying?”

“Neither,” she said. “Just curious what it reads.”

He looked at her a moment longer than the answer strictly required, the way people occasionally did when her flat honesty landed somewhere they hadn’t expected it to, and then shrugged, the particular shrug of a man who’d decided a stranger’s reasons were her own business and not worth the effort of chasing. “Good instrument, when it worked. Better than what most installers carry now. You’ll get an honest number out of it, once it’s running.”

She thanked him, meant it, and walked the two blocks back to Pearce’s with the meter in her bag, already running the crack and the dead display through the same mental checklist she ran on a hundred other devices before this one, and clocked in three minutes early with most of a repair plan already sketched out in her head.

* * *

She got to it properly that night, once the bay had gone quiet around her, Fitch’s light already off on the other side of the wall, the compressor cycling through its patient rhythm the way it always did once the day’s noise had cleared out of the building.

The crack in the housing turned out to be cosmetic, exactly the kind of thing that looked worse than it was and never actually explained a functional fault, and she set it aside without a second thought, because a scratch on a case had never once caused a display to go dark on its own. She opened the back panel instead, four small screws she recognised from a dozen other consumer devices built to the same lazy standard, and found the battery contact corroded through in a thin green crust along one edge, the same slow water ingress failure she’d fixed for the boy with the hearing aid not two weeks earlier, an old, familiar fault in an unfamiliar instrument.

She cleaned it the way she always cleaned it, a fine brush and a few careful passes of the solution she kept in her kit for exactly this job, fitted a fresh battery from the small stock she kept for jobs precisely this size, and pressed the power button once, without much expectation either way, because a corroded contact explained a dead display often enough that she’d learned not to celebrate a fix before she’d actually confirmed it held.

The display lit.

It came up plain, a single number on a small grey readout, no branding beyond a faded manufacturer’s mark she didn’t recognise, and she held it a moment in the quiet of the bay, watching the number sit steady, unmoving, reading whatever the bay itself happened to register at rest.

She didn’t know yet what the number meant, only that it was a number, produced by a working instrument, reading something real rather than nothing at all, and that was already further than she’d gotten with either of the two anomalies actually sitting in front of her.

It’s alive, Pip said, something almost delighted in it, the particular tone it used for a working fix regardless of how small.

“It’s alive,” Nadia agreed, and set it carefully on the bench beside the scooter, and looked at the two of them together for a while, the mystery and the instrument she’d built to start interrogating it, before she finally let herself go to bed.

* * *

She meant to start measuring the following evening, and instead spent most of it reading, because a working instrument without a framework to interpret it was, she decided somewhere around her second cup of tea, only half a tool, and she had never once in her life started using an instrument before she understood roughly what a good reading was supposed to look like.

She pulled up the class guides the way anyone would, the free public archive every classed practitioner in the province had access to, the same material a ward installer or a Guild recruit or a bored teenager with a new element had studied for as long as any of it had existed, and started reading with the specific expectation of a repair tech opening a service manual, looking for the section that explained how the thing actually worked before it told her what to do with it.

That section didn’t exist.

She read for over an hour before she let herself accept the conclusion properly, moving through page after page of what she’d assumed, going in, would function as background theory, and finding instead procedure stacked on procedure, numbered steps with no explanation attached to any of them beyond the bare instruction to perform them, in order, for a specified duration, and observe whatever the body eventually reported back.

Phase Two, one entry read, in the flat unadorned language the whole archive seemed to share. Seated, low stimulus environment, no fewer than twenty minutes daily. Continue until a warming sensation is reported at the base of the sternum. Progress to Phase Three only once this has occurred consistently across no fewer than seven consecutive sessions.

Nowhere did it say why twenty minutes rather than fifteen, why the sternum rather than anywhere else, why seven sessions rather than five or ten, or what was actually happening inside a body during any of it that produced the warming sensation the text treated as a simple, self-evident checkpoint. She read three more entries from three different sections of the archive, hoping one of them would finally break format and explain something, and found the same shape every time, confident, specific, entirely procedural, and never once curious about its own mechanism.

She tried a second entry, further down the same course listing, in case the pattern was a fluke of one particular author rather than the whole discipline, and found the same shape wearing different words. Phase Five, this one read. Standing, weight evenly distributed, palms open. Direct attention toward the extremities until a tingling sensation is reported. Discontinue immediately if the sensation becomes painful rather than warm. Consult a certified instructor before proceeding to Phase Six. No certified instructor existed for what she needed, and even if one had, a mentor meant a second person who could place her, and she’d built her entire cover on staying a person nobody needed to place.

“This is a recipe,” she said out loud, mostly to herself, the way she talked through a fault when nobody else was in the room to hear it. “It’s not a manual. It doesn’t explain anything. It just tells you what to do and when to expect a result.”

Welcome to forty years of published material, Pip said, sharper than it had been all evening, the particular alertness it carried whenever a subject actually interested it rather than merely occupying it. I’ve read most of it, or close enough. All of it reads exactly like that. I assumed you knew.

“I assumed there’d be more underneath it somewhere. A version for people who actually wanted to know why.”

There isn’t. I’ve looked. Genuinely looked, not the kind of looking where I give up after twenty minutes, because it bothers me the same way it’s bothering you right now. A pause, the kind she’d learned by now usually meant it was choosing its next sentence with more care than usual. Nobody built the underneath. The recipes worked well enough on their own that nobody who could have built it ever bothered. It’s forty years of people following instructions that happen to be correct, and not one person writing down why, because writing it down was never the part that paid.

She sat with that for a long moment, the meter dark on the bench beside her, its cable coiled the way she always coiled a cable when she wasn’t actively using it, and let the fact settle into her the way an unwelcome diagnosis usually settled, not as a surprise exactly, more as confirmation of something she’d already half suspected without having the evidence yet to say so outright.

“So if I follow it,” she said slowly, “I get whatever it promises, eventually, provided I do exactly what it says. And if something goes wrong partway through, or something about me doesn’t match whatever body they wrote it for, there’s nobody to ask why, because the people who wrote it don’t know either.”

That’s about the shape of it, yes.

“That’s not a manual,” Nadia said again, quieter this time. “That’s a checklist somebody copied so many times nobody remembers what it was originally checking.”

I don’t think I could have put it better, Pip said, and meant it, she could tell, the particular flatness it used when it had stopped being clever on purpose and had simply agreed with something because the thing was true.

She closed the tab eventually, not because she’d found what she was looking for, but because she’d confirmed, thoroughly enough to trust the conclusion, that it wasn’t there to find. Whatever was actually happening to the field when a body did what these instructions described, the archive had no interest in telling her, and no capacity to, and she wasn’t going to get an explanation for a scooter that shouldn’t run or a companion that shouldn’t exist by asking a document that couldn’t explain its own most basic procedure.

If nobody had built the underneath, she was going to have to look at the number herself and build it in the only way she’d ever built anything, from the outside in, one confirmed measurement at a time, discarding whatever didn’t hold up and keeping only what did. She wasn’t attempting whatever the archive was describing. She was watching a number. It felt, filed that plainly, like a considerably smaller project than the one the guides seemed to think everyone was undertaking, and she found she preferred it that size.

* * *

She started the following morning, a new notebook, thin and cheap, the kind Pearce sold three for a dollar near the till, because this felt like its own project rather than an extension of the one she already kept for the site, and the site’s notebook already had enough of its own weight to carry without a second subject added to it.

Her first entry was simple by design, because she’d learned, from years of tracing faults she didn’t yet understand, that a complicated first measurement usually told her nothing beyond how complicated it had been to take. She read the bay at rest before she’d touched anything, sitting on the stool by the bench with the meter flat in her palm, and wrote the number down along with the time, the date, and a single line describing the conditions, because a number without its conditions was a number she couldn’t trust later.

She read it again standing beside the scooter, cable disconnected, nothing running, and got the same number to within a margin she was fairly sure was just the meter’s own tolerance rather than anything meaningful. She read it a third time with her hand resting flat on the scooter’s frame, in case contact mattered the way it sometimes did with static, and got, again, nothing she could distinguish from the baseline.

No result, she wrote, and underlined nothing beneath it, because there was nothing yet worth underlining.

She carried the meter to school the next day, more out of a stubborn refusal to leave a variable untested than any real expectation it would show her anything, and took a reading in the corner of the cafeteria during the lunch she mostly spent not talking while Priya carried both halves of the conversation on her behalf, and another in the changeroom before practice, tucked into her bag where nobody would ask what it was for. Tomasz noticed her checking it once, between drills, and asked whether it was a heart rate thing, and she said something close enough to true that he lost interest before she’d finished the sentence, which suited her fine. Both readings sat inside the same narrow band the bay had already given her, ordinary, unremarkable, a number that apparently described most of the world equally and told her nothing about the two objects she actually cared about.

No result, she wrote again, twice, dated a day apart, the handwriting a little tighter the second time in a way she noticed and didn’t examine too closely.

She took it out along the fire road that Saturday, not far enough to risk the site itself, only far enough along the route to see whether the reading climbed as the city thinned out behind her the way she half expected it to, if density really did increase the way the seller had implied it should near anything ward-adjacent. It climbed, slightly, a fraction she wrote down carefully and then immediately distrusted, because a fraction that small could as easily have been the cold affecting the battery as anything true about the field itself, and she wasn’t going to promote a maybe into a fact just because a maybe was more interesting than another blank line.

You could just take it down there, Pip offered, on the ride back, careful in the way it got when it wasn’t sure an idea would land well. Next session. See what it actually does somewhere you know for certain the density is high.

“Not yet,” Nadia said. “I want a full week of baseline first. If I don’t know what nothing looks like, I won’t be able to tell the difference when something finally shows up.”

That’s a very patient way to be curious about something.

“It’s the only way that’s ever worked,” Nadia said, and pocketed the meter, and didn’t say anything else the rest of the way home.

She tried it against herself as well, the following night, cautious about how to take a measurement that wouldn’t simply mean nothing the way every other reading that week already had, and settled on holding the meter flat against the side of her skull, near the small dead patch of scar behind her ear where Pip’s hardware had lived, unremarked, for as long as she could remember having a body at all.

Do you have to point it right at me, Pip said, something careful under the dry delivery that she noticed and didn’t immediately name, though she filed it the way she filed most things that didn’t quite match the tone they arrived in.

“It’s not pointed at anything. It just reads whatever’s in front of it.”

I know. I still don’t love it. Devices get inspected, and I’m technically a device, and every single inspection anyone’s ever run on me has ended with somebody deciding I was broken. I’d rather you not be the one holding the instrument, even if I know exactly why you’re holding it.

She lowered the meter without being asked twice, because a request that specific, arriving in that particular register, wasn’t one she needed explained to her before she honoured it. “I wasn’t trying to find out if you’re broken.”

I know that too. It’s not really about you. A pause, brief, the kind that usually meant it had decided against saying more than it needed to. Try it anyway. I’d rather know than not, same as you would.

She read it once, quickly, and got a number indistinguishable from the rest of the week’s readings, no spike, nothing a cheap grey box could tell her about whatever was actually happening inside her own skull. She wrote it down anyway, conditions and all, and didn’t take a second reading that night, because Pip had asked her not to point it twice and she saw no reason a single data point mattered enough to cost it that.

Hana had the group at the diner two blocks from the gym that Sunday, the way Hana occasionally decided things without asking anyone first, and Nadia went because Hana had assumed she was coming and never actually checked, which remained the only reliable mechanism she had for ending up anywhere social at all. She took a reading in the corner booth while Priya argued with Tomasz about a movie neither of them had actually finished watching, the meter half hidden under her jacket on the seat beside her, and got a number that matched every other ordinary room she’d tested that week, warm bodies and fluorescent light and nothing underneath any of it that a cheap grey box could find.

No result, she wrote later that night, back at the bench, and added the diner to the growing list of places that had told her nothing.

By the following Wednesday the notebook held a full page of entries, half a dozen different settings, one column of numbers that refused to do anything a scientist could have called a pattern, and the same two words closing nearly every line of it. Pearce asked, ringing up a customer while she restocked a bin nearby, what the little grey box on her hip was for, and she said she was testing something for a job, which was near enough to true that he didn’t ask a second question, and went back to the till satisfied.

She read the page back once before bed that night, the way she read anything before she trusted her own conclusions, and found nothing wrong with any individual entry and nothing useful in the whole of them stacked together.

No result, she wrote again, and closed the notebook, and set the meter on the shelf above the bench where she’d be able to reach it without thinking in the morning.

For what it’s worth, Pip said, quiet, into the dark of the bay, I don’t think a week of nothing means you’re wrong about the two of them being connected.

“I know,” Nadia said. “It just means I haven’t asked it the right question yet.”

She turned off the light and let that sit, unresolved, the same way she let most things sit that hadn’t finished proving themselves either way, and went to sleep.
