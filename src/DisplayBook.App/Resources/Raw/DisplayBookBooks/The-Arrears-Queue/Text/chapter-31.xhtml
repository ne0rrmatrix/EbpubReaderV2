Chapter 31: Both, Neither

She built the plan the same night Val handed her the two addresses, sitting at the bench with her notebook open to a fresh page, running the arithmetic the way she ran anything that looked, on first pass, like it might actually have a solution hiding somewhere inside it.

Nine days left, once she’d subtracted the two already spent turning the problem over without acting on it. Nine days to move a panel that had taken months to get this wrong, and nine days that also, inconveniently, happened to sit directly inside the final cut before the tournament, the one that mattered, the one she’d already reclassified her whole season around making.

The reclassification alone had cost her weeks of careful work back in the autumn, a whole separate campaign of forms and weigh-ins and quiet negotiation with a body that kept adding lean mass faster than any ordinary training block should have produced, and she wasn’t willing to let nine bad days undo all of that on top of everything else. If she missed weight Saturday, the reclassification became a wasted season rather than a solved problem, and if she missed the clearance, the season ended for reasons that had nothing to do with weight at all. She had never before had to hold both of those outcomes in her head as equally live at the same time.

She wrote the two requirements out side by side, the way she wrote out any two symptoms that seemed to be fighting each other. Fix the panel: more protein, more iron, more calories, a surplus large enough to give her body something to actually repair itself with. Make weight: a deficit, sustained, tight enough to shed the last kilos before Saturday’s scale. Two columns. Two opposite signs on the exact same number.

Underneath the two columns she wrote the actual figures, the ones she’d half-memorised off the printout Val had left with her in November and never quite stopped turning over since. Haemoglobin low. Ferritin low enough that the lab had put a second mark beside it. LDH high. Haptoglobin low enough it barely registered on the scale at all. CK elevated, though that one, she reasoned, might just as easily be the wrestling. She didn’t need a medical degree to read a trend line, only enough of one to know which direction each number needed to move, and every direction pointed the same way: more iron, more protein, more raw material, and enough of a caloric surplus that her body would actually have something to build the fix out of instead of just enough to keep her upright through practice.

Against that she set the wrestling math, the numbers she knew even better, because she’d built this version of the spreadsheet herself back in September and updated it every week since. Current weight. Target weight. Days remaining. Safe weekly loss expressed as a percentage of body mass, the number Val had drilled into the whole team so many times it had stopped sounding like a warning and started sounding like a fact of nature. The two numbers didn’t just disagree. They sat on opposite sides of zero, a deficit demanded by one column and a surplus demanded by the other, both drawn against the same finite grocery budget, the same finite hours, the same finite body.

“There has to be a version where both of these work,” she said, out loud, mostly to convince herself.

Is there, Pip asked, careful. Or is that just the sentence you say before you start a problem you already suspect doesn’t have a clean answer.

She didn’t answer that, and started running the numbers anyway.

She had looked at the two addresses Val had written down for her more than once that first night, turning the slip of paper over the way she turned over any option she’d already half decided against before she’d finished considering it. A youth clinic. A family doctor two bus transfers from the bay. Either one would have given her an actual answer inside a week, a real clearance signed by someone with a licence instead of a plan built out of grocery receipts and hope.

Either one would also have put a stranger with a chart in front of a body that didn’t explain itself the way an ordinary sixteen-year-old’s body was supposed to. A resting heart rate a competitive athlete twice her age would have envied. A healing rate no clinic panel had a column for. Bloodwork that would read, to anyone without the specific context she couldn’t hand over, like a list of symptoms belonging to a girl in considerably worse trouble than she actually was, filed straight into a record the Ministry’s office could pull the moment Bhandari’s next review came due. She had built an entire year around keeping her file boring. A stranger’s honest chart was the single fastest way she knew to make it interesting, and interesting, for her, had never once meant safe.

So she had folded the slip of paper back into her notebook instead of her pocket, and built the other plan, the one she could run herself, at the bay, with a scale and a notebook and nine days, because it was the only version of solving this that didn’t also risk solving it directly into a caseworker’s file.

* * *

The first three days ran on hope more than evidence, and she let herself have that, the way she let herself have anything at the start of a plan before the data had a chance to argue with it.

She ate everything she could afford that carried iron and protein without carrying much else, dark meat, liver twice that week, eggs at every meal she could fit them into, a specific dense caloric efficiency she engineered the way she engineered anything, maximum nutritional yield per dollar, per gram, per minute of digestion. She trained lighter than usual at practice, not enough for Val to comment on it, careful, deliberate restraint dressed up as ordinary fatigue. She slept when she could, though sleep had never once been the part of this equation she had spare hours to actually give.

The grocery math alone took most of an evening to get right. Liver was cheap, cheaper than almost anything else on the shelf that carried real iron, but it came in blocks too large for a single serving, which meant freezing the rest in portions she measured out on the kitchen scale to the gram, because guessing wasted money she didn’t have to waste. Eggs were the better trade, a dollar figure she already had memorised down to the cent, and she built three of her five daily meals around them before she let herself add anything else to the plate. She kept a running column in the same notebook, dollars spent against grams of protein delivered, the exact discipline she’d have applied to any other supply problem, and told herself, writing it out, that a well-run budget could probably still find the extra margin the panel needed without blowing the one the scale demanded.

By day three the scale told her the truth the numbers had already been warning her about. She was up, not down, four hundred grams over where the cut plan said she should be standing.

* * *

“The iron’s heavier than the fat it’s replacing,” she said, staring at the readout, working the fault the way she worked any fault that refused to behave. “I’m eating denser. Denser weighs more per calorie, not less. I traded volume for density and the scale doesn’t care which one I was optimizing for.”

You could eat less of the dense food, Pip offered. Keep the iron. Cut the total.

“That’s what I’m already doing,” Nadia said. “That’s the whole plan. I’m already running the tightest version of both columns I can build. The scale’s telling me the tightest version still isn’t tight enough on one side or generous enough on the other.”

She sat with that for a long moment, the specific cold clarity of a system finally showing her its actual constraints instead of the ones she’d hoped it would have. Two requirements. One body. A wallet that had never once, all year, had room for two full demands at the same time.

* * *

She adjusted the plan on day four, tightening the deficit further, trusting the iron-dense choices to do more work per gram than an equivalent mass of anything else would have, and felt the adjustment land in her body almost immediately, a fatigue sharper than the ordinary cut exhaustion she’d spent months learning to work around.

Practice that Thursday cost her more than practice usually cost her. She missed a takedown she’d have hit clean a month ago, her legs a half-second slower into the shot than her mind had already told them to be, and Val watched the miss with the specific attentive stillness Nadia had learned to read as concern rather than criticism.

“You’re dragging,” Val said, afterward, quiet enough that the rest of the team didn’t catch it.

“I’m fine,” Nadia said, the reflex answer, the one that came before she’d even finished deciding whether it was true.

Val didn’t argue with the words directly, just held her eyes a second longer than a casual check would have needed. “Fine doesn’t miss a shot you’ve hit clean since October,” she said. “I’m not asking you to explain it right now. I’m telling you I noticed, and I’m going to keep noticing, because that’s the job.”

“Cutting,” Nadia said, which was true, and also not the whole truth, and she felt the gap between the two versions of the sentence sitting uncomfortably in her chest the whole walk home.

* * *

Day five put the whole equation in front of a math test she couldn’t afford to fail either, forty minutes of quadratics scheduled two class periods after a practice she’d spent running the same tightened deficit that was supposed to be buying her margin for the panel and instead seemed only to be spending margin she didn’t have from somewhere else entirely.

She caught herself rereading the same problem three times without the numbers settling into anything she recognised, a specific fogged blankness that had nothing to do with the maths itself, which she knew cold, and everything to do with a body running two incompatible programs at once and refusing to spare enough for a third. Her hands were cold at the wrist in a warm classroom. The Arrears queue from Thursday’s practice, a deep-tissue bruise along her hip she hadn’t even registered taking, was still draining at a rate that told her exactly how much of her repair budget the deficit had already claimed before the panel ever got a share of it.

She finished the test twelve minutes before the bell, checked her work twice out of habit rather than confidence, and walked out without any real sense of how well she’d done, which frightened her more than a bad grade would have. She had never once, in a year of running two lives at the same time, let the visible one slip without noticing exactly how far it had slipped. She hadn’t noticed this time until it was already twelve minutes too late to fix.

The cafeteria line that lunch put a tray of something warm and starchy directly in front of her at exactly the moment her whole body seemed to organise itself around wanting it, a pull sharper and less negotiable than ordinary hunger, closer to the specific ache she’d learned all year to recognise as her body making its own case for a column she wasn’t currently funding. She counted the coins in her pocket instead of the calories on the tray, the older habit winning out over the newer one, and bought the smaller portion, the one that fit the deficit and starved the argument her body was making just as thoroughly as it starved the panel. She ate it slowly, at the end of the table, and told herself it was enough, the way she told herself that most days now, and didn’t entirely believe it any more than she had the day before.

* * *

By day six the two columns had started actively fighting each other in ways she could no longer paper over with clever substitutions. The deficit, sustained now for nearly a week, had slowed her recovery exactly the way system after system in her own body kept insisting it would, the same seventy percent drop in clearance she’d catalogued months ago as an abstract number and was now living through as an actual, physical fact. Bruises from Thursday’s practice were still dark on Sunday. A blister she’d normally have healed clean in two days was still raw on the fourth.

If the deficit was doing that to her ordinary healing, she understood, sitting with the logic of it at the bench, it was almost certainly doing the same thing to whatever repair process the iron and protein were supposed to be funding. She was feeding the fault and starving the fix at the same time, and no amount of clever substitution changed the fact that both processes drew from the exact same slowed-down account.

She ran the mitigation numbers the way she’d run them a hundred times since the queue first taught her how, and the shape of it was unmistakable once she laid it out plainly. Forty points of a well-absorbed hit cleared clean through active mitigation. The same forty points taken flat off a queue already running behind schedule drained at barely a third of that rate, arrears stacking against arrears, the whole window stretched thinner every time she asked her body to do the work of clearing itself on a fuel supply that had already been rationed twice over. Whatever the panel needed to build new blood, new iron stores, new anything, it was standing in the same queue as the bruise on her hip and the blister on her heel, and the queue didn’t know or care which repair mattered more. It only knew how much substrate had arrived to spend, and this week, both requirements drawing against it, there had never once been enough.

You’re not actually moving the panel, Pip said, gently, once she’d worked the reasoning out loud. You might even be moving it the wrong direction.

“I know,” Nadia said, low. “I ran the logic three different ways tonight hoping one of them would come out differently. None of them did.”

* * *

She kept going anyway, because stopping wasn’t a plan either, and a bad plan followed carefully still produced more useful data than no plan at all followed by nothing. She adjusted again on day seven, shifting the balance further toward the panel, accepting a slower cut in exchange for whatever margin it might buy her body to actually repair something.

The scale punished the choice immediately. She stood on it Sunday morning and found herself six hundred grams over target with six days left before the tournament, a gap that would have been survivable a month ago and now sat considerably closer to the edge of what her body could reasonably shed in the time remaining without doing exactly the kind of damage the panel was already warning everyone about.

She sat with the scale’s number a long moment before she trusted herself to speak, running the last three days back through her memory to see where the miscalculation had actually happened, the way she’d run back through any failed build looking for the exact junction where the plan had stopped matching the result. She’d eaten less on day seven, not more, had trusted the higher iron density to carry the surplus the panel needed on a smaller total mass. The scale said the trust had been misplaced, or the maths had been right and her body simply hadn’t converted the surplus into anything the cut could afford to lose back.

“I bought margin for the panel and spent it against the cut,” she said, flat, staring at the number. “I don’t think there was ever a version where I didn’t have to spend it against one or the other.”

I don’t think there was either, Pip said. I think you’ve been running an experiment for a week now that was always going to come back negative, and I think some part of you already knew that on day one and ran it anyway because you needed to see the negative result yourself before you’d believe it. You don’t usually need nine days of physical evidence to trust a piece of maths you already suspected was true.

Nadia considered that, and found she didn’t have the energy left to argue with it. “Maybe,” she said. “I don’t think I could have believed it any other way. I had to run the actual test. I’ve trusted the maths before and been wrong about which variable actually mattered. I needed to be sure this wasn’t one of those times.”

She lay awake longer than usual that night turning over the other option, the one she kept setting down and picking back up without ever quite letting herself finish the thought. She could tell Val something closer to true. Not everything, never everything, but enough of a version of the truth that Val might understand why an outside clinic wasn’t a door she could safely walk through, might find some quieter way to sign off on her own authority the way she seemed to for half the things this team needed that never appeared on any official form.

You could ask her, Pip offered, careful, the same tone it used for a suggestion it half expected to be refused. She’s already covered for you twice this year without asking questions you couldn’t answer. She might do it a third time.

“Twice was a bruise she assumed was ordinary and a scavenging habit she assumes is about groceries,” Nadia said into the dark. “This is a whole blood panel and a body that heals wrong. That’s not a small favour. That’s asking her to lie on paperwork that has her name on it, for a reason she isn’t allowed to actually know.” She turned onto her side, the mattress cold under the arm she hadn’t warmed yet, and pulled the blanket higher, though it did little against a chill that seemed to be coming from somewhere further inside her than the room itself. “I’m not doing that to her. Not unless there’s nothing else left.”

Is there something else left, Pip asked, and she didn’t have an answer for that either, not one she trusted enough to say out loud.

* * *

Day eight brought the worst of it, a session at the site she’d scheduled weeks ago and couldn’t afford to skip, the queue from the last visit barely cleared, her body running on a deficit that had already cost her most of the margin she usually kept for exactly this kind of demand.

She lasted eleven minutes before her legs gave out the warning her mind had been ignoring for the last three, a specific hollow weakness that had nothing to do with fear and everything to do with a body that had simply run out of the substrate it needed to keep doing what she was asking of it. Her hands, gripping the rail on the second catwalk, felt distant in a way she didn’t like, the strength still technically there but arriving late, a half-second behind where her mind had already placed it, the same lag she’d felt in the shot she’d missed at practice, except here the margin for a late half-second wasn’t a lost takedown. It was a fall onto scrap metal three storeys down.

She climbed out early, the Elite’s slow patrol still two catwalks away, its treads grinding somewhere below in the same unhurried rhythm it always kept, indifferent to her the way it had been indifferent to her all year, and sat on the cold concrete of the entry point for a long time before her legs agreed to carry her the rest of the way home. She counted her own pulse against her wrist to have something to focus on, and it came back faster and thinner than it should have at rest, a number that told her, without needing a lab to confirm it, that the account she’d been drawing against all week had gone further into the red than she’d let herself admit even to Pip.

That was too close, Pip said, something sharp in it that she rarely heard. You need to eat something before you try to walk the rest of that fence line. I mean now. Not when you get home.

“I don’t have anything with me,” Nadia said, the words coming slower than she wanted them to.

Then sit until your pulse comes down on its own, Pip said. I’m not asking this time.

“I know,” Nadia said, and this time she didn’t argue, didn’t offer a plan, didn’t reach for the next clever adjustment. She simply sat with the fact of it, the cold concrete under her, the queue still draining slower than it should have been, and let herself feel, for the first time all week, the full size of the problem she’d been trying to out-engineer.

* * *

She weighed in for the tournament check on day nine, the second-to-last checkpoint before Saturday, and stood on the scale with a number that told her, plainly, what nine days of trying to solve both problems at once had actually bought her.

Eight hundred grams over. Not catastrophic. Not survivable on the current schedule either, not without a final push considerably more severe than anything the panel could afford to absorb.

Val watched the number come up and didn’t say anything for a long moment, the specific silence of someone doing her own arithmetic before deciding which version of it to say out loud.

“You’re not going to make it clean,” Val said, finally. “Not without a cut in the next four days that I already told you I’m not comfortable signing off on medically. And you don’t have a clearance yet either. I checked with the office this morning. Nothing’s come in.”

“I know,” Nadia said, and heard, saying it, how flat and tired the two words had become, worn down by a week of repeating some version of the same sentence to Val, to Pip, to herself, over and over, until the words had stopped carrying any real weight at all.

Val studied her for a moment longer than the number on the scale strictly required, the same attentive stillness from Thursday’s practice, except closer now, closer than Nadia liked being looked at by anyone who actually knew what colour her lips had been last October. “You look worse than you did nine days ago,” Val said. “Not better. I gave you those addresses because I wanted a doctor telling me you were fine, not because I expected you to spend the week trying to prove it yourself with a kitchen scale and willpower.”

“I was trying to fix it,” Nadia said, and the sentence came out smaller than she meant it to, an admission she hadn’t planned on making out loud.

“I believe you,” Val said, not unkindly. “That’s almost the worse part. You’ve been trying all week, and you still look worse, and that tells me something the clearance was always going to tell me eventually anyway.” She didn’t say what, not yet, and Nadia didn’t ask, because some part of her already suspected she wouldn’t like the answer any better spoken aloud than left sitting, unsaid, in the space between them.

“Can I have until Thursday?” Nadia asked. “I know that’s what we already agreed. I’m asking again because I don’t have anything new to tell you today, and I’d rather say that honestly than promise you something I can’t actually deliver.”

Val was quiet for a moment, weighing the request the same careful way she weighed everything involving this particular athlete, a pattern Nadia had watched her run a dozen times over the season without ever quite being able to predict which way it would land. “Thursday,” Val said finally. “But I want you resting between now and then. Not cutting harder. Not pushing the site, whatever it is you actually do out there that you think I don’t notice you’re tired from. Resting. If Thursday comes and there’s still nothing, I’m not waiting for Saturday to make the call I already know I might have to make.”

“Understood,” Nadia said, and meant it, even knowing, standing there on the cold rubber matting with the scale still glowing its unhelpful number behind her, that resting wasn’t a column either side of the equation had left her room to actually spend, not with a site queue still open and a cut still unfinished and a panel that needed more from her than rest alone had ever been going to give it.

* * *

She sat at the bench that night with both notebooks open in front of her, the cut log on one side, the panel’s remembered numbers scrawled from memory on the other, and looked at the two columns the way she’d looked at them nine days ago, except now the hope was gone, replaced by the specific flat certainty of a test that had run its full course and come back exactly the way the underlying physics had always said it would.

“There isn’t a version of this where both work,” she said, out loud, the sentence arriving without any of the reluctance it would have carried a week ago. “I ran it as carefully as I know how to run anything. I fed the panel and starved the cut, and I fed the cut and starved the panel, and every single adjustment in between just moved the damage from one column to the other. I attempted both. I failed both. That’s the actual result, and I don’t think there’s a cleverer version of the experiment I haven’t already tried.”

I know, Pip said, quiet. I watched you try every version. I don’t think you missed one.

“One wallet,” Nadia said, and almost laughed, a short, humourless breath through her nose. “I worked that out months ago. Core-building and the queue and just growing draw from the same account. I wrote it down like it was clever. I don’t think I understood, when I wrote it, that the wallet would ever be this small, or that I’d be the one deciding which bill didn’t get paid.”

You didn’t decide, Pip said. You tried to pay both. The account decided for you.

“Same result,” she said. “Different sentence.”

* * *

She closed both notebooks slowly, the same careful, deliberate motion she gave anything she was filing as finished rather than merely paused, and sat a long moment in the bay’s ordinary quiet, the compressor ticking through its cycle on the other side of the wall, the whole familiar room offering none of its usual comfort tonight.

“Four days,” she said, finally. “Four days until Saturday. Two days until Val needs an answer she can actually report.”

What are you going to tell her, Pip asked.

“I don’t know yet,” Nadia said, and the admission cost her something new this time, a plain honesty she hadn’t practised nearly as often as she’d practised the deflections that usually filled that space, a different shape of exhaustion than the physical kind she’d been carrying all week, the specific tiredness of a person who had run out of clever solutions and hadn’t yet found the nerve to say so out loud to the one person who was going to need to hear it.

She sat with the silence a while longer, the two failed columns still sitting open in her memory even with the notebooks closed, and thought, before sleep finally pulled the thought away from her entirely, that she had never once, in a year full of problems, run out of a version of herself clever enough to solve one.

She had run out now.

She lay down without changing out of her clothes, too tired to manage the extra motions, and stared at the ceiling’s water stain until it stopped resolving into a shape at all. Her pulse still ran a little fast and thin at the wrist, the same unsettled rhythm she’d felt sitting on the cold concrete two days ago, and she made herself breathe through it slowly rather than reach for the phone and start the numbers again, because there was nothing left in either column that another hour of arithmetic was going to change.

Four days to the tournament. Two days to whatever answer Val needed by Thursday. Somewhere behind both of those, a panel that hadn’t moved, that might have moved backward, sitting in a file with her name on it, waiting for someone with the authority to actually read it out loud and act on what it said. She had spent nine days trying to be the one who got to decide how that reading went. Lying there in the dark, the deficit and the queue and the test she’d barely finished all pressing at once against a body that had nothing left to spend on any of them, she understood, plainly and for the first time without any of the reflexive scramble for a counter-argument, that the decision had never actually been hers to make.

Sleep, when it finally came, didn’t feel like rest. It felt like the body simply refusing to negotiate any further that night.
