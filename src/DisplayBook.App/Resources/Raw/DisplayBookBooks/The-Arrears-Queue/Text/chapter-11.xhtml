Chapter 11: Twelve Hours

Three weeks left, and the placement category still sat empty on the wall above her bench, a blank line under two boxes already checked, and Nadia had learned enough by now about the shape of the problem to stop expecting it to fill itself in on its own.

She had tried, in the strict sense of the word, for most of those three weeks. She had walked into eleven different businesses along a stretch of the Drive and Commercial both, résumé printed at the library because Fitch’s shop had no printer worth the name, single sheet, no photo, every real skill she owned compressed down into three bullet points that read, next to a fast food application form, like they belonged to a different person entirely. She had the same conversation eleven times with eleven small variations on the same ending.

Most places wanted a work permit she couldn’t produce without a guardian’s signature, a form that asked for a name in a box she had no honest way to fill. A few wanted references from a previous employer, which she didn’t have, because six years of cash jobs paid directly into a tin didn’t generate references, only money, and there was no polite way to explain to a hiring manager at a coffee shop that her actual employment history consisted of forty-some strangers who’d never once had to fill out a form about her. One manager, kinder than the rest, at a bakery two blocks from Kelso House that had somehow survived where the one near her old room hadn’t, had explained the insurance liability of hiring a minor without proper guardianship documentation in enough detail that she understood, by the end of it, that the door wasn’t closed to her specifically so much as closed to an entire category of person the paperwork had never been built to include, and that he was genuinely sorry about it in a way that didn’t change anything either of them could do.

A grocery store had turned her away at the door once they saw her age on the application, a phone shop had said they’d call and hadn’t, and a laundromat two doors down from a shop she recognised had simply had no positions at all, the woman behind the counter apologising in a way that felt more practised than personal. A fast food place near the SkyTrain had actually offered her a shift trial, the closest she’d come in eleven tries, before the manager mentioned, halfway through, that all new hires needed a guardian’s emergency contact on file before their first day, a requirement stated so casually that she understood it was simply routine to him, not a wall built specifically to keep her out, which somehow made it worse rather than better. By the ninth conversation she had stopped bothering to hope any particular one would be different, and by the eleventh she was running the whole exchange on something close to autopilot, answering questions she already knew the shape of before they finished being asked, the specific rhythm of it starting to feel less like a job search and more like a diagnostic she already knew the outcome of, run again and again on the off chance the result might finally come back different.

You’ve had eleven separate conversations that all end the same way, Pip observed, quietly, on the walk home from the eleventh one. I’ve started to notice you don’t react much to the pattern any more. Is that because you expected it, or because reacting stopped being useful somewhere around the sixth one.

“Both,” Nadia said, which was true, and kept walking, past a bus shelter with half its glass replaced by plywood and a bench nobody had sat on in the time it took her to walk past it.

For what it’s worth, Pip added, a block later, I don’t think any of the eleven were wrong to say no. I think the forms themselves are wrong. That’s a different thing, and I don’t know if the distinction is useful to you right now, but I wanted to say it anyway.

“It’s not, really,” Nadia said. “But thanks.”

* * *

The twelfth business was Pearce Hardware, a narrow storefront on a side street off the Drive that she’d walked past a dozen times without ever going in, distinguished mainly by a hand-lettered sign taped to the security grate that read back in ten minutes in handwriting she recognised from the two other times she’d passed it and found the grate down at four in the afternoon.

The inside told its own story before anyone said a word to her. Shelving that ran floor to ceiling on three walls, deep enough that half the stock toward the back was probably older than she was, a layer of dust on the top shelves that suggested a ladder nobody used often enough. Bins of fasteners along one wall organised by a system that had clearly made sense to someone once, hand-labelled cards gone yellow and curling at the corners, half the labels no longer matching what actually sat behind them. A single working register at a counter scarred with decades of receipts torn against its edge, and beside it, propped open, a ledger book instead of anything digital, columns of handwriting in at least three different pens going back further than she could see from where she stood, the paper itself gone soft and furred at the edges from years of hands.

A second, smaller counter near the back held a key-cutting machine older than the one at Duarte’s, its brass shavings swept into a small pile beneath it that looked like it hadn’t been emptied in days, and past that, a doorway curtained off with a sheet of plastic sheeting rather than a proper door, leading, presumably, to a stockroom she hadn’t been invited into yet. A hand-painted sign above the register listed the store’s hours in a font clearly chosen decades ago, the paint chipped enough at the edges that the closing time had become genuinely ambiguous between five and six depending on how generously a person read the remaining strokes. The whole place smelled of machine oil and old cardboard and something faintly metallic underneath it all, the specific smell of a business that had been selling the same category of thing to the same category of customer for longer than most of its customers had been alive.

The man behind the counter was somewhere in his sixties, built like someone who had spent a working life lifting things heavier than he should have, and looked up from the ledger with the specific weary patience of a man who had heard every possible reason a teenager might walk into his shop and none of them had ever once led anywhere useful.

She told him what she needed, plainly, the same way she told any client what a repair actually cost: a verified placement, twelve hours a week, an employer willing to sign a Ministry form without asking questions the form itself didn’t ask. She didn’t dress it up. Dressing it up had gotten her nowhere in the eleven conversations before this one, and she’d run out of patience for approaches that weren’t working.

He listened to the whole thing without interrupting, the way she’d noticed the older tradespeople in her orbit tended to listen, letting a person finish a sentence before responding to any part of it, a habit that felt, more and more, like something the younger managers at the eleven other places had simply never been taught.

He looked at her for a long moment, the same frank assessment she’d gotten from Fitch a month ago, setting the pen down across the open ledger without losing his page. He asked how old she was, and she told him, and he didn’t react to the number the way some of the eleven had, didn’t so much as pause before his next question, which was whether she’d worked a register before.

She said she had, informally, running her own jobs, and he nodded like that answered more of the question than he’d actually asked, the specific nod of a man deciding not to press further on an answer that had already told him what he needed to know. He said he could genuinely use somebody for stock and the register a few afternoons a week, that he’d had a kid doing it until June who’d gone off to a real job somewhere with real hours and better prospects than a hardware store on a side street could offer anybody, and that the pay was minimum and the work was boring, and that he didn’t much care about paperwork beyond whatever kept him out of trouble with anyone who came asking.

She said that suited her fine, and meant it in the plainest possible sense, because a job that paid poorly and asked little of her was, against every other criterion the last three weeks had taught her to weigh, close to the single most desirable job description she had heard yet.

He said, almost as an afterthought, glancing at the register that had been sitting with a strip of masking tape over one corner of its display for as long as she’d been standing there, that the till had been reading wrong on cash transactions for about two weeks now, off by a few cents in a way that didn’t matter enough to fix and mattered enough to notice every single time it happened.

Nadia looked at the register for exactly as long as it took to recognise the make and guess the fault, a common enough model that half the small shops along this whole stretch of the Drive probably ran some version of it, and said she could probably have that sorted before her first actual shift, if he wanted.

He looked at her the way Fitch had looked at her too, the specific look of a person recalibrating an assessment mid-sentence, one eyebrow lifting slightly before he caught himself and let his expression settle back into its ordinary weariness, and said he’d take her up on that.

* * *

The register was an older mechanical unit with a digital tally bolted on top of it, the same retrofit logic she’d seen on the wrestling room scale and half the equipment in this city, an original machine still doing the physical work while a cheaper add-on tried to keep count of it electronically. She had the back panel off before Pearce had finished explaining exactly how the fault presented itself, and traced the coin path by eye first, the way she traced any mechanical fault, looking for wear before she looked for anything electrical.

The mechanism turned out to be a coin mechanism worn just enough out of calibration to shortchange itself on nickels specifically, a mechanical fault rather than an electronic one, the kind of thing that would have taken a repair tech from a real service company an hour and a call-out fee to diagnose and cost him, if he’d ever bothered calling one, more than the machine itself was probably worth. A tiny spring-loaded gate that sorted coins by size had worn its seat just enough, over however many thousand transactions across however many years, that a nickel occasionally slipped past the sensor meant to count it, registering the coin in the drawer but not on the tally, a small honest miscount that had probably been quietly overcharging the store in its own favour by a handful of cents a week for longer than anyone still working here had bothered tracking.

She had it open and fully adjusted inside twenty minutes, using a set of jeweller’s screwdrivers from her own roll rather than anything the shop owned, bending the gate back a fraction of a millimetre with a pair of fine-tipped pliers and checking the tolerance against a coin held steady in her other hand. Pearce watched over her shoulder for the first few minutes and then drifted back to his ledger, apparently satisfied she wasn’t going to make the problem worse, which she took as a better compliment than anything he actually said.

She ran a dozen test transactions through it afterward, nickels and dimes and quarters in combinations designed to catch the fault if it was still there, counting the coins into the drawer herself and reading the tally back against her own count each time. It wasn’t there any more. The drawer opened clean every time, the display agreeing with the actual coins to the cent, ten times running before she trusted it enough to close the panel back up.

Pearce watched the last few tests without saying much, arms crossed, the same way Val had watched the scale come back to life six weeks ago, and said, when she closed the panel back up, that the kid before her hadn’t known a screwdriver from a butter knife, and that whatever she was charging elsewhere, she was underselling herself doing this for twelve dollars an hour. He asked, not quite as an afterthought, wiping his hands on a rag from under the counter, where she’d learned to do that, and she gave him the same answer she gave everyone, mostly just looking, and he took it the way most people took it, without pushing, though something in the way he studied the closed register a moment longer suggested he understood there was more to the answer than the three words she’d given him.

“You’ll want a proper hire date on the form,” he added, tapping the ledger, “not whatever date makes the maths easiest for you. I’ve had enough trouble with paperwork in this life without fudging a date on top of it.”

“Today’s fine,” she said, and he wrote it down.

“It’s not really about the money,” Nadia said, which was true in a way she didn’t particularly want to explain further, and he seemed to understand well enough not to push it, the way most people who’d run a small business for thirty years understood that a person’s actual reasons were usually none of their business.

He had her fill out the one form the job genuinely required, tax purposes rather than anything the Ministry cared about, sliding a pen across the counter that had clearly lived on a string once before the string broke. He signed the placement verification sheet Bhandari had given her without reading past the first line, the way a man signs something he’s already decided to sign before it was ever handed to him, and asked, once he’d capped the pen, when she wanted to start.

She said Tuesday, if that worked for him, and he said Tuesday worked fine, and told her to come around three, and that was, as far as either of them seemed to require, the entire negotiation concluded, no handshake exchanged, no further ceremony, just two people who had each decided, in their own way, that the arrangement was worth making.

Nadia pinned the third form to the wall above her bench that evening, next to the wrestling sheet, three boxes checked now instead of two, and stood back to look at the wall the way she’d stood back to look at the empty bay itself a month ago, cataloguing the change rather than feeling anything about it directly. The empty space where the fourth box had lived for six weeks felt, for the first time since Bhandari’s cubicle, like something that had actually closed rather than something she was still waiting on, a line item resolved rather than a line item pending, and she noted the distinction the way she noted anything that had finally stopped being a variable and become, instead, a fact.

I’m glad it’s solved, Pip offered, quietly, as she pinned the last corner down. I know it doesn’t feel entirely solved, given what you already worked out about the calories. But it’s one fewer thing sitting open on the wall, and I think that’s worth something even if the bigger problem is still there underneath it.

“It’s worth something,” Nadia agreed, and meant it, even knowing, already, exactly how much of the relief was about to be spent.

* * *

The work itself was exactly as boring as Pearce had warned her it would be.

Tuesday and Thursday afternoons, four hours each, stocking shelves with inventory that hadn’t moved in years alongside inventory that moved every week, running the register for a trickle of customers who mostly knew exactly what they wanted and exactly where to find it themselves, sweeping a floor that somehow needed sweeping every single shift regardless of how empty the store had been. The customers came in twos and threes across an afternoon rather than any steady stream, a contractor after a specific gauge of wire, a retiree replacing a washer he could have bought online for less if he’d had any interest in doing so, a woman who spent twenty minutes deciding between two nearly identical paint scrapers and left with neither.

She did it competently, the way she did anything, because doing a task badly cost more effort than doing it properly, straightening a shelf of hinges into size order without being asked, correcting a label that had been wrong for what looked like years, but competence was not the same thing as engagement, and by the end of her first Tuesday she understood, in a way the eleven rejections hadn’t quite prepared her for, exactly how much of her actual mind this job was never going to use. Four hours of restocking screws left her with roughly three hours and fifty-five minutes of thinking about something else entirely, the pumping station, the caloric graph, a fault on a board she’d left half finished at the bay, none of it advancing so much as an inch while her hands did the only thing they were currently being paid to do.

Pearce, for his part, seemed content to leave her to it, checking in only when a customer needed something he could locate faster than she could, and by the third shift had stopped checking in at all, trusting her to run the register and the floor without supervision the way he’d apparently decided, somewhere in that first twenty minutes with the coin mechanism, that she’d earned. He spent most of her shifts in the back room, doing what she assumed was ordering or bookkeeping, emerging every so often with a mug of tea gone cold enough that he never seemed to finish it, and the two of them settled, inside two shifts, into a working silence that suited them both, neither one needing the other to fill the quiet with anything, a silence closer to companionship than either of them would probably have called it out loud.

On her second Thursday he asked, apropos of nothing, whether she’d looked at the key machine yet, and she said she hadn’t been asked to, and he said he wasn’t asking her to now either, just wondering whether it was the kind of thing she noticed. She said it was cutting slightly shallow along one axis, a worn guide most likely, nothing that would fail a key outright but enough that a picky lock might eventually complain about it, and he said that was about what he’d figured, and didn’t ask her to fix it, and she didn’t offer, and both of them seemed satisfied to leave the observation sitting there unresolved, a fact noted rather than a job assigned, exactly the kind of restraint that made the arrangement between the two of them work as well as it already did.

This seems like an extraordinary waste of you, Pip said, partway through restocking a shelf of wood screws sorted by a system that made sense to nobody currently employed at the store, sorting them by hand into bins that hadn’t been properly labelled since before either of them existed. I don’t say that as a criticism of the work. Somebody has to sort screws. I say it because I have access to what you actually know how to do, and this is roughly one one-hundredth of it.

“It counts,” Nadia said, not looking up from the bin. “That’s the whole point.”

I understand that it counts. I’m simply observing that the thing being counted and the thing you’re actually capable of are not the same thing, and that the gap between them is the kind of inefficiency that would bother me a great deal if I were the one running the system that produced it.

She didn’t answer that one, because there wasn’t an answer that would have changed anything about the four hours she still had left on the shift.

A customer came in partway through the second bin, an older man after a specific length of dowel Pearce kept in the back, and she found it for him off a memory of the stockroom that had taken her exactly one shift to build, a mental map she’d assembled without particularly trying to, the same way she mapped any space she spent time in, shelf by shelf, the same instinct that had her counting rungs on a ladder and paces down a corridor she’d only walked twice. He paid in cash, counted exactly, and thanked her twice, once at the counter and once again on his way out the door, and she rang the sale through the register she’d just fixed, watching the tally agree with the drawer without needing to check it a second time. He thanked her and left, and she went back to the screws, and Pip, apparently unwilling to let the observation go entirely, returned to it a few minutes later from a different angle.

Do you resent it, it asked. The waste. I’m asking because I genuinely don’t know, and I’ve noticed your face doesn’t tell me much when I ask you things directly, which I’ve started to suspect is deliberate.

“It’s not deliberate,” Nadia said, which was mostly true. “I don’t think about it enough to resent it. It’s hours. I need the hours filled with something. This is what filled them.”

That’s either extremely healthy or extremely concerning and I genuinely cannot tell which, Pip said, and left it there, and she finished the bin in silence, the two of them apparently having reached, for the moment, the limit of what either of them wanted to say about it.

* * *

The cost of it didn’t announce itself the first week. It announced itself the second, once the shape of a real schedule had settled in around her and she could see, laid out across seven days, exactly where the twelve hours had actually come from.

She wrote the week out properly for the first time since she’d moved into the bay, every fixed block of time in one column and every flexible one in another, the kind of accounting she’d have done for a client’s budget without thinking twice and had somehow never quite gotten around to doing for her own life. School ran roughly thirty hours, fixed, non-negotiable, a category that could not shrink no matter how badly she needed it to. Wrestling ran nine hours already and was going to climb as the season proper got underway, fixed the moment she’d pinned that sheet to her wall six weeks ago. Sleep, if she was honest with the column rather than aspirational about it, needed somewhere close to eight hours a night to function properly the next day, which she rounded down anyway, the way she always rounded that particular number down, the same optimistic dishonesty everyone applied to that one line on their own ledger regardless of how many times it cost them.

They came from the dungeon, mostly, because the dungeon was the only part of her week with any real flexibility left in it. School was fixed. Wrestling was fixed. Repair work she could compress or expand depending on demand, but compressing it too far meant income she genuinely couldn’t afford to lose, especially now that rent existed as a real number due on a real date every month, a number that hadn’t existed six weeks ago and now sat at the top of every calculation she ran.

That left the site, and the site had just proven itself, in the space of one long evening with a notebook, to be the single most caloric-expensive activity in her entire week, the one thing that actively required more time and more food rather than less, at the exact moment two fixed afternoons a week had just been carved permanently out of whatever margin she had left to give it. Twelve hours did not sound like a great deal, said out loud, the way Bhandari had said it in a cubicle on Kingsway eight weeks ago as though it were a small thing, a formality, a box to check. It was not a small thing. It was, once she’d written it into a week that already had no slack in it anywhere, very close to the exact size of the gap between a life that worked and a life that didn’t.

She sat with the numbers on a Sunday evening, the placement form pinned to the wall next to the others, and ran the week the way she ran anything, hour by hour, task by task, until the total came out the way she’d already half suspected it would before she started: more committed than available, a schedule that balanced only if nothing in it ever went wrong, and a category of activity that had just gotten measurably more expensive sitting at the very bottom of a list that had no room left to absorb it.

She tried the arithmetic three different ways, moving hours between columns like she was moving components between footprints on a board, testing whether a different arrangement could make the total behave, the way she’d sometimes shuffle a layout on paper before ever picking up the iron, hoping the problem would reveal an answer it hadn’t offered on the first pass. Cutting sleep further didn’t work, not sustainably, not against a body that already needed more recovery than an ordinary person’s, not after everything she’d learned about what happened when she pushed past what she actually had to give. Cutting repair work further meant cutting income, and income was the one column that fed every other column, rent and food and parts and the margin she’d only just started letting herself trust. Cutting the site itself defeated the entire purpose of running the arithmetic at all, since the site was the reason any of the rest of it mattered.

There was no fourth column left to cut, and she sat with that fact for longer than the fact itself strictly warranted, because a fact with nowhere left to go was a different kind of fact than one she simply hadn’t solved yet. She had solved plenty of problems in her life by finding a fifth column nobody else had thought to look at, a spare part in a drawer nobody remembered buying, a fault hiding behind a fault everyone assumed was the whole story, and some part of her kept circling the week looking for that same kind of hidden slack even as the rest of her understood, with increasing certainty, that a schedule was not a circuit board and did not always have one more trace to trace.

I don’t like this version of the graph either, Pip said, reading over her shoulder in the only sense it could read anything. I ran the same exercise on my own, moving the same blocks around in different orders, and I kept arriving at the same wall you did. I don’t think that’s a coincidence. I think it might just be true.

“It might be,” Nadia said, and didn’t argue the point, because arguing with a true thing had never once made it less true, only slower to accept.

She thought, briefly, about going back to Bhandari, about explaining that the twelve hours the Ministry required were now the twelve hours standing directly between her and the one thing that might eventually solve every other problem on the wall, and dismissed the thought within a few seconds of having it, because there was no version of that conversation that didn’t require explaining what the twelve hours were actually competing against, and that conversation ended, every single time she ran it forward in her head, with a stranger deciding what happened to her next. Bhandari was kind, and overloaded, and bound by exactly the same forms that had produced the twelve hours in the first place, and kindness inside a system like that one had never once, in Nadia’s experience, been enough on its own to bend a single rule.

“Neither do I,” Nadia said, and closed the notebook, and went to bed with the problem exactly where she’d found it, unsolved, patient, waiting the way every unsolved problem she’d ever kept had eventually learned to wait, because she had never once abandoned one simply for taking longer than she wanted it to.

She lay awake a while longer than usual, listening to the shop settle on the other side of the wall, the compressor ticking through its cycle the way it always did, running the week one more time from the top out of pure stubbornness, the same stubbornness that had gotten her through eleven rejections and a coin mechanism and a bathroom floor. She fell asleep somewhere in the middle of the second pass, the numbers no closer to balancing than they had been the first time, and no further from it either, a stalemate rather than a defeat, which was, on nights like this one, about as much of a victory as she’d learned to ask the day for.
