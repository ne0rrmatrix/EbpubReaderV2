Chapter 19: The Review

The appointment slip had said ten fifteen, and Nadia arrived at ten, because arriving early had always cost her less than arriving anywhere close to late, and sat in the row of plastic chairs outside Bhandari’s cubicle with her folder on her lap, the one she’d built herself months ago out of a dollar-store binder and three colour-coded dividers, because a system that lived only in Bhandari’s stack of forty overlapping files was not a system she trusted to survive contact with a deadline.

School attendance, verified weekly by the district, a printout she’d picked up from the office herself rather than wait for it to arrive through whatever channel was supposed to forward it automatically and regularly didn’t. Supervised activity, Val’s signature at the bottom of a wrestling roster, dated and countersigned, the ink slightly smudged where Val had signed six names in a row without lifting the pen properly between them. Verified placement, Pearce’s block handwriting on a form headed Employer Verification of Hours, twelve dollars an hour times however many Tuesdays and Thursdays the quarter had actually contained, arithmetic she’d already checked twice against her own log before checking it a third time out of habit rather than doubt.

Three tabs. Three green checkmarks she’d drawn in herself, in pen, on the inside cover, because the folder was hers and the checkmarks were for her, not for Bhandari, who would never once look at the cover and would go instead directly to whatever printout the system itself had already generated from three separate databases that, as far as Nadia had ever been able to tell, barely spoke to each other.

You’ve read through that folder four times since you sat down, Pip observed, arriving late and thin in the flat fluorescent light of the waiting area, its attention the usual half-distance away it kept above ground in daylight. I don’t think there’s anything new to find on a fifth pass.

“I know,” Nadia said, low, not looking up from the page in front of her. “I’m not looking for anything new. I’m checking it’s still what it was the last time I looked at it.”

That’s the same thing you do with the scooter’s frame, Pip said. Checking a thing that already passed inspection, in case the inspection itself was somehow wrong the whole time.

“The inspection’s wrong more often than people think,” Nadia said, and closed the folder anyway, because Bhandari’s door had opened, and a woman she didn’t recognise was leaving it with the particular unfocused expression of someone who’d just been told something that would take a few minutes to properly land.

She watched the woman cross the waiting room without really seeing anything in front of her, a stack of papers held loosely in one hand, and thought, cataloguing the walk the way she catalogued anything, that whatever had happened behind that door hadn’t gone the way this morning had been supposed to go. It wasn’t her business, and she didn’t make it her business, only filed the observation the way she filed most things that happened around her without being aimed at her, a data point about the building rather than about herself.

The chair beside her had been empty when she sat down and stayed empty the whole wait, other names called from other rows, other files moving in and out of other cubicles along the same corridor, the whole floor running on the same overloaded rhythm it had run on the last time she’d sat in this exact chair, months ago, waiting for a different version of this same appointment. Nothing about the room had changed. The carpet was the same grey-blue it had been in the spring, worn to a paler patch in front of each cubicle door where a season’s worth of nervous feet had stood exactly where she was about to stand.

* * *

Bhandari’s cubicle looked exactly as it had the last time, three felt walls instead of four, the same curling school photo of two kids in matching sweaters pinned at one corner, a stack of files that had grown, if anything, slightly taller since the spring, distinguishable from each other by shape and thickness the way Nadia distinguished a board’s fault by the pattern of its damage rather than by any label anyone had bothered to attach.

“You’re early,” Bhandari said, not looking up from the screen, one hand already reaching for the mouse to pull the right file, the gesture of someone who could locate a person’s entire compliance history without needing to ask their name first. “Most people your age aren’t early for anything, in my experience.”

“I didn’t want to be late at all,” Nadia said, which was true and also not the whole reason, and sat in the chair across the desk that had, she noticed, been reorganised at some point since the spring, the stack of files rearranged into a slightly different configuration that told her nothing except that time had passed here the same way it passed everywhere else.

Bhandari pulled the file up properly, scrolled through what Nadia assumed was a summary screen, and made a small sound that might have been approval, the specific economical sound of someone who reviewed hundreds of these a season and had learned to compress her reactions down to the smallest gesture that would still register as a reaction at all.

“All three categories logged,” Bhandari said. “Every week accounted for, since the last review. That’s not nothing. I see a lot of files where one category slips the moment the pressure’s off, and yours didn’t slip at all.”

“I kept a copy myself,” Nadia said, and set the folder on the desk without opening it, an offer rather than an insistence, and Bhandari glanced at it, seemed to weigh whether to actually open it, and set it aside instead, unopened, trusting the system’s own printout over whatever Nadia had built to double-check it.

“I believe you,” Bhandari said. “The system agrees with you, which helps, but I’d have believed you either way.”

Nadia filed that sentence the way she filed most things Bhandari said, carefully, without immediately knowing what to do with it, because a caseworker who believed her without needing the paperwork to confirm it was, in Nadia’s experience across three previous reviews and two previous caseworkers before Bhandari, close to unprecedented.

The first of those caseworkers, a man named Delgatty whose name she still remembered mainly for how rarely he’d looked up from his own screen, had run every review as a pure paper exercise, box checked or box unchecked, no interest whatsoever in whether the human being sitting across from him bore any resemblance to the file describing her. The second, a woman covering a leave for six months during Nadia’s time at a group placement two towns over, had been kind in a performative way that cost Nadia more to sit through than Delgatty’s indifference ever had, a kindness aimed more at the caseworker’s own sense of herself than at anything Nadia actually needed from the room. Bhandari fit neither pattern, which was, Nadia had decided somewhere around the second review under her file, both the best thing about the assignment and the hardest one to know what to do with.

“You transferred here from group housing before Kelso,” Bhandari said, not quite a question, scrolling through something further up the file. “I don’t think I’ve actually asked you about that placement directly. Was it survivable?”

“It was fine,” Nadia said, the word doing the same work it always did, flattening six months of a shared room and a set of house rules written for a much younger cohort into something small enough to hand across a desk without either of them having to spend real time on it.

“Fine,” Bhandari repeated, in a tone that suggested she’d heard the word enough times from enough files to recognise exactly how much weight it was and wasn’t carrying, and let it go without pushing, the same restraint she’d shown with the trainer’s note a few minutes later, a caseworker who seemed to understand, better than most adults Nadia had dealt with, the difference between a door worth knocking on and one better left alone for now.

* * *

The interview ran through its structure the way it had run the last time, a series of questions Bhandari worked through from a form on her screen, most of them procedural, a few of them landing somewhere closer to a real question than the form probably intended. Housing situation stable. Yes. Any change in guardianship status. No. Any new income sources requiring declaration. No, which was true in the specific narrow sense the form was built to catch and not remotely true in any sense that would have satisfied a more searching question, a distinction Nadia had gotten comfortable enough with by now that answering it no longer cost her anything visible.

Bhandari asked about the trainer’s clearance next, glancing at a note in the file that Nadia couldn’t see from her side of the desk, and said his last submitted bloodwork summary had flagged as borderline again, the same flag it had carried since the first review, a note that apparently followed her file around indefinitely without ever quite escalating into anything.

“He signs off on it every season,” Nadia said. “He just also notes it every season. I think that’s just how he does the form.”

“It is how a lot of trainers do the form,” Bhandari agreed, not pressing it further, making a note that looked, from what Nadia could see of the angle, like nothing more than a checkbox rather than a flag of her own. “I’m required to log that it’s logged. I’m not required to do anything about a borderline note that a qualified professional has already signed off past. I just wanted you to know it’s there, in case it ever comes up somewhere else.”

Nadia thought, sitting very still in the chair across the desk, about exactly what somewhere else might mean, and about how close that sentence had come, without either of them naming it, to the shape of the thing she actually needed to keep hidden. Bhandari didn’t elaborate, and moved on to the next line on the form, and Nadia let the moment pass without either of them acknowledging how narrowly it had passed.

That was close, Pip said, quiet, present enough in the small windowless cubicle to notice the room properly. I don’t think she meant it as close as it felt.

“She didn’t,” Nadia said, under her breath, while Bhandari typed. “That’s what makes it worse. She wasn’t even trying.”

* * *

The rest of the interview ran quickly after that, Bhandari working through the remaining boxes with the practiced economy of someone managing a caseload four times what any single desk was built to hold, checking, confirming, moving to the next line without wasting a motion anywhere in the process. It should have felt, Nadia thought, exactly like the fight she’d walked into six weeks ago on the catwalk, a known structure, a known set of questions, a clock she could see the shape of even without a number attached to it.

It didn’t feel like that at all.

Every question carried a second question underneath it that the form couldn’t see and Bhandari almost certainly could, whether the version of her life reflected on the screen was the true one, whether the categories she’d logged so carefully actually added up to a life anyone would call stable if they looked at it directly instead of through three colour-coded printouts, and there was no clock on that second question, no closing bell, no number she could check against a meter and know, definitively, whether she’d survived it or not.

“That’s everything on my end,” Bhandari said, finally, closing the file with the small definitive motion of someone who had run this exact closing gesture a great many times this season alone. “You pass. Categories fully logged, no gaps, no flags requiring action. I’ll note the file as clear through the next quarter.”

“Okay,” Nadia said quietly, and felt the sentence land somewhere that didn’t match the relief it should have produced, a pass that read, from the inside, considerably closer to a held breath than a victory.

Bhandari didn’t close the laptop right away. She sat back a little, the specific loosening of posture that signalled, in Nadia’s limited experience of this particular caseworker, that the official portion of the meeting had ended and something less scripted was about to follow it, a pattern she’d clocked at the last review too and hadn’t known, until this exact moment, that she’d been quietly dreading its return.

“Can I ask you something that isn’t on the form,” Bhandari said.

“Sure,” Nadia said, quietly, because sure had always been easier than the alternative.

“How are you actually doing,” Bhandari said. “Not the categories. You.”

* * *

Nadia had an answer ready for almost every version of that question the world had ever asked her, a small library of responses calibrated over years to cost her the least and reveal the least and satisfy whoever was asking without requiring either of them to sit any longer with whatever the honest answer would have actually involved. Fine. Busy. Managing. A rotation of three words that had gotten her through every teacher, every coach, every well-meaning adult who’d ever asked the question in passing on the way to something else.

Bhandari wasn’t asking on the way to something else. Bhandari had closed the laptop, folded her hands on top of it, and was simply waiting, with the specific patient attention of someone who had asked the question because she genuinely wanted the answer and had, unusually, budgeted the time to actually receive it, which was, Nadia understood distantly, exactly the thing that made the question so much harder to survive than anything the form had asked her.

There was no clock on it. There was no number. There was no way to know, in advance, what the correct weight of honesty was, how much would satisfy the question without opening a door that led somewhere she couldn’t afford Bhandari to follow her through, and she felt, sitting in the small windowless cubicle with a kind, overworked woman waiting patiently across the desk for an answer that cost the woman nothing to ask and could cost Nadia everything to give honestly, something considerably closer to the specific cold clarity of a bad hit landing than to anything the word conversation usually produced in her.

“I’m managing,” Nadia said, finally, the safest word in the rotation, and heard, saying it, how thin it sounded in a room this quiet, against a question that patient.

Bhandari looked at her for a moment, not pushing, not calling the answer out for the deflection it plainly was, and said, gently, that managing was a word she heard from a lot of files that were doing considerably worse than the paperwork suggested, and that she wasn’t going to make Nadia say anything she didn’t want to say, but that the door in this particular cubicle stayed open for exactly this kind of conversation whenever Nadia wanted to use it, not only on the quarterly schedule, any time, categories or no categories.

“Thank you,” Nadia said quietly, and meant it, and found the meaning of it sitting somewhere uncomfortably close to the surface, a warmth she had no ready defence against, offered without a single string attached to it, costing Bhandari, as far as Nadia could tell, absolutely nothing beyond the sentence itself.

You’re doing the thing you did in the hallway with Priya, Pip said, quiet, careful. Going very still. I don’t think Bhandari’s noticed. I noticed.

Nadia didn’t answer, because answering would have meant explaining, and explaining would have meant sitting even longer inside a feeling she had no category for, something that landed nowhere near fear and nowhere near relief either, an unbounded softness with no edge to find and hold onto the way she found and held the edge of everything else.

* * *

Bhandari opened the laptop again before Nadia had fully recovered her footing, the official portion of the meeting resuming with the same practiced economy it had run on before, and said there was one more thing, not a flag, just a heads up worth having three months ahead of needing it.

“The Ministry’s revising the verified-placement standard next quarter,” Bhandari said. “Region-wide, not just your file. They’re tightening what counts as an employer of record, more documentation, quarterly banking reconciliation instead of the simple hours form Pearce has been filling out for you. It’s meant to catch fraud, apparently, though most of the fraud they catch this way is smaller than what it costs to catch it.”

“What does that mean for me,” Nadia asked, the folder still sitting closed on the desk between them, the three green checkmarks inside it suddenly considerably less permanent than they’d felt twenty minutes ago.

“It means the next review might ask Pearce for things he’s never had to provide before,” Bhandari said. “Payroll records, a business number if he doesn’t already have one on file, proof the hours track to an actual bank deposit rather than just a signature on a page. Most small operators like him aren’t set up for that kind of paperwork. It’s not a problem yet. I just don’t want you walking into January the way some of my other files are going to walk into it, blindsided by a category that used to be the easy one.”

Nadia thought about Pearce’s ledger, kept the same way his register had been kept before she’d fixed it, mostly by memory and a stack of receipts in a shoebox, a system that had survived thirty years of small-town bookkeeping and had never once needed to survive a quarterly banking reconciliation. She thought about the twelve dollars an hour paid in cash more often than not, about a business that ran, by design and by necessity, on exactly the kind of informal trust the new standard seemed built to eliminate.

“Does everyone’s placement run into this,” she asked, “or just the small ones.”

“Mostly the small ones,” Bhandari admitted. “A big employer already has payroll software spitting out exactly what the Ministry’s going to want. It’s the shops like Pearce’s that are going to feel it, the ones that have always run on a handshake and a notebook. I don’t love that part of it either, for what it’s worth. I didn’t write the policy. I just have to apply it once it lands.”

“That seems like it’s going to cost a lot of kids their placements,” Nadia said, “over paperwork that has nothing to do with whether the work actually happened.”

“It will,” Bhandari said, plainly, no attempt to soften it into something more palatable than it was. “I’ve already seen the memo. I expect to spend a good chunk of next quarter helping files scramble for a second placement because their first one couldn’t produce a bank statement fast enough. I’m telling you now specifically because you’re one of the files I don’t want scrambling in January if I can help it.”

“Three months,” she said, mostly to herself, filing the number the way she filed any deadline, a fixed point on a calendar she could plan backward from.

“Three months,” Bhandari agreed, unhurried. “I know it’s not what you wanted to hear on a day you actually passed clean. I try to tell people the bad news early rather than let it arrive as a surprise at the worst possible moment. It rarely feels like a kindness in the room. It usually is one, eventually.”

* * *

Bhandari closed the file properly then, the small definitive motion she’d used to close the whole interview a few minutes earlier, and said that was everything, genuinely everything this time, and that Nadia should call the office directly if Pearce needed help understanding what the new standard was actually going to ask of him, rather than trying to puzzle it out alone from whatever notice eventually landed in his mailbox.

“He’s not going to call anyone,” Nadia said. “He barely uses the till’s calculator function. He’s not going to phone a government office about a paperwork policy he hasn’t even heard of yet.”

“Then you call,” Bhandari said. “On his behalf, if you have to. I’d rather field one more phone call from a sixteen-year-old than lose a placement that’s clearly been working for both of you.”

Nadia said she would, and meant it in the moment, already filing the offer away next to everything else the morning had handed her, and stood to leave, the folder gathered back up off the desk and held against her chest again the way she’d carried it in, three green checkmarks inside it still true for exactly one more quarter.

The walk out through the waiting area passed in the same fluorescent hush it had passed in on the way in, other files waiting in the same row of plastic chairs, other versions of the same overloaded morning playing out across a floor of identical cubicles, and Nadia carried the folder against her chest rather than in her bag, an old habit from years of exactly this kind of appointment, keeping the one document she trusted closest to the part of her body she trusted the most.

You passed, Pip said, once they were out on the street, its presence thinning the way it always thinned once they’d put enough distance between themselves and anything underground.

“I passed,” Nadia agreed, low, the cold air doing some of its usual work, though not, this time, nearly enough of it.

That should feel like more than it looks like it feels, Pip said. I want to point that out, because I don’t think you’re going to point it out to yourself.

“It does feel like something,” Nadia said. “It just doesn’t feel like the thing it’s supposed to feel like. The categories passed. I don’t know if I did.”

What does passing look like, to you, if the categories aren’t it, Pip asked, careful, the question genuinely open rather than rhetorical.

Nadia walked most of a block before she answered, turning the question over the way she turned over anything that refused to resolve cleanly on the first pass. “I don’t know,” she said, finally. “I think that’s the actual problem. There isn’t a version of this where I get to check a box that says the whole thing, not just the categories, actually worked. There’s no clock on it. There’s no number. Bhandari asked me how I was doing and I didn’t have anything true to give her that wouldn’t have cost me more than the truth was worth.”

She wasn’t grading you, Pip said, gently. I don’t think she was looking for a right answer.

“I know,” Nadia said, quiet. “That’s what made it worse. If she’d been grading me I’d have known exactly what to say.”

You know what to say to a fight because a fight has a wrong answer, Pip said. Miss the angle, take the flat contact, watch the number climb. You can see exactly what failure costs. I don’t think Bhandari’s question had a wrong answer in it at all, and I think that’s specifically what made it unbearable rather than in spite of it.

“Everything has a wrong answer,” Nadia said, quiet, more to herself than to Pip. “I just don’t always get told what it is until afterward.”

That’s not the same as everything having one you can see in advance, Pip said. I think you already know that, even if you don’t want to sit with it right now.

She didn’t answer that, and let the silence hold for most of a block, the two of them moving through it the way they moved through most silences lately, Pip present enough to notice and patient enough not to fill the gap just because it was there to be filled.

* * *

She thought about the three months the rest of the way home, the number settling into the same category as every other deadline she’d ever carried, a fixed point to plan backward from, except this one had no clean plan attached to it, no repair she could make with a soldering iron and a spare part scavenged from a dead washing machine controller. Pearce’s bookkeeping wasn’t a fault she could trace to a swollen capacitor. It was a system built on thirty years of a small operator’s trust in cash and memory, about to collide with a standard written by people who had never once had to run a register with nothing behind it but a shoebox of receipts and a ledger in a hand that shook slightly these days when he added up a long column.

She would have to find something. She always found something, eventually, the way she found something for every other fault the world handed her, but she walked the last few blocks home turning the shape of the problem over without yet seeing where the solution in it might be hiding, a category that used to be the easy one, about to become, in three months, considerably less easy than anything she had budget left over to spend on it.

She ran the options the way she ran any repair with more than one possible fix, listing each of them out and testing it against the constraints before letting herself get attached to any single approach. She could ask Pearce to formalise the bookkeeping properly, a business number, a real accounting system, except that would cost him money he clearly didn’t have to spare on a shop that survived, as far as she could tell, on thin margins and thirty years of goodwill rather than on any cushion built for an unexpected expense. She could find a second placement somewhere already set up to handle the new documentation, a chain store, a franchise with payroll software built in, except that would mean leaving a shop she’d fixed the register in, leaving Pearce short a set of hands he’d come to rely on, and losing the one part of her week that asked nothing more complicated of her than counting stock and making correct change. She could try to explain the situation to Bhandari directly, ask for some kind of exception, except exceptions were exactly the kind of thing a system running four times over capacity had no spare attention left to grant, and asking for one risked drawing exactly the closer look she could least afford.

None of the options survived contact with the actual constraints for more than a few seconds of consideration, and she filed each rejected approach the way she filed a repair path that hadn’t panned out, not a failure exactly, just a branch that had needed testing and had come back negative, one fewer direction left to try before whatever direction actually worked finally presented itself.

You’re already three moves into a problem that technically doesn’t exist yet, Pip observed, quiet, watching her work through it with the specific patient interest it reserved for anything methodical. The standard doesn’t change until next quarter. You have three months.

“Three months isn’t as long as it sounds,” Nadia said. “Not when the fix has to work around a person who’s already stretched as thin as Pearce is, and a system that isn’t going to bend for either of us just because we ask nicely.”

I know, Pip said. I just want it noted, for the record, that you spent the walk home from a review you passed cleanly solving a problem that isn’t due for ninety more days. I don’t think that’s nothing either. I think it might be most of what managing actually looks like, from where I sit.

“Managing isn’t the same as doing well,” Nadia said. “Bhandari asked me the second one. I only know how to answer the first one, and I don’t think I know how to fix that gap the way I’d fix anything else.”

Maybe that’s worth writing down somewhere too, Pip said. Not tonight. Just eventually, once you’ve got a spare page and somewhere quiet enough to actually look at it properly.

Nadia didn’t have an answer for that one that felt honest enough to say out loud, so she let it stand, unanswered, the way she let most of Pip’s better observations stand lately, filed rather than argued with.

Three months, Pip said again, quiet, as they turned onto the last stretch before the bay. I know you’re already planning backward from it. I can hear you doing it.

“I have to,” Nadia said. “It’s the only thing I actually know how to do with a number like that once it’s in front of me.”

I know, Pip said. I just wanted you to notice you were doing it again, the same day you were supposed to be allowed to just have passed something, for once, without immediately turning it into the next problem.

Nadia didn’t answer that, because there wasn’t an honest answer that didn’t concede the point entirely, and let herself into the bay with the folder still held against her chest, three green checkmarks inside it, true for exactly one more quarter, and set it down on the bench beside the notebook that still, most nights, read no result, next to a ledger she hadn’t started keeping yet for a crime she hadn’t committed yet, though some distant, methodical part of her, already running the arithmetic of Pearce’s shoebox against a standard she couldn’t meet honestly, had already begun, quietly, to understand which direction the next three months were going to push her.
