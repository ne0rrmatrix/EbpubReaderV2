Chapter 22: Scrap

The bruise on her left forearm hadn’t finished surfacing from a session four days ago, still darkening at the edges instead of fading the way it should have by now, and she stood at the bench turning her arm under the bay’s one good light, cataloguing the discolouration the way she catalogued any symptom that refused to behave on schedule.

Four days. A hit that clean should have cleared inside two, even on an ordinary week. This wasn’t an ordinary week. The tournament was eleven days out, her weight already trimmed close enough to the number that every gram left to shed came from somewhere her body would rather keep, and the deficit she was running to get there had done to her regeneration exactly what the arithmetic said it would do, slowed it, dragged it, left a bruise sitting stubborn and dark on her arm four days after a hit that should have been ancient history by now.

She’d run the numbers twice already tonight, the way she ran any number she was about to build a decision on top of. Clearance down, by her own rough estimate, somewhere close to seventy percent of what it ran at full recovery. Not zero. Not nothing. Just slow, the queue from her last session still sitting somewhere behind the bruise, unfinished business her body hadn’t gotten around to closing out yet.

You’re thinking about going down tonight anyway, Pip said, quiet, watching her turn her arm under the light.

“I need the session tonight,” Nadia said. “Variable three on the reproduction test. It has to run tonight or the spacing on the whole nine-day window goes uneven, and uneven spacing means I can’t trust whatever the results say afterward.”

Your last queue hasn’t cleared, Pip said. You’re looking at your own arm right now because it hasn’t cleared.

“I know,” Nadia said. “I ran the math anyway. A partial queue isn’t a full queue. I’ve got headroom. Not much, but I checked it twice.”

Twice isn’t the same as enough, Pip said, careful. I want to say that plainly, because I don’t think you’d let anyone else get away with running a test this close to the edge without saying it back to them first.

“If Val handed me a set of numbers this close to a limit,” Nadia said, “I’d tell her to run it anyway, as long as the margin was real and not imaginary. I checked the margin. It’s real.”

Val doesn’t run experiments on a body that’s already fighting a caloric deficit and a partially drained queue at the same time, Pip said. I don’t think the comparison holds up as well as you want it to.

“Nothing about this year has held up the way it was supposed to,” Nadia said, not unkindly, more tired than sharp. “I’m used to running the numbers on a situation that already stopped being ideal a long time ago. This is just one more version of that.”

I understand the reasoning, Pip said. I still don’t like it, and I don’t think I’m going to start liking it. I want that on the record too, the same as I put the ledger on the record.

“Noted,” Nadia said quietly, and zipped the bag firmly closed.

Nadia lowered her arm and looked at the meter sitting on the bench, the readout she’d already checked against her own estimate of the remaining queue, the numbers that had said, on two separate calculations, that tonight was survivable. Tight. Not comfortable. Survivable.

“I checked it,” she said again, quieter this time, and started packing her bag.

* * *

The ride out felt different in a way she noticed and filed without fully naming, the cold biting harder than it should have against a body that usually ran hot enough to shrug off a Vancouver November without much comment. She caught herself flexing the bruised forearm at every stop light, testing the range of motion the way she’d test a repaired joint before trusting it back into service, and told herself the stiffness was just the cold and not the queue talking.

She descended slower than usual, letting the entry point’s cold settle into her properly before she started the walk toward the alcove, and felt, somewhere in that first hundred metres, the specific low hum of a body that hadn’t fully recovered from the last time it had been asked to do this. Not pain exactly. A kind of readiness that wasn’t all the way there, the sensation of a machine running at eighty percent and being asked to perform like it was at a hundred.

She checked the meter twice more on the walk in, the way she checked anything she was already committed to but hadn’t stopped auditing, and both readings agreed with the estimate she’d built at the bench. Tight. Survivable. The word sat differently down here than it had sitting at the bay under the good light, less like a conclusion and more like a hope she was in the process of testing against a building that had no interest whatsoever in whether her arithmetic held.

The tunnel smelled the way it always smelled this deep, wet stone and old iron and something faintly chemical underneath both, a smell she’d stopped consciously registering months ago and only ever noticed again on nights like this one, when every sense seemed to be reporting for duty a little more urgently than usual. She passed the junction where the corridor split toward the west gallery, a route she’d mapped thoroughly weeks ago and hadn’t walked since, and gave it no more than a glance on her way past, her attention entirely on the alcove and the test waiting inside it.

Last chance to turn around, Pip said, as they neared the junction where the corridor split toward the alcove and, the other direction, back toward the surface entry.

“I’m going,” Nadia said, quiet, and kept on walking.

* * *

The variable tonight was posture, the guide’s alternate configuration she hadn’t yet tested, weight forward rather than back, and she settled into it against the alcove’s cold wall with the same careful attention she’d given every previous session, meter propped on its levelled stone, notebook open to a fresh page.

She was six cycles into the breath count when the Elite found her.

It shouldn’t have. The alcove sat two hundred metres off the patrol line, mapped and confirmed safe across three prior sessions, and she’d never once had it deviate from the circuit in all the weeks she’d used this spot. Tonight the pattern had shifted, whether by the slow procedural drift the machine accumulated across every encounter it had ever run or by some cause she’d never get the chance to trace, and the first she knew of it was the cold arriving ahead of anything else, dropping fast and dropping hard, the specific wrongness her body had learned months ago to treat as a starting gun.

She was up and moving before her mind had finished processing the temperature change, six years of wrestling instinct converting alarm into action faster than conscious thought could keep pace with, meter and notebook abandoned on the stone behind her.

The first contact came before she’d found real footing, a glancing blow along her already-bruised forearm that lit the old damage up fresh and hot, and she felt the Window open behind it, the queue starting its climb from a baseline that wasn’t zero, that had never been zero tonight, stacking new debt on top of old the way she’d known it would and had told herself, twice, was survivable.

Forty, Pip said, flat, reading the meter she could no longer see. That was a clean absorb. You’re fine.

She didn’t answer that at all. Answering cost time she genuinely didn’t have to spare.

She backed toward the maintenance passage without turning her back on the machine, feet finding the corridor’s narrower mouth by memory rather than sight, her attention locked entirely on the specific slow deliberateness of the Elite’s advance, no urgency in it, no anger, the same attentive incuriosity it brought to every encounter, total focus and no interest whatsoever in what she was beyond an obstacle currently occupying ground it wanted.

She’d read this opening a dozen times before and knew, even now, even with her pulse hammering and the cold air burning in her chest, that the first several seconds of any encounter were the ones that mattered most, the window where a wrong read cost the least and set the shape of everything that followed. She watched its shoulder, not its hands, the tell Val had drilled into her a hundred times on the mat translated wholesale into a language the machine had never once been designed to speak and somehow spoke anyway, mass and leverage obeying the same physics whether the thing throwing the weight was flesh or forty-year-old cast iron.

* * *

The corridor narrowed here, a maintenance passage barely wide enough for two people to pass, and she used it the way she’d use any tight space against a bigger opponent, forcing the exchange into a line where only one angle of attack existed at a time instead of the open field the Elite usually got to choose from.

Six years of wrestling had taught her that geometry was a weapon before technique ever was, that a smaller body in the right space could neutralise a larger one simply by refusing to fight on ground that favoured size, and she’d mapped this exact stretch of corridor weeks ago with precisely this contingency in mind, filed away and never used until tonight handed her a reason to reach for it.

Second contact. She read it early, redirected along the angle Val had spent a season correcting, and felt it land clean, absorbed rather than eaten, the specific satisfaction of technique working exactly as trained even with her pulse hammering and her breath already ragged.

Forty again, Pip said. Queue’s climbing but it’s climbing the way it should.

Third contact came faster than she’d planned for, the Elite’s attrition pattern accelerating in a way she hadn’t seen before, and she took it wrong, a half-second late on the redirect, weight in the wrong place.

The mistake was small. A hip turned a fraction late. Weight caught square instead of glanced. In wrestling it would have cost her two points and a reset to neutral position. Down here it cost considerably more, the full force of the blow driving straight through rather than around, absorbed by nothing but bone and the specific unforgiving arithmetic of a body that had run out of clever angles to hide behind.

Three hundred.

She staggered, catching herself against the corridor wall with a hand that came away scraped, the pain of it barely registering against everything else her body was currently trying to process at once. Her vision narrowed for a half-second, the specific tunnel-vision her wrestling coaches had warned her about years ago, the body’s own emergency protocol kicking in and stripping away everything except the single most urgent problem in front of her.

The Elite pressed forward, unhurried, unbothered, exactly as attentive and exactly as incurious as it had been ninety seconds ago, before any of this had started, entirely unaware that the margin between one outcome and the other had just narrowed to something she could count on one hand.

That’s bad, Pip said, something in its voice she’d never once heard from it, not calm. That’s genuinely bad. You need to check the number right now.

She didn’t need to check it. She could feel it, a wrongness in her own chest that had nothing to do with breath and everything to do with a queue sitting somewhere close enough to the cap that her body seemed to know it before her mind had finished the arithmetic.

* * *

Run.

Not a decision. A conclusion that arrived faster than thought, the same six years of mat instinct that had gotten her moving in the first place now telling her, with total clarity, that the only correct move left on the board was distance.

She broke for the corridor’s far end, the Elite’s bulk too slow through the narrow passage to match her stride for stride, and felt the queue behind her doing arithmetic she didn’t have spare attention to run herself, every second of continued proximity a second of continued risk even without another clean hit landing.

Cap, Pip said, one word, the flattest, most urgent single syllable it had ever given her.

She didn’t ask how close. Close enough that Pip had said it at all was close enough.

Her legs burned in a way that had nothing to do with technique and everything to do with a body running on a cut, on four hours of sleep, on a queue already stacked higher than it should have been before tonight had even started, and she pushed through it the way she pushed through the last round of any match, past the point where the body wanted to negotiate, because negotiating wasn’t available right now, not with this margin, not with a corridor still to clear before any of it would matter.

The corridor bent left. She took it without slowing, boots skidding on wet concrete, one hand out to catch the wall and keep her feet under her.

Line of sight broke behind her.

She didn’t stop. Distance and line of sight both, the only reset condition that mattered, and she kept running another thirty metres past the bend before her legs finally gave her the verdict her mind had been waiting for, buckling under her all at once, dropping her hard onto the wet concrete floor.

* * *

She lay there for a long moment, chest heaving, the cold floor soaking through her jacket, and did the only thing that made sense to do with a body that had just been asked for everything it had.

She counted her own pulse first, the way she’d been taught years ago on a wrestling mat, two fingers pressed against her throat, and found it high but steady, coming down already from wherever it had spiked during the run, the specific reassurance of a body that had been pushed hard and had not, in the end, actually been broken by it. Her scraped hand throbbed dully where it had caught the wall, and she flexed it experimentally, cataloguing the damage the way she catalogued anything, superficial, nothing structural, a problem that would resolve itself given time she wasn’t certain her body currently had spare to give it.

The cold of the concrete had gone from uncomfortable to genuinely dangerous in the minutes she’d spent lying there, her core temperature dropping in a way that would, under different circumstances, have worried her considerably more than it currently registered, her whole nervous system apparently still triaging threats in the order they’d actually kill her rather than the order a calmer mind might have chosen.

She checked the meter.

Her hands shook too badly to read it cleanly the first time. She waited, breathing through the shake the way she’d breathe through anything, and checked it again.

The queue sat six points under the cap. Six, out of a hard limit that ran into the hundreds. A margin so thin it barely qualified as one at all, the kind of number that in any other context she’d have called a rounding error, and tonight represented the entire distance between her lying on this floor breathing and not lying anywhere at all.

You’re alive, Pip said, quiet, something in its voice still not fully recovered from wherever the last two minutes had taken it. I want to say that plainly. You’re alive, and it was closer than anything I’ve ever watched happen to you.

“I know,” Nadia said, the words coming out thin, mostly breath.

I told you the queue hadn’t cleared. I want you to remember that I told you, later, when tonight isn’t quite so close any more.

“I know,” she said again, and didn’t have anything else to add to it, because there wasn’t a version of tonight’s decision she could defend right now with her hands still shaking against the cold concrete.

* * *

She stayed down longer than she needed to, letting her pulse settle, letting the queue’s drain do what small work it could while she caught her breath, and it was somewhere in that stretch of quiet, staring up at the corridor’s ceiling without really seeing it, that her mind did the thing it always did once the immediate crisis had passed, the same reflex that had carried her through every dislocated finger and cracked rib the mat had ever handed her. It stopped replaying the fear and started working the problem.

She sat up slowly and looked back down the corridor, toward the bend where line of sight had broken, and found herself, without quite deciding to, cataloguing the last clear look she’d gotten of the Elite before the wall had cut it from view. A new plate, bolted rather than welded, along its left shoulder assembly, dull grey steel against the older rust-streaked iron everywhere else on its frame.

She knew that plate.

She’d seen it three weeks ago, in the west gallery, on a bank of decommissioned control housings she’d walked past on her way to map a section of the site she hadn’t yet explored, distinctive machined edges and a manufacturer’s stamp worn nearly illegible, the same specific grey steel now bolted onto a shoulder that had been bare rust the last time she’d gotten a clean look at it.

You’ve gone very still again, Pip said, quiet.

“It took the plate from the west gallery,” Nadia said, low, the words arriving with the same flat certainty she’d have used identifying a part off a stripped board. “I saw that housing three weeks ago. It’s not there any more. It’s on its shoulder now.”

You’re certain, Pip said, careful, the same careful tone it had used at the bay two hours ago about the margin she’d insisted was real.

“I’m certain,” Nadia said. “Same stamp. Same edge wear. I don’t forget a part once I’ve looked at it properly, and I looked at that one properly.”

She made herself stand, testing her legs before committing her weight fully, and walked a slow, careful loop of the immediate area rather than sitting still, the movement doing double duty, generating a little warmth against the cold and letting her mind work the problem the way it worked anything better in motion than at rest. She thought back through the west gallery visit properly now, reconstructing it the way she’d reconstruct any scene she needed an honest account of, the bank of control housings arranged along the far wall, most of them stripped bare decades ago, a handful still holding components nobody had ever bothered to remove because removing them would have cost more than the components were worth to anyone keeping a Guild ledger.

She remembered noting the grey steel plate specifically, at the time, for its condition rather than its potential use, an unusually clean piece of hardware in a gallery otherwise coated in forty years of rust and grime, the kind of detail that caught a repair tech’s eye out of pure professional habit even when there was no job attached to noticing it. She hadn’t thought once, in the three weeks since, about what might eventually happen to that housing. She hadn’t needed to. Nothing in this building had ever moved on its own before, as far as she’d ever had reason to suspect.

It had moved now.

* * *

She sat with the realisation the way she sat with any fault that had finally presented itself after a long search, turning it over methodically rather than letting it arrive as a shock. The Elite had been changing. She’d noticed it in pieces over the last months, a joint that moved differently than it used to, a patch of plating that hadn’t been there in October, and had filed each observation separately without ever once connecting them into the single obvious pattern sitting in front of her now.

It was scavenging. Rebuilding itself out of the station’s own bones, decades of parts nobody had ever bothered to remove because nobody had ever wanted forty-year-old cast iron and conduit badly enough to haul it out, and every session she survived against it, every hit it landed and every hit it took, cost it something that it apparently went and repaired afterward, better than before, using whatever the building still had left to give.

“It’s getting stronger because of me,” she said, quiet, the whole shape of it settling into place with the same click a fault made when the last piece of evidence finally arrived. “Every time I fight it and it doesn’t manage to actually finish me, it goes and fixes whatever I damaged, and it doesn’t just fix it. It upgrades it. Different specification every time.”

That would explain the joint in October, Pip said, slowly. And the plating change in early November. I noticed both. I didn’t connect them the way you just did.

“I’m a repair tech who scrounges parts and rebuilds things better than they were,” Nadia said, and something in the observation landed harder than she expected it to, sitting there on the cold concrete with her pulse still not fully settled. “So is it. We’re doing the same thing to the same building.”

Two scavengers, Pip said, quiet.

“Two scavengers,” Nadia agreed. “Except it doesn’t know that’s what’s happening. It’s not competing with me on purpose. It’s just doing what it does, and I keep giving it a reason to do it more.”

She sat and thought about the mirror in it for a long moment, the specific discomfort of recognising herself in something she’d spent months trying to survive rather than understand, something she’d never once looked at as anything other than a threat to be managed and was now, apparently, going to have to think about as a kind of colleague she hadn’t asked for. She stripped parts off dead boards and rebuilt them, patiently, into something better than the original had ever been. She’d done it to the scooter, to the register at Pearce’s, to half the equipment that passed through her hands on any given week. The Elite did the same thing to itself, on a scale she’d never had the parts or the years to attempt, patient in a way nothing patient should have any right to be, forty years of undirected repair slowly accumulating into a machine that got better every time something tried to stop it.

Does it bother you, Pip asked, careful. Being compared to it, even just in your own head.

Nadia considered the question honestly, sitting with it the way she sat with anything that required an answer she hadn’t already prepared. “No,” she said, finally. “It’s not a flattering comparison, but it’s an accurate one, and accurate matters more to me than flattering ever has. I’d rather know what I actually am than feel good about a version of myself that isn’t true.”

That’s a very particular way to take an insult, Pip said, something almost fond in it.

“It’s not an insult if it’s just a fact,” Nadia said. “It only becomes an insult if I decide to feel bad about it. I don’t think I’m going to bother.”

* * *

She thought about the implication the way she thought about any tactical fact that had just changed the shape of a problem she’d been living inside for months, turning it over for everything it might eventually be worth rather than reacting to it in the moment, the same unhurried methodical process she’d have applied to any repair with more than one working part to consider. The station held a finite amount of scrap. Whatever it hadn’t already taken was still sitting in galleries and storage rooms across the whole depleted site, waiting for whichever scavenger got to it first, and she was, by every measure that actually mattered, considerably better at finding useful material in a pile of junk than a machine running on forty years of undirected salvage instinct ever would be.

She didn’t act on the thought tonight. Her legs still weren’t fully steady, the queue still sat close enough to the cap that anything more than sitting still felt like a risk she’d already spent her entire margin on once tonight, and a realisation this large deserved more consideration than she had spare capacity to give it while lying on a wet concrete floor two hundred metres from where she’d nearly died.

She filed it instead, the way she filed anything too large to solve immediately, a fact now permanently on the record, waiting for whatever future version of herself would have the resources and the recovery to actually do something with it.

There was a version of the idea, she thought, working through it in outline the way she worked through any plan she wasn’t ready to execute yet, where she got ahead of the competition entirely. Map every gallery. Catalogue every piece of usable scrap the station still held before the Elite got to it. Strip the good material for her own bench, sell what she couldn’t use, and starve the one part of this whole arrangement she couldn’t otherwise touch, a machine she could never kill without losing everything the site currently gave her, but might be able to slow simply by being faster and more thorough than it was at the one thing they were apparently both doing to the same building.

It was, she thought, exactly the kind of plan she was good at, patient, systematic, built out of nothing more exotic than paying closer attention than anyone else had ever bothered to. It didn’t require strength she didn’t have or a fight she couldn’t win. It only required time, and thoroughness, and the specific stubborn willingness to walk every gallery in this building twice before anything else got there first, which was, she reflected, more or less the entire skill set she’d spent six years building without ever once imagining it might someday apply to a building rather than a board.

It was also, she recognised immediately, not a plan for tonight, not with her legs still unsteady and a partially drained queue sitting six points closer to catastrophe than she ever wanted to feel again. Tonight was for surviving the walk home. The gallery could wait for a version of her with more margin to spend on it.

What are you thinking, right now, exactly, Pip asked, quiet, after a long silence.

“I’m thinking I need to get up,” Nadia said, “and I’m thinking about how close six points actually is, closer than I ever want to feel again, and I’m thinking I’m not doing another partial-queue entry until this cut is over, no matter what the reproduction schedule says about spacing or how badly the timing matters to it.”

I’m glad to hear that last part, Pip said, quiet. I was going to say it myself, in exactly those words, if you hadn’t gotten there first.

She pushed herself up off the concrete slowly, testing her legs before trusting her full weight to them, and began the long walk back toward the surface, the west gallery’s missing housing sitting in her memory now beside every other fact she’d ever catalogued about this building, filed, understood, and, for tonight at least, left entirely alone.

The walk out ran longer than usual, her pace slower than she liked, every step a small negotiation between what her legs wanted and what the distance to the surface actually required of them. She kept the meter in hand the whole way, checking it at intervals that felt excessive even to her, unwilling to trust a single reading after tonight, wanting the confirmation of a second and a third before she let herself believe the number was actually holding steady rather than still quietly climbing somewhere she couldn’t see.

It’s holding, Pip said, somewhere around the fourth check, gentle rather than impatient. I’ve been watching it too, this whole time. It hasn’t moved in ten full minutes, and I would have told you the second it did.

“I know,” Nadia said. “I just want to be sure.”

I understand, Pip said. I think I want you to be sure too, after everything that happened tonight. Check it as many times as you need to, all the way home if that’s what it takes to actually believe it.

The cold air at the surface hit her differently than it usually did, sharper, more insistent, a body that had spent the last hour running closer to its own limits than it had ever run before registering the ordinary world as somehow louder and brighter than it used to be, every streetlight and every passing sound arriving with a clarity she hadn’t asked for and couldn’t immediately switch off. She stood at the entry point for a long moment before climbing onto the scooter, letting her pulse settle one final time, letting the enormity of the evening arrive in pieces small enough to actually hold rather than all at once.

Six points. Two scavengers. A plate that used to sit in the west gallery, quietly, uselessly, for who knew how many years, and now didn’t sit there any more at all.

She rode home slowly, more carefully than she’d ridden in months, and did not once, the whole way back, let herself forget exactly how close six points had actually been, or how easily tonight could have ended with a number nobody would ever have gotten the chance to read.
