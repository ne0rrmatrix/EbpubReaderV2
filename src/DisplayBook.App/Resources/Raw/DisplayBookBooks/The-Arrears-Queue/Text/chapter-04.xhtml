Chapter 4: Under the Table

One day.

She had stopped writing the number on her wrist. There was no point to it any more, not when the number was small enough to hold without paper, and she carried it the way she carried everything else that had run out of anywhere useful to be written down: at the back of whatever she was actually doing, present without being in the way of it.

Ferreira had said, when Nadia put her head in that morning before school, that there was no news yet, that she’d put in a call and was waiting on a callback, in the tone of someone who had said that sentence about a hundred other files before this one and knew exactly how much weight it could and could not carry. Nadia had said thanks and left it there, the same way she left most conversations that ended in a version of the same sentence, and gone to school, and school had run the way Tuesday had, unremarkable enough that nothing about it stayed with her past the final bell.

Hana had texted twice during second period, both about Saturday, the first asking whether Nadia liked a specific kind of cake and the second, four minutes later and with no acknowledgement of the first, saying never mind, she’d just bring three kinds. Nadia had answered the second one with a single word, which seemed to satisfy whatever the question had actually needed, and put the phone away and hadn’t thought about either message again until she noticed, walking out of last period, that she still hadn’t. Priya had sent the address at lunch, exactly as promised, a house off Fourth with a photo attached of what was apparently the correct gate to use, since there were evidently two and only one of them was the right one, and Nadia had saved it without replying, because saving it answered the only part of the message that had actually required an answer.

She had three jobs booked for the afternoon and evening, which was one more than usual and exactly the amount of work that made a day feel like it had gone somewhere by the time it ended, regardless of what the day was actually going to do about the number written on the letter on her wall.

* * *

Duarte’s shop was two doors down from a laundromat on Victoria Drive, a narrow storefront with a hand-lettered sign that had been repainted at some point in a slightly different shade of the same red, shoe repair and key cutting in gold script over the door, the kind of business that had been in the same spot long enough to have outlasted three different neighbours on either side of it. The front window held a display of resoled boots and a rack of blank keys nobody bought as often as they used to, everything organised with the specific care of a person who owned very little shelf space and had made peace with that a long time ago. A bell on the door announced her rather than any sensor, an actual bell on an actual spring, the kind that had probably been there since before either of the two shops it had outlasted were built.

He had a laptop on the counter when she came in, closed, with a receipt from a repair chain on Broadway sitting on top of it, and didn’t waste time on anything before the problem. The machine had died on him a week ago, no lights, no fan, nothing, and the chain shop had kept it for four days before telling him it needed a new motherboard, three hundred and forty dollars before tax, and that whatever was on the drive was probably gone regardless, water damage they said, though he swore he’d never spilled anything on it in his life, not once, not near it, and said this with the particular offence of a man who ran a shop where nothing was ever out of place.

He needed it for the shop, the ordering system and two years of alteration records and a spreadsheet of measurements for regular customers he had never once written down anywhere else, hem lengths and shoulder widths and the specific way three different men liked a cuff broken, none of it backed up anywhere because it had never once occurred to him that it needed to be. Three hundred and forty dollars was most of a slow week’s takings for a business that mostly ran on a slow week these days, and he said so without dressing it up, the flat arithmetic of somebody who’d done that particular subtraction more than once this year already.

Nadia took it into the back room he’d cleared a corner of for her, a workbench wedged between a shelf of shoe lasts and a wall pegboard hung with cutting keys in a system only he understood, the whole space smelling faintly of leather and machine oil under the solder flux she was about to add to it. A radio somewhere in the front of the shop played something low enough that it was more rhythm than song by the time it reached her.

She opened the case and started where she always started, with her eyes rather than a tool.

The DC jack was clean. No scorching, no cracked solder around the barrel connector, nothing a visual pass would have caught, which told her nothing was wrong that a chain shop tech glancing at it for ninety seconds would have seen either, and probably explained the motherboard verdict: nothing visibly wrong, therefore something invisibly wrong, therefore replace the whole board and stop looking. She checked the hinge next, the ribbon cable that ran the display, because that was the second most common thing that killed a laptop and got blamed on the board, and found it seated properly, no fraying at the flex point, no discolouration at the connector to suggest heat cycling had done anything to it over however many years the machine had been opened and closed, thousands of times probably, given how much a shop counter got used.

She put it on her bench supply instead of the wall charger, current limited low, and brought the voltage up slowly, watching the display rather than the laptop, the way she always watched the instrument doing the actual measuring rather than the thing being measured. It pulled hard the instant she crossed a few tenths of a volt, current climbing to the limit before the voltage had gotten anywhere near what the board actually needed to see, the supply folding back exactly the way it was built to when it met a dead short rather than a load. She tried it twice more to be sure, backing the current limit down further each time, and got the same fold-back at the same low voltage every time. Not a dead board. A shorted one, somewhere on the main rail, close enough to the input that it never got the chance to do anything else, never mind boot, never mind post a single diagnostic light.

That distinction mattered more than most people understood, and it was the entire reason the chain shop had gotten it wrong. A dead board, the kind that genuinely needed replacing, still drew a small standby current when powered, a few milliamps somewhere in the system waking up and failing later. A hard short at the very first fraction of a volt meant something specific and much smaller had failed, not the processor, not the whole assembly, one component that had decided to become a wire instead of whatever it was supposed to be.

She powered the board down completely, pulled the battery, and started tracing with the multimeter in diode mode, working outward from the DC input along the main rail component by component, resting the black probe on ground and walking the red probe from part to part, watching for the one reading that dropped to a hard short instead of the small expected junction drop every semiconductor on the board was supposed to give her. Most of the board read exactly the way a schematic said it should, a quiet chorus of the same two or three numbers over and over, which was its own kind of information, a map of everywhere the fault wasn’t. It took most of twenty minutes and four false starts, components that read low but not zero, before she found it sitting close to the charging controller: a small ceramic capacitor, barely bigger than a grain of rice, reading dead short in both directions, no visible damage on the case at all, nothing that would have shown up under any light a person could shine on it without a meter already in hand telling them exactly where to look.

She lifted it with the hot air rather than pulling it, careful of the pads under it, tacky flux keeping the tip from disturbing anything around it, and touched the supply back on with the cap gone entirely, no replacement yet, just to confirm before she committed to anything.

The board woke up. Current draw normal, voltage climbing clean, no fold-back at all, the fan spinning up for its usual half-second self-test the moment it had power to spare for one.

That confirmed it, and confirmed something else she’d suspected the moment she found a shorted cap with no visible cause: liquid, a small amount, months old by the look of the faint discolouration she found on the board underneath once she got the loupe on it properly, a corrosion halo barely wider than the component itself. Something that had seeped in slowly enough to corrode through one capacitor’s internal barrier layer over time rather than killing the board outright the day it happened, the kind of failure that looked, to anyone without a bench supply and twenty minutes to spend, exactly like a board simply giving up on its own for no reason at all, weeks or months after whatever had actually caused it.

She replaced the capacitor with one from her tote, the same value, tinned fresh, and closed the machine up, and it booted on the first press into a login screen with a wallpaper of what she guessed was Duarte’s family, two kids on a beach somewhere, all of it exactly where it had always been, untouched, because nothing had ever actually reached the drive.

* * *

He paid her sixty and looked, for a moment, like he wanted to pay her more, which she declined before he could offer it, because sixty was what the job was worth by her own arithmetic and taking more than a job was worth turned every future price she quoted into a negotiation instead of a fact. He asked how she’d known to look at a single capacitor out of however many hundred were on the board, and she said the supply had told her where to look, current before voltage, and the rest was just checking every candidate until one of them was wrong, which was true and also not really an answer to what he’d actually been asking, and he seemed to understand that and didn’t push it any further.

He said he’d tell people, the way clients often said it, half a promise and half a reflex, and asked whether she had a card, something with a number on it he could hand to someone else the way he handed out his own. She said the parts board was the easiest way to find her, and wrote her handle on the back of one of his own shop cards from the stack by the register, in the small careful print she used for anything that needed to still be legible after a week in someone’s wallet, because she didn’t have a card of her own and had never seen the point in making one. He said he’d tell people, and she said that was fine, and meant it in the flat sense that more work was more work regardless of where it came from, without any particular feeling attached to being the kind of person whose name got passed along a street like this one, sixty dollars and two years of alteration records against three hundred and forty dollars and a shrug.

He said, while she packed her tools back into the roll, that if her boots ever needed resoling she should bring them in, on the house, and nodded once at the ones she was wearing, which had a wear pattern on the outside edge of both heels that told anyone who looked closely enough exactly how she stood when she was bracing against something. She said thanks and looked down at them for a second, genuinely considering it rather than deflecting, because free was free and the boots did in fact need it, and said she’d take him up on it once the season got busier and the wrestling started eating through them faster. He said any time, and meant it the plain way people who ran small shops on streets like this one tended to mean things, an offer made once and left open rather than repeated, no follow-up needed and none coming.

Her phone buzzed twice on the walk out, both messages from the board, both jobs she had to say no to before she’d even finished reading them properly: one wanted same-day, which the evening had no room left for, and the other was two bus transfers further out than she could reach and still make her third booking on time. She typed both replies standing on the sidewalk outside Duarte’s window, next week if it’s still broken, sorry, and sent them, and noted, without slowing down to think about it any further, that this was the third week running she’d turned down more work than she’d taken, not for lack of parts and not for lack of clients, only for lack of the hours a day was willing to hold.

* * *

The walk to Szabo’s ran along Victoria and cut across at a light that took its time changing regardless of whether anyone was actually crossing, long enough that she’d watched, more than once, a full cycle of traffic pass through an intersection with nobody in it at all, past a bank branch that had closed and become a phone-repair kiosk that had itself closed within the year, the paper still taped over its windows gone yellow and starting to peel at one corner, a business failing inside the shell of a business that had already failed. Further along, a hardware store that had survived everything so far had its security grate half down at four in the afternoon, a hand-written sign taped to it saying back in ten minutes in a hand Nadia recognised from three previous visits as belonging to somebody who genuinely meant it every time and was reliably wrong about the number, off by twenty minutes at least on two of those three occasions by her own count.

Szabo’s was fifteen minutes further along, a surplus and salvage shop with a roll-up door that only ever came up halfway and a floor plan that made sense to nobody but him, shelving stacked to the ceiling with bins that had handwritten labels in a hand that had gotten shakier over whatever number of years the labels spanned.

He was behind the counter when she came in with her tote of stripped components, the router boards and the phone parts and everything else she’d pulled clean over the past two weeks, sorting through a bin of his own with a jeweller’s loupe pushed up onto his forehead like a second pair of eyebrows he’d forgotten he was wearing. He set the bin aside without hurrying and took her tote instead, and went through it the way he always did, piece by piece, holding each part up to the fluorescent light over the counter before he named a price for it, turning some of them over twice, setting a few aside without comment that she knew from experience meant no, not this one, before he’d said a word.

He held one connector up close to the light for a long moment, turning it over twice, and made the same face he always made when he found one of her joints on a salvaged connector she’d resoldered rather than fully stripped, and said, without looking up, that her iron was too hot again, that she was cooking the pads instead of wetting them, that he’d told her this in March and she’d apparently spent the summer not listening to him. She said the joint held. He said a joint holding wasn’t the same as a joint done right, that holding was the bare minimum a joint owed anybody and he expected better out of somebody who otherwise clearly knew what she was doing, and that one day she’d cook a pad clean off a board she actually cared about and remember he’d said so. He counted out her total anyway, to the dollar, sliding coins across the counter with the same hand that had just finished criticising her, because whatever he thought of her technique had never once affected what he paid her for good parts, a distinction he seemed to consider entirely obvious and never explained.

A regular came in partway through, an older man after nothing in particular, browsing two aisles over with the unhurried patience of somebody with nowhere else to be, and Szabo called something across the shop to him without breaking the rhythm of counting Nadia’s parts, the two of them running an entirely separate conversation over the top of hers that neither seemed to find odd. It was the kind of shop, she’d worked out over a year of coming here, that ran less like a business and more like a room several people happened to occupy at the same time, all of it under Szabo’s flat, running commentary.

He’d had the place, by his own account, for close to thirty years, long enough that he sometimes forgot which decade a given story had happened in and had to work it out from what was still standing in the neighbourhood at the time, this shop next door or that one, gone now, replaced twice over by whatever replaced things these days, a bakery becoming a nail salon becoming, most recently, nothing at all behind a paper-covered window nobody had bothered to lease. He never explained why he still did it, and Nadia had never asked, on the theory that a man who criticised her soldering every single visit and still paid her fairly for the results didn’t owe her an explanation for anything else about himself, any more than she owed him one for hers. He had never once asked where she went to school or who she lived with, and she had noticed the absence of those questions the same way she noticed most absences, filing it without deciding what, if anything, it meant, though she suspected it meant less than it would have from anyone else, and more likely meant only that he had learned, across thirty years of a shop like this one, that most of the people who came through his door had a reason not to be asked.

She spent most of it back across the counter on stock she was low on, connectors and a reel of the thirty-gauge wire she went through faster than anything else, a handful of the small ceramic capacitors in the value she’d just used at Duarte’s because she was down to two of them and that felt, this particular week, like tempting something, and a battery cell to replace the one still sitting in the tin from the phone she’d fixed two weeks ago. Szabo rang it up against her total from the parts he’d bought, the two numbers mostly cancelling each other out the way they usually did, an economy that ran almost entirely on trading one kind of dead electronics for the working pieces of another.

She asked, because it had been sitting in the back of her afternoon since Duarte’s, whether he knew anyone who dealt in salvage from the sealed sites, the old industrial ones nobody used any more.

He looked at her for a second longer than the question strictly needed, the specific look of somebody deciding whether a question was idle or not, set down the part he’d been holding, and pushed the loupe further up onto his forehead as though he needed both eyes clear to actually answer it properly. He said there wasn’t much of that left worth chasing, not any more, that the Guild took the good material out of anything it harvested before it ever sealed the doors, and what was left behind was mostly the kind of thing that had made him old before his time, wiring nobody had inspected in decades and structures that hadn’t been surveyed since whoever surveyed them last retired and never got replaced. He’d gone into two such sites himself, years back, before his knees made the idea of climbing over rubble in the dark sound less like an adventure and more like a hospital visit, and come out with barely enough scrap to cover the gas.

He mentioned, almost as an aside, already turning back toward the regular still browsing two aisles over, the old pumping station out past the reservoir. Said there’d been talk once, years ago, about what was still down in it, some low-tier nuisance the Guild hadn’t bothered clearing all the way before they sealed it, category filed and forgotten. As far as he knew nobody had gone back for any of it since, because whatever material was left wasn’t worth the walk against whatever might still be running down there, harvested sites being harvested for a reason and sealed sites being sealed for a better one.

He said it the way he said most things, flatly, a fact passed along rather than a warning issued, the kind of thing he’d have said to anyone who asked the same question, not a caution aimed at her specifically, and went back to the man two aisles over before Nadia had said anything in response, the conversation already closed on his end even if it wasn’t quite closed on hers.

She said thanks and didn’t ask anything further, because he’d already moved on to the next thing on his counter and the next conversation with the man two aisles over, and because she had a third job to get to before six, but she filed the name the way she filed most things that arrived sideways rather than head-on, without deciding yet what, if anything, it was worth.

* * *

The third job was small, a phone screen for a woman two blocks from Kelso House who’d found her through the same parts board that found her everyone. The woman answered the door already apologising for the mess of a front hall that wasn’t particularly messy, a habit Nadia had noticed in a lot of clients, as though a stranger with a toolroll in the house required some kind of preemptive explanation for how the house actually looked. The screen swap was routine, the kind of job that had stopped requiring any real attention from her years ago, and she worked it while the woman’s two younger kids watched from the hallway in the specific silent fascination children reserved for anyone doing something with their hands that they weren’t allowed to touch. Twenty minutes and twenty-five dollars, done before the light outside had fully gone, and the woman thanked her twice, once handing over the phone and once again at the door, the same pattern Delacourt had run weeks ago, and Nadia still hadn’t worked out why the second one felt necessary to anybody.

The woman mentioned, folding the twenty-five into Nadia’s hand rather than leaving it on the counter for her to pick up, that her sister two streets over had a tablet with a dead charging port, no rush, whenever Nadia had a window. Nadia said she’d message about it, and meant it, and added the address to the mental list she kept of work that was waiting on nothing but her own available hours, a list that had gotten longer every week since the start of September and showed no sign of getting shorter on its own.

It was the fourth address like that she’d added to the list this week alone, on top of the three jobs she’d actually managed to fit into a day that only had so many hours in it regardless of how many people wanted a piece of the ones that were left after school and wrestling and whatever the Ministry decided to schedule next.

By the time she got back to the room it was past eight, and she hung her jacket on the radiator that wasn’t running out of habit more than expectation, since October was still weeks off and the radiator knew it as well as she did. She sat at the bench with the week’s cash counted out in front of her the way she counted it every few days, less for the arithmetic itself than for the habit of confirming the number matched what she remembered it should be, coins and small bills sorted into piles the way she sorted components, by denomination rather than by any story attached to where each one had come from.

Sixty from Duarte. Forty from Szabo’s buyback against what she’d spent restocking, which came out closer to thirty-five once she accounted for the capacitors and the battery cell honestly rather than rounding the way she was tempted to. Twenty-five from the phone. Thirty from the courier two weeks back that was still sitting uncounted in the same pile, folded in with two smaller amounts from jobs before that stretching back further than she bothered adding up tonight, a running total she trusted more as an order of magnitude than as an exact figure, because exact figures were for boards, not for a life assembled out of however many strangers’ broken things she’d gotten to in a given month.

It came to more, this week, than the nine hundred a month a room would have cost her. More than twelve hours a week at anything the Ministry would have recognised as a placement could have paid at any wage a sixteen-year-old could legally be offered, even before accounting for the fact that no such placement had actually materialised in the three weeks since Bhandari had described one, and that everyone she’d asked so far either wanted an adult’s signature on the application or didn’t hire under seventeen at all, categories designed, she assumed, around some version of a teenager who had a parent to co-sign the difference. And none of it, the sixty or the forty or the twenty-five or any of the rest of it, existed anywhere a form could see. A whole working week that would show up on no document the Ministry’s system had a field for, filed instead in a cigarette tin on a bench in a room she had one hour, technically, still left to be allowed to sleep in, by a letter written in a typeface nobody had revisited since before she was born.

She noted this the same way she noted a reading that didn’t match a datasheet, a fact about the system rather than a complaint against it, the kind of fact you filed and moved past because there was no component on the board you could actually replace to fix it. She put the cash in the tin and went back to the board still open on the bench, a job she’d started two nights ago and not yet finished, someone else’s dead router she’d picked up cheap off the board specifically to fix and resell rather than to strip, because the evening had run out before the board had, and there was at least one thing in the room tonight that would end up in better shape for her having spent an hour on it than it had been in when she started.

The voucher letter was still on the wall, one day written across the bottom in her own hand from that morning, and the room-search tab was still open on her phone where she’d left it, the same four hundred listings in the same order they’d been in for two weeks, and she left both of them exactly where they were, because looking at either one again tonight wasn’t going to change what either of them said.

She worked until her eyes stopped focusing properly on the loupe, which came earlier than she wanted it to, the letters on the router’s silkscreen blurring at the edges no matter how many times she blinked them back into focus, and packed the half-finished board back into its case rather than force another twenty minutes out of a day that had already given her everything it had. The cash tin went into the drawer under the bench, the one with the lock that barely worked and had never once stopped anyone determined enough to try it, though nobody at Kelso House ever had, and she turned off the light and lay down still half dressed, the smell of solder flux and Duarte’s leather and machine oil all still faintly on her hands, and let the one day on the wall be somebody else’s problem for the next seven hours, because there was nothing left in her tonight, not one working hour of it, that was going to solve it any faster by staying awake over it.
