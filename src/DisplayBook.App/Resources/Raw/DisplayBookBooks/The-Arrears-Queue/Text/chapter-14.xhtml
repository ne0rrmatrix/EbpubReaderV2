Chapter 14: No Signal

The scooter had started as a purely practical problem, the kind she solved without thinking too hard about it, the same way she solved a dead phone or a jammed lock: the fire road sat too far from any bus stop she could actually reach on the schedule the rest of her week left her, and a bicycle would have meant carrying it up a ladder she needed both hands free for, and walking the whole stretch had already eaten more of her precious evening light than she could really afford to keep losing as the days shortened steadily toward winter.

She’d found the frame on the parts board in late October, a short listing that read barely running, motor dead, going cheap, and paid twenty dollars cash for a machine that had clearly been left outside in one too many winters already, the deck cracked at one corner and the whole thing carrying the specific defeated posture of something its last owner had simply, finally stopped believing in altogether. The seller, a man clearing out a shed before winter properly set in, had thrown in a spare set of grip tape and a bag of assorted hardware without being asked, the same casual generosity she kept running into on this side of the city, people who had long since stopped expecting anything back for a small kindness and gave them out anyway, freely, without ceremony.

She’d spent three separate evenings on it since, stripping the dead motor for salvageable parts, rebuilding the controller from a donor board she’d pulled off a scrapped e-bike months earlier, replacing a battery pack that had swollen enough to lift its own housing clean off, and truing the front wheel with a small hand file until the wobble in it disappeared entirely. Each evening had followed the same shape as every other repair she’d ever done, methodical, unhurried, the small satisfaction of watching something broken become something whole again under her own two hands. By the second week of November it ran clean, quiet, faster than anything she strictly needed for a fifteen-minute stretch of fire road, and she found herself taking the long way round to a job or two just to feel it under her a little longer than the actual route ever required.

You’ve put more hours into that scooter than into some of the boards you actually get paid for, Pip observed, one evening, watching her true up a wheel with a small hand file she kept in the drawer for exactly that kind of fine adjustment, the bay quiet around them both, the compressor on the other side of the wall ticking through its usual cycle.

“It’s the only thing that gets me to the site without eating an hour of my week,” Nadia said. “That’s worth the hours.”

I’m not arguing the logic. I’m just noticing the affection underneath it, which you’d probably deny if I called it that directly.

“I’d deny it,” Nadia agreed, and kept filing, the small even rasp of metal on metal the only sound in the bay for a while after that, comfortable rather than empty.

* * *

The inversion had been building for weeks before she actually named it, the same way most of her observations about Pip had built slowly rather than arriving all at once, small accumulated differences she hadn’t bothered to add up until the pattern got obvious enough to stop ignoring.

Underground, Pip was entirely present in a way it never quite managed anywhere else, every observation sharp, every question landing the moment the situation actually called for it, a kind of attention she suspected, though she’d never once asked directly, cost it something to sustain. It noticed things down there she’d have missed on her own, a shift in the thing’s footing half a second before it moved, a change in the cold’s texture near a wall she hadn’t thought to check, small margins that had probably kept her out of trouble more times than she’d ever properly credited it for.

Aboveground, in ordinary rooms, at school, at Pearce’s, even here at the bench some evenings, it drifted. Not absent exactly. Present the way a person was present on a phone call they weren’t fully listening to, responses arriving a beat late, half-formed, occasionally answering a question she’d asked two minutes earlier rather than the one she’d just finished asking. She’d catch it mid-sentence sometimes, a thought trailing off into nothing, as though whatever had been holding its attention a moment ago had simply reasserted itself and pulled the rest of the sentence away with it.

It was strangest during class, she found, a room full of other people’s conversations apparently competing for whatever bandwidth Pip actually had up here, and she’d taken, over the last few weeks, to simply not expecting company during the school day at all, treating any comment that did arrive as a small bonus rather than the baseline she’d come to expect underground.

She tested it, once, deliberately, standing in the cafeteria line with a question she already knew the answer to, just to see how long the reply took to land. Four seconds. Long enough that a person watching her face would have seen the pause and wondered what had caused it, and she’d learned, over the following weeks, to build that pause into her own rhythm without thinking about it, a small tax on every ordinary conversation that ran alongside whatever else it was doing.

She ran the same test twice more over the following days, once in the wrestling changeroom and once at Pearce’s counter during a quiet stretch of the shift, and got roughly the same delay both times, which told her the effect was consistent rather than incidental, a genuine property of wherever they both happened to be standing rather than a one-off distraction that would simply pass. She wrote none of it down, because it wasn’t the kind of fact a notebook could hold any better than her own memory already did, but she filed it the same way regardless, quietly, alongside everything else about Pip she was slowly assembling into something like an understanding.

I don’t entirely understand it either, Pip admitted, when she finally asked outright, sitting on the edge of her bed one evening with the bay quiet around her. Down there I feel like I’m all the way present. Up here it’s like trying to hold a thought in a room with the radio on, except the radio is everything, all the time, and I can’t quite make myself stop hearing it.

“Is it the field,” Nadia asked, sitting back against the wall with the notebook still open on her lap. “Density. Stronger down there, weaker everywhere else, the same way the cold is stronger the closer I get to it.”

That’s the theory I keep landing on too. I don’t have a way to test it properly, no instrument for it the way you have your meter for anything electrical. I just know that the two versions of me feel different enough that I’ve started thinking of them as two different qualities of the same thing, rather than one steady presence that occasionally gets distracted from you.

“That’s not reassuring.”

No, Pip agreed. It really isn’t. I’m sorry. I wish I had a cleaner answer for you.

Also, entirely unrelated, it added, a moment later, in the specific tone it used whenever a thought had simply arrived and demanded to be said out loud before it could be properly examined for relevance, I need to apologise to someone eventually, and I don’t currently know how, and it’s bothering me more than it probably should.

Nadia looked up from the wheel she was truing. “What did you do.”

I genuinely don’t want to get into it right now, Pip said, already retreating from the subject with a speed that told her more than the words themselves did. It isn’t urgent. It isn’t dangerous. It’s just going to take some working out, and I’d rather work it out properly before I say anything else about it.

“Okay,” Nadia said, and let it go, because pressing a closed door rarely opened it any faster, and because she had learned, over the last two months, that Pip’s stray remarks about its own life above ground tended to arrive exactly like this, unexplained, unfollowed, gone as quickly as they’d surfaced, and that chasing every one of them down would have cost her more time than she currently had to spend on mysteries that weren’t actually hers.

Thank you, Pip said, quietly, and didn’t bring it up again that evening, though Nadia noticed, filing it the way she filed most things, that it hadn’t actually promised to bring it up any evening after this one either.

* * *

Practice that Thursday ran late, a scheduling mix-up that had two teams sharing the gym for twenty minutes longer than either coach had planned for, and in the crush of bodies and gear bags someone’s ankle rolled badly on the edge of the mat where it met the hard floor, a girl from the other team going down hard enough that the whole noisy room stopped cold, all at once.

Nadia happened to be closest. She was already down on her knees beside the girl before anyone else in the room had even finished processing what had actually happened, checking the ankle carefully with the same flat competence she’d bring to any joint that had just taken a genuinely bad angle, feeling carefully for the specific give of a break against the more ordinary give of a sprain, and found the latter, which mattered a great deal, because a sprain iced and elevated correctly for the next crucial ten minutes healed considerably better than one simply left to swell on its own while everyone stood around arguing about whether to call someone in.

She had the girl’s foot carefully elevated on a rolled mat and a bag of ice, produced quickly from somewhere in Val’s kit, pressed against the joint before the other coach had even reached them, talking the girl through the whole thing in the same flat, steady voice she used for anything that needed doing under pressure, name the injury, name the plan, keep the panic out of both. She stayed kneeling there patiently, holding the ice firmly in place, until the swelling had visibly slowed and the girl’s breathing had settled out of the sharp, panicked rhythm it had started in, checking the toes for colour and sensation twice more than was strictly necessary because a second check had never once cost her a single thing and a missed sign could have cost the girl considerably more.

The other coach thanked her twice, the way people always seemed to feel the need to, kneeling down herself now that the crisis had passed to check her own team member over properly, and asked how Nadia had known to check for a break before anything else. Nadia said mostly just feeling, which was true, and left it there, the same answer she gave everyone who asked, and the coach seemed to accept it the way most people accepted it, without pushing any further. Val, watching quietly from a few feet away with her arms crossed, said nothing at all, though something in the specific way she watched suggested she’d filed the whole moment somewhere she fully intended to keep it, ready to draw on later if she ever needed it.

Tomasz caught her afterward, in the noise of the gym clearing out, bag already slung over one shoulder, and asked, half joking, whether there was anything she actually couldn’t fix. She said plenty of things, and he laughed like that wasn’t really an answer, and she let him think that, because explaining the actual list, starting with a scale in this very room and ending somewhere out past the reservoir he’d never believe if she told him, would have taken considerably longer than either of them had time for tonight.

It cost her twenty minutes she hadn’t planned to spend, twenty minutes that pushed her evening schedule tighter than she liked against a session she still needed to run before the weekend closed, and she didn’t regret a single one of them, filing the whole incident the way she filed most things she did for people, without examining too closely why it had never once occurred to her not to. The other coach asked, before the two teams finally cleared out of the gym, whether Nadia wrestled for this school or the other one, having apparently lost track in the chaos of whose roster she actually belonged to, and Val answered for her before she had to, a short flat sentence that closed the question without opening any others, and Nadia was grateful for it in a way she didn’t say out loud.

* * *

She rode out that Saturday with the scooter running clean under her for the first time on the actual fire road rather than a test loop around the block near the bay, cold air biting at her face in a way that felt, oddly, good, a clean physical sensation with no complicated weight attached to it at all.

The gate with the slack chain came up faster than she expected at speed, headlights of a distant car sweeping across the treeline once before disappearing entirely, and she killed the throttle in time, walked it through, and left the scooter propped against a tree just inside the fence line, out of sight from the road, chained through the frame to a trunk thick enough that nobody was walking off with it easily even if they somehow found it out here.

The building sat exactly where it always sat, patient under its sagging wire, and she went down through the roof hatch the way she always went down, counting the rungs, letting her ears do the first work before her eyes had anything to report. The session itself ran the way sessions had started running lately, familiar, managed, six clean exchanges and one that clipped her at an angle costing more than the rest, her breathing settling into the same steady rhythm she used on the mat, base low, angle everything, the room and the cold and the thing itself narrowing down to the next half-second and nothing beyond it.

She photographed the foreleg on the way out this time, three quick frames from a distance she judged safe, the flash off, her hands steady enough now that the shots came out usable despite the low light and the short window she gave herself to take them. She checked each frame before she pocketed the phone, confirming the panel was actually in focus rather than trusting the shot blind, because a blurred photograph would have cost her the whole point of taking the risk to get it. The panel itself, up close, looked exactly the way it had from a distance the first time, a slightly different grain to the metal, less rust, the unmistakable signature of a repair rather than an original part, and she filed the confirmation without yet knowing what to do with it.

Got it, Pip said, the moment the third frame landed, its full attention snapping back into focus the way it always did the instant something in the room actually mattered. That should be enough to compare properly once you’re somewhere you can actually look at it, side by side with whatever you can dig up from the first two visits, if anything survived that far back.

She nodded, packed the phone away, and started the walk back toward the fence, the cold already beginning its familiar work on her chest, the same low ache that told her the queue had opened and was already, somewhere she couldn’t feel yet, beginning its slow count toward whatever it would cost her tonight, a bill she’d long since stopped resenting and simply learned to budget around.

* * *

The scooter wouldn’t start.

She stood at the tree for a full minute pressing the throttle before she let herself actually look at the problem rather than simply willing it away, cursing herself for not treating the walk back with the same caution she treated the walk out, and knelt on the cold grass beside the frame.

Once she had the deck panel off, she found nothing obviously wrong. Battery reading full, the display confirming exactly the charge she’d left it at that morning. Controller board seated properly, no scorching, no visible damage on any trace. Motor leads intact, no corrosion, no loose connector, the crimps she’d done herself holding exactly as tight as she’d left them three weeks ago. Every individual component, checked in isolation, reported exactly the healthy status it was supposed to report, and none of them, together, produced so much as a flicker from the display when she pressed the throttle again.

She swapped the battery for the spare cell she kept in her bag for exactly this kind of emergency, wired in fresh, terminals bright and clean, and got nothing at all, not even the small confirmation click the display usually gave on connection. She checked the ground connection twice, then a third time, using the multimeter rather than trusting her eyes, and the continuity read clean every single time, the reading identical down to the last digit no matter how many times she repeated it, which told her nothing was intermittently loosening or reseating itself, only that the fault, whatever it was, wasn’t hiding in anything a resistance test could catch. It was, by every measure her tools could actually give her, a perfectly functional machine that had simply, categorically, refused to do the one thing it was built to do.

She sat back on her heels in the cold grass, the ache in her chest starting to properly announce itself now, and ran the fault the way she ran any fault that refused an easy diagnosis, from the outside in, ignoring what she expected to find and trusting only what she could actually measure.

Do you need help, Pip asked, something careful in it, the way it always sounded when it wasn’t sure whether an offer would be welcome or simply in the way.

“I need quiet,” Nadia said, not unkindly, and Pip, to its credit, gave her exactly that.

She traced it the way she traced anything, multimeter out despite her hands starting to lose their steadiness, checking continuity along every connection one at a time, working outward from the battery the way she’d work outward from any suspect power rail, and found, twenty minutes in, with the ache in her chest climbing toward something she was going to need to eat against soon, nothing. No fault. No open circuit. No short. A perfectly healthy machine that simply, inexplicably, would not run, sitting in front of her like a diagnosis with no symptom attached to explain it.

Is it the cold, Pip offered, tentative, clearly reaching for anything that might help. Some batteries perform worse in low temperature. I don’t know if that applies to this particular chemistry.

“It’s not the cold,” Nadia said, more sharply than she meant to, and immediately regretted the edge in it. “Sorry. It’s not the cold. This one’s rated for it. I checked that specifically before I bought the cells.”

It’s all right, Pip said, and let the silence sit for a moment before trying again, more careful this time. For what it’s worth, I don’t think this is a fault you’re going to find with a multimeter, no matter how many times you run it. I don’t know what it is instead. But everything you’ve checked reads correct, and a correct reading on every single component usually means the fault, if there is one, lives somewhere your instruments simply aren’t built to see at all.

“That’s not comforting at all,” Nadia said, echoing, without quite meaning to, the exact words Pip had once said back to her weeks ago, in almost the exact same tone.

I’m aware, Pip said. I don’t have a comforting version available to give you tonight. I only have the honest one, and I’d rather give you that than something reassuring and false.

She gave up on the throttle after the fourth attempt produced the same nothing as the first three, and pulled the battery lead altogether, unclipping it with fingers gone clumsy from the cold, because a machine with no power connected to it at all was at least a machine that made sense, dead for a reason rather than dead for no reason. She meant to walk it the rest of the way, hands on the grips the way she’d walked it home once before over a flat tire, and had already leaned her weight into the first step when the deck came up under her palms on its own, smooth and silent, the whole frame surging forward against a throttle she wasn’t touching, a battery that wasn’t connected, and a motor she couldn’t feel turning inside its own housing.

She let go. It stopped dead the instant her hands left the grips, settling back onto the grass exactly as inert as it had been for the last half hour, the battery lead still swinging loose against the frame where she’d left it.

She put her hands back on the grips, deliberately this time, watching rather than reacting, and it happened again, the same smooth surge, faster than a dead scooter had any business moving, faster, if she let herself be honest about the number, than it had ever moved for her with a working battery in it at all.

She rode it home like that, the lead still hanging loose and disconnected the whole way, current from nowhere climbing clean under a throttle wired to nothing, the ride so silent under her that she could hear her own breathing over it for the first time since she’d built the thing. She didn’t check the display. She didn’t trust it to tell her the truth even if she had. She made it back to the bay with the crash arriving in full somewhere on the last stretch of the fire road even so, forcing her to stop, one hand still locked on the grip out of simple disbelief, and eat everything she’d packed before her hands shook too badly to manage the wrapper, the ordinary crisis at least following the ordinary rules even while the scooter refused to follow any at all.

* * *

She got the scooter carefully into the bay that night more out of sheer habit than any real intention to work on it any further, propped it carefully against the wall right beside the bench, and sat down slowly on the wet floor right in front of it once the worst of the crash had finally passed, jacket still damp from the ride and the grass she’d been kneeling in, the concrete cold enough under her that she felt it through her jeans within a minute or so, and didn’t bother moving to fix it.

She looked at the thing for a very long time, saying nothing at all.

It sat there the way it always sat, a machine she no longer had a plain word for, every component something she’d chosen and fitted and tested herself, from the carefully crimped connectors to the truing on the front wheel to the small patch of paint she’d touched up on the cracked corner of the deck with a colour that didn’t quite match but was close enough that it never once bothered her. And somewhere inside it, in a fault she hadn’t found and had no evidence actually existed, something had happened tonight that her instruments simply hadn’t been built to see.

She ran the whole evening back from the start, the way she ran anything that refused to resolve, checking each step against what she actually remembered rather than what would have made the tidiest story. The ride out, clean. The session, ordinary, six managed exchanges and one that clipped her. The walk back to the tree, nothing unusual, no new cold, no sound, nothing that had registered as different from any of the three visits before it. And then a machine that had worked perfectly an hour earlier simply refusing to acknowledge that it existed, for exactly as long as it chose to, and then moving under her own two hands with no battery connected and no motor she could feel turning, with no explanation offered for either half of that sentence.

You’re very quiet, Pip said, eventually, once enough time had passed that the silence itself had started to feel like its own kind of answer.

“I don’t have anything to say yet,” Nadia said. “I checked everything I know how to check. It all reads fine. It’s not supposed to be able to do what it just did.”

What does that mean, in your framework. When everything reads fine and the thing does something it shouldn’t be able to do at all.

“It means I’m checking the wrong things,” Nadia said, and looked at the machine a while longer, cold seeping up through the concrete, no answer arriving, only the same flat unresolved fact sitting in front of her exactly the way it had sat for the last hour.

Does it frighten you, Pip asked, quietly. A machine you built yourself doing something you can’t explain.

Nadia considered the question honestly, the way she considered most things Pip asked her at the end of a long night, when her guard had worn down enough that the easy answer no longer felt like the true one.

“No,” she said, finally. “It bothers me. Frightened is a different thing. I was frightened on a bathroom floor. This is just a fact I don’t have an explanation for yet, and I don’t like that, but not liking a fact isn’t the same as fearing it.”

That’s a very fine distinction.

“It’s an important one,” Nadia said. “If I let every unexplained thing frighten me, I’d never get anything done. I’d just be frightened all the time.”

Given the actual year you’ve had, that would be a perfectly reasonable state to be in, Pip said, something dry in it, the first thing close to a real joke either of them had managed since the tree line. I don’t think anyone alive would blame you for it, honestly.

“I’d blame me,” Nadia said, and almost smiled, the closest she came to it most nights, and reached out to run a thumb along the deck of the scooter, the crack she’d touched up, the paint that didn’t quite match, an ordinary object that had never once, in three whole weeks of steady nightly work, given her even one single reason to doubt it, until tonight had come along and quietly changed all of that.

* * *

Pearce mentioned, the following Tuesday, apropos of very little, ringing up a customer’s paint while he talked, that a woman two streets over had asked whether anyone knew a kid good with electronics, said her grandson’s hearing aid had gone dead over the weekend and the clinic wouldn’t see him until the new year, and would Nadia mind stopping by after her shift.

She went straight over from Pearce’s that same afternoon, still in the exact same jacket she’d worked her whole shift in, and found a small, tidy apartment two blocks off the Drive, the grandmother apologetic and clearly quite worried, the boy himself sitting very still on the couch with the specific quiet of a child who’d learned, in the space of one long weekend, exactly how much the whole world shrank when a single small device stopped working properly.

The aid turned out to be a corroded battery contact, the same slow water-ingress failure she’d seen a hundred times on a hundred other devices, the specific green crust she could have identified with her eyes closed by now. She had it working again in under ten minutes with a contact she cleaned rather than replaced, using a fine brush and a solution she carried in her bag for exactly this purpose, because the boy was seven and didn’t need to wait on a part she’d have had to order in when the fix sitting right in front of her was already this straightforward.

He tested it carefully, one hand cupped near his ear, and his whole face changed the moment the small hum of an ordinary room reached him again, a transformation she found herself watching more closely than she usually watched anything, filing the shape of it without quite understanding why it seemed to matter more, this time, than the fix itself ever had before.

His grandmother tried to pay her sixty dollars for what had taken ten minutes, pressing the bills into her hand with the particular insistence of someone who felt genuinely, deeply relieved and needed somewhere to put that feeling, and Nadia said fifteen was fair, and the woman insisted a second time, and Nadia held the line the way she held it with everyone, because a price that didn’t match the work made every future price she quoted into a negotiation instead of a fact. The woman finally accepted the fifteen, though she pressed a second bill into Nadia’s coat pocket on the way out the door when Nadia wasn’t looking directly at her, which Nadia only found later, at home, going through her jacket, and decided not to make an issue of, because returning it would have cost the woman considerably more dignity than simply keeping it ever cost Nadia at all.

The boy, once the aid was back in and working, said thank you in the careful, deliberate way of someone who had only recently learned to hear his own voice properly again, testing the volume of it the way a person tests a stair they aren’t sure will hold their weight, and Nadia said he was welcome, and meant it in the flat, uncomplicated way she meant most things, without dwelling on it any further than that.

She walked home with fifteen dollars in her pocket and a second bill she hadn’t asked for, the scooter’s unsolved fault still sitting somewhere behind everything else she was doing, present the way a held breath is present, and didn’t mention the boy to Pip at all, not because it wasn’t worth mentioning but because some part of her had simply filed it, the way she filed most of what she did for people, as a task completed rather than a story worth telling.

You’re doing it again, Pip said, somewhere along the walk, gentle rather than accusing. The thing where something good happens because of you and you don’t say a word about it. I only know because I was there. If I hadn’t been, that whole afternoon would simply not exist anywhere except in a seven-year-old’s memory and a grandmother’s.

“It doesn’t need to exist anywhere else,” Nadia said, quiet but certain. “It happened. That’s enough. It doesn’t need a witness to have been real.”

I don’t think that’s quite true, but I also don’t think tonight is the right night to argue it with you, Pip said, and let the subject rest there, and the two of them walked the rest of the way back to the bay in a comfortable silence that had, by now, stopped feeling like an absence of conversation and started feeling like its own particular kind of company.
