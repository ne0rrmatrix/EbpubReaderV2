Chapter 6: The Window

The gap came four days later, a Thursday with no job booked and no practice scheduled, the kind of hole in a week that opened up rarely enough that she’d learned to notice it the moment it appeared on her calendar rather than let it fill itself in without her.

She had checked the weather twice the night before, an old habit from every job that meant standing outside for any length of time, and confirmed nothing more useful than that it would probably not rain, which out here counted as good news regardless of how it actually turned out.

She packed the list from the drawer that morning before school: the headlamp, charged overnight; four spare batteries taped together so they wouldn’t rattle against anything else in the bag; the multitool instead of the single flathead; a spool of the brightest paracord she owned, for marking a way back out of somewhere she hadn’t mapped yet. She added, without it being on the original list, a full water bottle and the two protein bars she kept in her bag for days that ran long, and looked at the small pile on the bench for a moment before she zipped it all into the bottom of her pack, underneath her school things, where a teacher glancing into the bag would see binders and nothing else.

School ran the way school ran, Priya talking through most of second period about a documentary topic that had changed twice more since the last time Nadia had heard it, Hana confirming plans for a thing on the weekend that Nadia agreed to without quite processing what it was. None of it stayed with her the way it usually didn’t. She had spent most of the day with a small, steady portion of her attention on the pack under her desk, checking without looking that the zipper hadn’t worked itself open, the way she checked anything she’d packed for a job that actually mattered.

She was on the bus by half past three, and this time she didn’t go all the way out to the Henderson stop. A satellite map the night before had shown her a decommissioned fire road cutting into the watershed land from the north side, closer to a different bus route entirely, unmarked on the transit app but visible enough from above once she knew to look for the straight grey line through the trees, a service access that had probably been used weekly once and now showed no sign of tire tracks newer than the moss growing up through the gravel.

She got off two stops early and walked in from there instead, along a gated access road with a chain slack enough to duck under and a sign identical to the one at the Henderson fence, warning of the same restricted access to nobody in particular. The road ran straight for the better part of a kilometre before it curved to follow the contour of the land, trees close enough on both sides to shut out most of the afternoon light, and she walked it at a pace just under a jog, watching her footing on gravel gone loose in patches where roots had pushed up through it.

* * *

The fire road brought her to the perimeter wire from a different angle than before, further from the structure itself, and she followed the fence line on the inside of it this time rather than the outside, moving through waist-high grass that hadn’t been cut in years, until the low concrete shape came into view again through the trees, exactly where she remembered it, sitting in its clearing like something that had simply always been there.

The cold reached her again before she was close enough to see the building clearly, the same specific drop she’d felt through the fence four days ago, sharper now with nothing between her and its source. She stood still at the tree line and let it settle over her, checking her own reaction the way she checked a meter reading, and found nothing in herself that resembled fear yet, only the particular alertness of standing next to something she didn’t understand.

The roof hatch was where she’d expected it to be, at the top of the rusted ladder bolted to the outside wall. Up close, two of the rungs were gone entirely rather than merely rusted through, snapped clean at the weld, and she tested each remaining one with her weight before committing to it, climbing the way she’d climb anything unfamiliar, hands first, weight distributed, always a second point of contact before she trusted the first.

The hatch itself was not locked. It had a latch, the kind meant to be operated from inside with a simple turn handle, and from outside it had been secured at some point with a padlock through a hasp that had since rusted through the hasp itself rather than the lock, the whole assembly hanging loose enough that she lifted it off in one motion and set it down on the roof beside her. Whoever had welded the ground-level door shut had never come back up here to finish the job properly. It had never seemed worth it, probably, once the main door was sealed, a maintenance hatch on a roof nobody was going to climb to.

She lifted the hatch. Stale air came up to meet her, cool and mineral and utterly still, air that had not moved in longer than she could guess at, and below it, a ladder descending into dark that her headlamp only pushed back so far before it gave up and let the rest stay black.

* * *

She went down slowly, one rung at a time, headlamp sweeping the walls as she descended, counting rungs out of the same habit that made her count anything, twenty-two of them before her boots found solid floor, which put the ladder at somewhere close to five metres, roughly matching what she’d estimated from outside based on the ventilation stacks.

The interior told her its own version of the story the outside had already told. Concrete walls streaked with decades of mineral deposit where water had once run and long since stopped, pale trails branching down from every seam like something that had bled slowly for years and then simply stopped bleeding. A catwalk ran off into darkness in one direction, steel grating underfoot, guardrail on one side rusted to a texture like coral. Pipe runs overhead sat thick enough to have once moved a serious volume of water, most of them dry now, a few still holding a stagnant inch that reflected her light back at her in a thin black line whenever the beam crossed it.

The cold was total down here, sourceless, the kind of cold that didn’t belong to any weather system she could name. Her breath showed in the beam of the headlamp. She tied off the paracord at the base of the ladder, secured to a pipe bracket that had survived everything else, and let out slack as she went, marking her own way back the only way she trusted.

The catwalk led her past two side rooms, both empty, one holding the rusted shell of a control panel with every gauge smashed or corroded past reading, needles frozen behind glass gone opaque with age, the other stripped down to bare concrete and two core-drilled holes in the floor that matched the ones she’d seen outside, whatever equipment had lived here taken cleanly and completely, right down to the mounting bolts. A third room further along held nothing but a floor drain and a coil of hose rotted to something closer to fibre than rubber, hanging off a wall bracket in a shape that had clearly not moved in decades.

She moved carefully, the way she moved through any structure she hadn’t mapped, checking her footing before her weight, checking the ceiling before she walked under anything that looked capable of coming down, keeping one hand near the paracord at all times without consciously deciding to. Twice she stopped to note a branch point in the catwalk, a side passage she didn’t take, filing each one the way she filed a schematic she hadn’t traced yet, present, available, not yet understood.

She heard nothing the entire way. Not water, not wind through the open vent stack, not her own footsteps once she noticed how little sound the concrete under her boots was actually returning, each footfall arriving and then simply ending, no echo carrying it onward the way an empty structure this size should have carried it. The silence had a quality to it that felt less like absence and more like something actively held, though she couldn’t have explained the difference to anyone who asked, only that she noticed it the way she noticed a circuit that was too quiet to be doing nothing, the specific wrongness of a system that ought to be making noise and had chosen, deliberately, not to.

* * *

The cold sharpened all at once, a full degree or more in the space of a single step, and she stopped, checking the reading against herself the way she’d have checked an instrument, because a gradient that fast meant a source close by, closer than anything she’d felt yet.

She turned the headlamp down the catwalk ahead of her, toward a wider chamber where the pipe runs converged, and found nothing in the beam at first, just more concrete, more rust, the catwalk continuing on toward a shape at the far edge of the light that resolved, as she watched it, from architecture into something else.

It was not built the way anything else in this building was built. It had the mass and the material of a valve assembly, cast iron gone dark with age, rust in patches across a housing that still showed, in places, the ghost of a manufacturer’s stencil, letters she couldn’t make out from here and had no wish to get closer to read. But it stood on limbs that no valve assembly had ever needed, four of them, jointed wrong for anything that had started life as machinery, angles that made sense for nothing she’d ever studied on a schematic, planted on the catwalk with the specific stillness of something that had been standing exactly there for longer than she wanted to think about.

It made no sound at all, not even the sound its own mass should have made simply existing, no creak of settling metal, no drip, nothing. It was not looking at her. It was facing down the catwalk in the other direction entirely, toward whatever it had been doing before she arrived, some slow patrol along a route it had walked ten thousand times since before she was born, oriented toward a task rather than toward a threat, the specific incuriosity of something that had never once needed to look over its shoulder because nothing had ever come down here to make it.

She held her breath without deciding to, the way a person holds still around something large and sleeping, and understood, in the same flat diagnostic part of her mind that catalogued everything else, that she had two real options and about four seconds in which either one was still available to her: leave now, quietly, while it remained facing the other direction, or stay long enough to actually see what it was going to do next, and that the second option was not one any repair technician with proper judgement would have chosen.

She stayed anyway, one more second, because the itch to actually understand what she was looking at had never once, in her whole life, lost to caution when the two of them disagreed, and a second, it turned out, was exactly long enough for the decision to be made for her.

She stood very still, the way she stood still around anything unpredictable, and did the thing she always did first, before feeling anything about a situation: she mapped it.

The catwalk ran maybe a metre wide here, guardrail on the left side only, open drop on the right down to a lower level she couldn’t see the bottom of in her headlamp’s throw. The pipe run overhead sat low enough to duck under but not low enough to hide behind. Thirty metres back to the ladder, the way she’d come, marked the whole way by the paracord she’d been paying out. Twenty metres ahead, the shape stood at the point where the catwalk widened into the chamber, no cover between her and it in either direction, nothing to work an angle off except the guardrail and her own feet.

She ran the only calculation that mattered in the two seconds she had to run it: the way back was clear, and the thing between her and understanding what this even was had not turned around, had not registered her at all, was still facing whatever slow patrol it had been walking before she arrived.

She took one step backward, testing the catwalk under her weight before she committed to it, the way she tested everything.

The catwalk creaked.

It turned.

* * *

There was no warning after that. It closed the distance faster than four legs built from repurposed machinery had any right to move, no wind-up, no telegraph, mass covering twenty metres of catwalk in less time than it should have taken her to finish reading the number on a clock. She had time for exactly one thought, wrestle it, not run, because running on an unfamiliar catwalk in the dark against something faster than her was a losing bet on every axis she could name, her footing worse than its, terrain unknown, distance to the ladder longer than distance already closed, and then it was on her.

The first hit caught her flat across the shoulder, full weight, no give in it at all.

Something in her chest should have broken. She felt the impact land, felt her body driven sideways into the pipe run behind her hard enough to knock the air out of her lungs, and braced for the pain that should have followed it, the specific white flare of a shattered collarbone.

It didn’t come. There was pressure, and force, and the after-echo of contact ringing through her skeleton, and underneath all of it, nothing that resembled the injury the hit should have caused. Her arm still moved. Her shoulder still took her weight when she pushed off the pipe to get her feet back under her.

[Mitigation Window: 90s]

The words sat in her vision for exactly as long as it took her to not understand them, and then the thing came again.

She got her forearms up this time, angled, catching the second hit on the frame of her arms rather than her chest, redirecting the force down and sideways the way she’d redirect a throw, and the impact still drove her back a full step, her boots skidding on wet concrete, but it landed different. Smaller. Absorbed rather than eaten whole.

It didn’t stop coming. It didn’t roar or lunge or do anything that looked like rage. It simply kept closing the distance every time she opened it, methodical, patient, the way a machine executes a task it has executed ten thousand times before, and every contact it made cost her something she couldn’t see and could feel accumulating anyway, a pressure building somewhere behind her sternum that had nothing to do with her lungs.

She based low. She’d spent six years learning what her body did instinctively when something heavier tried to move her, weight dropped, feet wide, hips under her rather than behind her, and when the third hit came she took it on a braced forearm and let her whole structure absorb it into the ground instead of into her spine.

That one barely registered.

It didn’t pause to register anything either way. It came again, same rhythm, same total absence of hesitation, and she caught this one on an angle, turning her hip into the strike the way she’d turn into an opponent’s weight instead of fighting it head on, redirecting most of the force out past her rather than through her. Her boots skidded on the wet concrete anyway, one foot going out from under her before she caught it, and the guardrail cracked against her hip hard enough to leave its own separate ache entirely, unrelated to whatever the thing itself was doing to her.

The fifth caught her flat again, a glancing blow off the side of her skull that should have put her on the floor unconscious, and instead put a white ring around the edge of her vision for one full second before it cleared, her balance gone for half a step, recovered on the next. She tasted copper and didn’t have time to work out where from.

She didn’t have a count running. She didn’t need one. Something in her had started tallying the difference between the hits she managed and the hits that landed clean, a pressure building behind her sternum with every clean one that felt less like pain and more like debt, an account somewhere filling toward a number she couldn’t see and had no way to check.

The thing shifted its weight for the next approach, and in the half-second that gave her, she saw the whole rest of the catwalk behind it clearly for the first time, wide open all the way to a second chamber, no way past it, no way around, only back the way she’d come.

She broke right instead of back, putting a pipe run between herself and the thing’s next line of approach, and ran, the paracord unspooling loose against her leg with every stride as she gained on her own trail faster than she’d laid it.

* * *

She didn’t stop at the ladder. She went up it three rungs at a time, one hand grabbing rail and the next grabbing rung, not looking back, not checking her footing the careful way she’d checked it on the way down, because careful was a luxury that belonged to a version of this climb that wasn’t happening.

She came up through the hatch into grey daylight that felt, for one disoriented second, like the wrong planet entirely, and didn’t stop moving until she’d put the fence and forty metres of trees behind her, dropping to a walk only once her lungs told her, flatly, that they were done being asked for anything else.

She stood at the tree line with both hands braced on her knees, breathing hard, and ran her hands over her own shoulder, her own ribs, the side of her head where the fourth hit had landed, checking for the blood or the give of something broken that her body insisted, against everything she’d just felt happen to it, was not actually there.

Nothing. No blood. No obvious break. A dull, general ache spread wide across her whole chest and one side of her skull, the kind of soreness she recognised from a hard practice, not from being hit by something built out of cast iron. She pressed a thumb into the spot on her shoulder that had taken the first, worst hit and found tenderness there, real and present, but nothing like the grinding wrongness of a fracture, nothing that made her breath catch the way a broken bone always made her breath catch, and she had broken enough of her own bones over six years of this sport to know the difference on contact.

She checked the time. Less than two minutes had passed between the thing turning on the catwalk and her clearing the tree line, and somewhere in that window sat five hits that should each, individually, have ended her afternoon in an ambulance, and hadn’t. She checked it again against the stopwatch function she’d started without remembering starting it, some reflex left over from every timed drill Val had ever run, and got the same number back the second time, which meant it was real and not just her sense of time collapsing under the weight of everything that had happened inside it.

The words were still sitting somewhere at the edge of her memory, plain block text with no flourish to them at all, exactly the kind of thing she’d expect from a system that had never once bothered explaining itself to her before today.

Ninety seconds. She didn’t know yet what that number meant, only that it had appeared the same instant the first hit landed and hadn’t reappeared since, which suggested it was a statement about a duration rather than a warning about anything specific, a fact offered once and left for her to work out the rest of on her own.

She recoiled the padlock loosely back through the hasp on her way past the roof hatch, more out of habit than any real intention to hide that she’d been there, and started the walk back toward the fire road with her legs still not entirely trusting the ground under them.

* * *

The bus ride back gave her forty minutes to sit with it, forehead against the cool glass of the window, watching the trees give way to houses give way to the city rebuilding itself in reverse the way it always did on this route. She ran the sequence of it again and again, not because rerunning it produced anything new but because rerunning things was what she did with information that hadn’t finished being information yet.

Four hits. Five, she corrected herself, counting the one off the guardrail that hadn’t really been a hit from the thing at all, more a hit from the environment because of it. Two of the five landed the way a hit was supposed to land, flat, full weight, exactly the kind of contact that should have put her in an emergency room within the hour. Three she’d managed into something smaller, redirected, absorbed, turned into the ordinary bruised ache of a hard practice rather than whatever the other two should have cost her.

She did not have language yet for the difference between those two categories. She only had the fact of it, sitting in her chest like a board she hadn’t finished tracing, and the specific unbearable itch of a fault she could feel the shape of but not yet name.

She thought about the two hits that had landed flat, ran them again frame by frame the way she’d rerun a diode-mode trace that had returned an answer she didn’t trust the first time. Full weight, no redirection, contact that should have translated directly into broken bone and hadn’t. And the three she’d managed, angled, based, absorbed through structure rather than through flesh, that had left almost nothing behind at all. It was the same physics either way, the same mass moving at the same speed. The only variable that had changed between one outcome and the other was what she’d done with her own body in the half-second before contact, which meant, if she was reading it correctly, that whatever was protecting her wasn’t protecting her from the hit itself. It was only changing how much of the hit got through.

That distinction mattered enough that she pulled out her phone right there on the bus and typed it into a note before the shape of the thought could slip, three short lines, no punctuation to spare, the kind of note she left herself when a diagnosis was still forming and she didn’t trust her own memory to hold it steady until she got to a bench. A woman across the aisle glanced at her once, at the dried blood she hadn’t noticed on her own collar, and looked away again just as quickly, the specific incuriosity of a city that had long since stopped asking strangers what had happened to them.

Her jaw ached on the left side where the glancing blow had caught her, and when she pressed a finger to the inside of her cheek there was a small cut there, already closing, the coppery taste from the fight explained and apparently already resolving on its own, which was its own separate fact she filed without examining too closely, because examining it meant thinking about what a fact like that actually implied, and she had exactly zero data yet to build that thought on top of.

* * *

Kelso House was quiet when she got in, Marek’s pasta smell from the kitchen the only sign anyone else was home at all, and she went straight up to the room rather than stopping anywhere along the way, her legs telling her, in a low steady complaint she recognised from every hard practice she’d ever run, that the day’s account had come due whether or not she’d finished processing it.

She sat at the bench without taking her jacket off and pulled a fresh page toward her, the same kind of plain lined paper she used for parts lists and invoice notes, and wrote the date at the top out of the same habit that made her label everything else she worked on.

Then she wrote what she actually knew, in the flattest language she had, the way she’d write up a fault report for a client who needed the facts and nothing else: contact, five instances. No visible injury on four of five. A phrase in her vision reading ninety seconds, timestamped to the first contact and not repeated since. A soreness that felt disproportionately mild against what should have happened to her body in that room, and already fading faster than any soreness had any business fading.

She looked at the four lines for a long moment, the pen still in her hand, and didn’t have a fifth line to add yet, because whatever came next hadn’t happened.

She made herself eat, because eating was the correct next step after any hard practice and she saw no reason this should be different, one of the protein bars from the bottom of her pack, still slightly warm from the pocket she’d carried it in, and then, when that didn’t feel like enough, the rest of what was in the fridge that didn’t need cooking. She ate more than she meant to, standing at the bench rather than sitting, and noticed the appetite without questioning it, filing it next to the four lines already written down, one more fact in a column that was starting to feel less like a coincidence and more like a pattern she hadn’t assembled the shape of yet.

She tried to work on the router board from the drawer and found her hands slower on it than usual, a faint tremor she hadn’t had this morning making the tip of the iron wander half a millimetre off where she meant to put it, twice, close enough to a cold joint on the second attempt that she stopped, set the iron down, and decided the board could wait for a night when her hands were behaving properly.

She capped the pen, left the page open under the bench light where she’d see it first thing, and sat back on the edge of the bed, waiting, without entirely knowing what she was waiting for, only that some part of her, the same part that never trusted a single reading, was certain there was more of this coming and hadn’t arrived yet. The soreness in her jaw and her ribs had, if anything, gotten milder over the last hour rather than worse, which should have been reassuring and instead sat wrong in a way she couldn’t name, a recovery running faster than any recovery she’d ever clocked from a hard practice, faster than made any sense at all.

She was right to wait. It arrived a little after nine.
