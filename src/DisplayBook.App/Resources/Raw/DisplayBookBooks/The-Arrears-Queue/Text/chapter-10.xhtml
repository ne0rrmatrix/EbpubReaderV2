Chapter 10: What It Actually Pays

I found something, Pip said, three evenings after she’d moved into the bay, while she sat at the bench running a bead of solder along a joint that didn’t strictly need redoing, the kind of work she did with her hands when the rest of her mind was busy somewhere else. I don’t know if it’s something or nothing yet. I’d like your opinion.

It had taken her most of the week to get used to the fact that Pip’s presence changed depending on where she was, vivid and constant underground and something quieter, more distant, up here in ordinary rooms, as though whatever carried its voice worked better in some places than others. She’d stopped asking why. It was one more thing on a list of things she didn’t yet have an explanation for, filed next to the missing light behind her ear and the profile page she’d never once thought to open.

“Go ahead,” Nadia said, not looking up from the iron.

There’s a registration profile. Yours. I found it the way I find most things, which is that I apparently already knew where to look before I decided to look for it, which continues to be the single most unsettling part of existing. It has a field on it I don’t understand.

She set the iron down in its stand and pulled up the same registry app she’d used at the fence line six weeks ago, the one that had told her, with three flat sentences, what the pumping station officially was. She’d never once thought to point it at herself.

* * *

Her own profile loaded the way the site’s had loaded, plain and fast and entirely unadorned: name, tier, class, level. Logistical Clerk. F-Tier. Level 1. Exactly what it had always said, exactly what it had said the day some registrar half her lifetime ago had glanced at a screen and moved on to the next student in line, the same three words she’d seen glide past on Mr. Okafor’s monitor without either of them giving it a second look.

The layout was as flat and unadorned as everything else the Guild seemed to produce, black text on white, no icons, no colour, the visual grammar of a government form rather than anything designed to be looked at twice. Below the headline numbers, in a smaller field she’d genuinely never scrolled down far enough to see before, sat a single line she hadn’t known existed. Progress to next threshold: a number, and beside it, in brackets, a second number representing whatever the full threshold actually was. There was no explanation attached to either figure, no tooltip, no plain-language summary the way the site’s classification had come with one. It was a number sitting alone on a page, the way a raw sensor reading sits alone before anyone has bothered to calibrate it against anything.

She read it twice, the way she read anything that told her something and nothing at the same time.

“That’s not zero,” she said, out loud, to the empty bay.

No, Pip agreed. It is very much not zero. I ran a comparison against the timestamp on the profile’s last update, which I also apparently already knew how to find, and the number changed twice. Once roughly six days ago. Once roughly three days after that. Do those dates mean anything to you.

They meant everything. Six days ago was the first entry, the fight on the catwalk, five hits and a night on a bathroom floor. Three days after that was the second, the controlled test, three managed exchanges and a clean disengagement before anything dangerous had a chance to build.

Two visits. Two increases. And in both of them, she had done exactly one thing that mattered: she had made contact with something above her level, held its attention, and walked away without killing it, because killing it had never once been on the table, not against something built out of cast iron with four legs and no interest in her survival either way.

* * *

She sat with the number for a long time, the bay quiet around her, the iron cooling slowly in its stand, running the same handful of facts through every arrangement she could put them in, the way she’d run a fault that refused to resolve into a single clean cause no matter how many angles she tried it from.

If the payout came from damage dealt, the number should have sat at zero, because she had dealt no damage to the thing on the catwalk at all, not once, not in either visit, her entire strategy in both encounters built around surviving contact rather than causing any. She hadn’t so much as landed a strike, hadn’t tried to, had spent every second of contact managing what was coming at her rather than putting anything back the other way. If the payout came from time spent inside the building, the number should have scaled with the length of each visit rather than jumping in two distinct steps that lined up, as far as she could tell, with the two periods where she’d actually been standing in front of the thing itself. She’d spent a good twenty minutes exploring on the second visit before she ever provoked contact at all, mapping the deeper corridor, and none of that exploring had moved the number, only the thirty-odd seconds she’d actually spent in front of it had. If the payout came from proximity alone, from simply being near it, the number should have moved continuously the whole time she was down there rather than jumping only during the parts where contact was actually happening, and it hadn’t moved at all during the long, cold, careful walk in either time.

None of those explanations survived contact with what she actually remembered doing. She crossed each one off in the notebook as she eliminated it, a small clean line through each hypothesis, the same method she used on any board with more than one plausible fault, work outward from what the evidence actually supported rather than what would have made the tidiest story.

What was left, once she’d ruled out everything that didn’t fit, was smaller and stranger than any of them: she had been paid, in both visits, for holding its attention. For being the thing it was oriented toward, engaged with, actively contesting, for however long that contest actually lasted. Not for winning. Not for dealing damage. Not even, particularly, for surviving cleanly, since her second visit, the better-managed one, the one with almost no cost to her body at all, had produced roughly the same jump in the number as her first, the one that had nearly killed her on a kitchen floor.

Duration. Not outcome. She wrote it in the notebook the way she wrote anything she needed to trust later, in plain language, no speculation past what the two data points actually supported: the payout tracks contested attention held, and appears indifferent to how well or badly that contest is managed once it starts. She left a margin note beside it, small, almost an afterthought, reminding herself that two data points was thin evidence for anything, and that the theory deserved a third test before she let herself believe it completely.

That’s an unusual way to structure a reward, Pip offered, once she’d finished writing it. Most systems I’ve read about reward outcomes. Kills, objectives, completed tasks. This one appears to reward the argument itself rather than who wins it, which strikes me as either very clever or very badly designed, and I genuinely cannot tell you which.

“Maybe both,” Nadia said, and underlined the word duration a second time, hard enough that the pen nearly went through the page.

A theory with two data points wasn’t a theory she trusted. It was a pattern, and patterns lied to her more often than they told the truth, usually by leaving out the one variable that actually mattered, and she had learned that lesson too many times on too many boards to skip the step now just because the answer felt good. A board that read fine on two tests and failed on the third had cost her a client’s trust once, early on, before she’d learned to distrust anything that hadn’t survived at least a third look, and she had no intention of repeating that particular mistake on something with considerably higher stakes than a returned repair fee.

* * *

She went back three days later, on a Thursday with no job booked and no practice, and this time the plan wasn’t survival and it wasn’t caution. It was measurement, the same discipline she’d bring to any board she genuinely wanted to understand rather than merely fix, and she treated the whole day beforehand accordingly, eating well ahead of the trip in a way she hadn’t bothered to on either of the first two visits, banking whatever margin she could before she spent any of it.

She packed heavier than either of the first two visits, twice the food, a second headlamp as backup, extra batteries taped in pairs so they wouldn’t rattle, and went down through the roof hatch with a specific goal in mind rather than an open one: find the thing, hold its attention as long as she could safely manage, and get out with a number to compare against the two she already had. She counted the rungs on the way down out of habit, twenty-two, the same as always, a small confirmation that nothing about the building itself had changed even if everything about her purpose there had.

It found her in the same wide chamber as before, patrolling the same unhurried route it had walked both times, its four wrong-jointed legs carrying it along the same worn path in the concrete she could now see clearly, a shallow groove polished smooth by however many years of exactly this same patrol. This time she didn’t wait for it to notice her. She stepped into its path deliberately, closed the distance herself, and when it turned, she held her ground instead of testing the reset the way she had six days ago, plotting her escape route with one part of her attention and holding the rest in reserve for whatever came next.

She managed the exchanges the way she’d trained herself to over two visits and a great many hours of thinking about nothing else, base low, angle everything, absorb what she couldn’t redirect, and let the contact run, her breathing already settling into the same steady rhythm she’d use for a long round on the mat rather than the ragged gasping of the first fight. The first three came exactly the way she expected them to, and she took each one cleanly, redirecting force out and past her rather than through her, her body doing the work almost without her having to think it through step by step any more, technique settling into something closer to reflex than deliberate execution.

Four exchanges. Six. Somewhere around the seventh her forearm caught a glancing edge she hadn’t angled quite right, and she felt it, a real jolt of something sharper than the others, and adjusted her stance a half-degree for the next one rather than letting the mistake compound itself. She lost count somewhere past eight, her whole attention narrowed down to the next half-second of contact and nothing beyond it, the way a hard round of live rolling narrowed everything down to exactly that, the room and the cold and the number in her own head all falling away until there was only the next exchange and the one after it.

She broke off at what felt, by instinct alone, like roughly forty seconds, well short of the number from that very first bracketed message, which still sat somewhere in the back of her mind as a ceiling she didn’t yet trust herself to go anywhere near, and walked the reset the way she’d walked it before, backward, watching, one careful step at a time, until it lost interest and turned away, the same unhurried patrol resuming as though she had never once interrupted it at all.

She checked the profile the moment she cleared the tree line, hands still not quite steady, standing under the same stand of trees where she’d watched the light fail four days into this whole business, and found a number nearly three times the size of either of the first two jumps.

She ran the comparison twice, dividing the new figure against the duration she’d roughly tracked in her head, forty seconds against twenty, forty against thirty, and got a ratio that didn’t match either simple multiple, high enough to suggest the return accelerated with time rather than simply scaling with it, low enough that she didn’t yet trust the arithmetic past a rough shape. Three data points were not enough to fit a proper curve to. She knew that, the same way she knew a single reading off a meter never told the whole story of a board, and she filed the ratio as a working hypothesis rather than a fact, exactly the distinction that had kept her out of more bad conclusions over the years than she could count.

The confirmation should have felt like winning. It felt, instead, like watching a scale settle on a number she already suspected and hadn’t wanted proven.

* * *

The bleed started on the bus, which was new. The first two times it had waited for evening, hours of ordinary calm before anything announced itself. This time an ache bloomed behind her ribs somewhere around the second stop, mild at first, easy to mistake for the ordinary soreness of a hard session, and she sat with her forehead against the cool glass the way she had on the ride back from her very first entry, watching the same trees give way to the same houses give way to the same city rebuilding itself in reverse, and felt the ache deepen with every stop rather than settle the way an ordinary bruise would have.

By the time she reached her own stop it had climbed into something she recognised immediately, the same accumulating pressure from the bathroom floor, arriving faster this time, harder, proportional in a way that made the arithmetic feel less like a theory and more like a threat with a schedule attached to it. She walked the last two blocks faster than she wanted to, aware of her own pulse in a way that had nothing reassuring about it, counting doorways the way she’d counted rungs on a ladder, one landmark at a time, because counting was the only thing she trusted her mind to keep doing properly.

She made it inside her own door before her legs went, which was the one mercy the evening offered her, sliding down the wall just inside the alley entrance rather than making it as far as the bed. She ate everything she’d packed for exactly this, methodically, sitting on the floor of a room that was hers with her back against a bench that was hers, tearing into the wrapper of the second dense bar with hands that had gone unsteady again in a way she recognised now well enough not to fight, and rode it out in under fifteen minutes this time instead of the near-hour the first crash had taken, faster resolution against a larger cost, another data point she filed even through the grey creeping in at the edges of her vision.

The tremor was worse this time in one specific way and better in another, sharper at its peak but shorter, gone almost as suddenly as it had arrived, and she sat with her back against the bench leg long after the worst of it had passed, letting her pulse settle at its own pace rather than rushing to trust it, checking it against two fingers at her wrist the way she’d checked it on a kitchen floor weeks ago, counting until the count held steady twice in a row. The concrete floor was cold under her, the same specific cold she’d come to associate, oddly, with recovery rather than danger, a texture her body had apparently learned to read as the beginning of the end of a crisis rather than the crisis itself.

You’re doing the thing where you refuse to lie down even though lying down would clearly help, Pip said, somewhere in the middle of it, an edge of something that wasn’t quite panic but was close enough to it that Nadia noticed even through everything else. I would like to formally register a complaint about this pattern.

“Noted,” Nadia said, through her teeth, and ate another handful of dates.

I don’t actually know what I would do if this went wrong, Pip added, quieter, once her breathing had started to even out. I want you to know that. Not because I think it will. Because I noticed I didn’t know the answer, and not knowing felt like something I should say out loud instead of just carrying around.

Nadia didn’t answer that one, not because she had nothing to say but because she didn’t yet trust her own voice to say it steadily, and silence, tonight, felt like the more honest option available to her.

* * *

The second half of the problem arrived the way it always arrived, uninvited, sometime the following evening, while she was sitting with the satisfaction of a hypothesis that had survived three separate tests rather than two, cleaning her tools before she put them away for the night, the smell of solder flux still on her hands the way it always was.

She pulled the notebook back a few pages, past the fault report from the first night, to the numbers she’d started keeping since then almost without deciding to: what she ate, roughly, on an ordinary day, and what she’d eaten in the twenty-four hours after each visit, when the appetite that had first alarmed her in the cafeteria kicked in properly. She’d been tracking it the way she tracked anything that might turn out to matter, a habit from the bench applied to her own body without her ever quite framing it that way at the time, entries scrawled in the margins between repair notes because she hadn’t yet decided the eating deserved a page of its own.

The gap between the two columns was not small. It was not, on reflection, even close to small, and looking at three separate days’ worth of it laid out together rather than one at a time made the pattern impossible to read any other way than the way it actually read.

She did the arithmetic the way she did any arithmetic that mattered, slowly, checking each step against the one before it rather than trusting the total until she’d built it twice. An ordinary day, for a person her size doing what she actually did with her days, ran somewhere in the range of two and a half thousand calories, maybe a little more on a hard training day, a number she’d had a rough sense of for years from tracking her own weight against the sport without ever needing to think about it too precisely. The day after either of the first two visits to the pumping station, working backward from what she remembered actually eating, without ever once feeling like she’d eaten too much, ran closer to five, and that was accounting only for what she remembered clearly, which she suspected undercounted the true total by a meaningful margin, given how many of those calories had gone down in a blur on a kitchen floor with her hands shaking too hard to measure anything.

Twice her ordinary intake, at minimum, to process one contested engagement lasting under two minutes. And those two engagements had been, by any reasonable measure, cautious ones, brief ones, sessions deliberately kept short specifically because she hadn’t yet trusted her own body enough to push them any longer.

The third visit broke that pattern cleanly, and she added its numbers to the page with the particular grim satisfaction of a hypothesis confirming itself in exactly the direction she hadn’t wanted it to. Forty seconds of contact instead of twenty or thirty. A crash that had started on the bus rather than waiting for evening, resolved faster but arrived harder, and an appetite that evening that made the first two look almost restrained by comparison, plate after plate at the shop’s small hot plate until Fitch, passing through to lock up, had raised an eyebrow at the sheer volume of it without saying anything.

She totalled the third day separately, then averaged all three against duration, and watched a rough shape emerge from three points that was rougher than she wanted and clear enough that she trusted it anyway: the cost did not simply add up as she stayed longer. It climbed, faster than duration alone accounted for, the way a system draws harder on a supply the deeper it goes into whatever reserve is left, rather than pulling steadily the whole way through.

She sketched a crude curve on the page beneath the numbers, three points and a line drawn freehand through them, and stared at the shape of it for a while, because a curve like that meant something specific and unwelcome: the longer she stayed, the worse the exchange rate got, which meant efficiency wasn’t something she could simply train her way past. It was a wall built into the mechanism itself, and walls built into a mechanism didn’t move for technique, only for resources she didn’t currently have enough of.

You’ve gone very quiet, Pip said.

“I’m doing math,” Nadia said, and kept doing it.

* * *

The number she needed came out somewhere she didn’t like. A single visit, run properly, run long enough to actually matter for the kind of progress the registry app had shown her, was going to cost something in the neighbourhood of five thousand calories to process cleanly, more if she pushed the duration further, which the whole logic of the payout suggested she eventually would need to if she wanted this to become anything more than an interesting fact about a building nobody else knew existed.

Five thousand calories, on top of whatever she was already spending on an ordinary day of school and repair work and a wrestling season that hadn’t even properly started cutting weight yet, a season that was going to start demanding its own separate toll from the exact same body inside of a month. Five thousand calories on a grocery budget that had, for two years running, been built around not wasting a single dollar on anything she didn’t strictly need, a discipline she’d practised so long it had stopped feeling like discipline and started feeling simply like how food worked.

She ran the cost of that in actual money, the way she ran everything eventually, converting calories into groceries into dollars the same way she converted milliamps into a fault. Cheap calories were cheap, rice and oil and the store brand of everything, sardines at a dollar-forty a tin for thirty grams of protein, the same arithmetic she’d run standing in a Safeway two months ago on a very different kind of night. But cheap calories in the volume this required started adding up in a way that stopped being trivial the moment she actually wrote the weekly total down, multiplied out against however many sessions a week she’d need to run for the payout to actually mean anything.

She wrote three different scenarios in three separate columns, cautious, moderate, and the pace the registry app’s threshold number implied she’d actually need if she wanted to reach the next level inside a season that mattered rather than a season that had already ended by the time she got there, and labelled each column the way she’d label a set of test results, so that whoever read the page back later, even if that reader was only ever going to be herself, would know exactly what assumption each number rested on. All three came in over what her current income could absorb without cutting into rent, or parts, or the small margin she’d only just started letting herself think of as safety rather than surplus.

It came to more than she currently had room for. Not impossibly more. Not a number that made the whole project obviously insane. But more than her current margin could absorb without something else giving way to make room for it, and she didn’t yet know what that something else was going to have to be, and every candidate she could think of, school, wrestling, sleep, the trade itself, was a thing she couldn’t actually afford to lose either.

I don’t like the shape of that graph, Pip said, quietly, once she’d finished writing the final number down and sat looking at it. I ran it forward a few sessions on my own, extrapolating from what you just worked out. The cost climbs faster than I think you want it to. I could be wrong. I don’t have much data yet. But I don’t think I’m wrong.

“You’re not wrong,” Nadia said, and didn’t bother pretending otherwise, because pretending had never once fixed a number that refused to balance.

I could try to think of something, Pip offered, a beat later, something careful in the way it said it, as though it had learned already, in barely a week, when a suggestion was welcome and when it wasn’t. I don’t know if I’m useful for this kind of problem yet. I mostly know things. I don’t know if knowing things helps with a problem that’s really just about not having enough money.

“It helps to know exactly how much,” Nadia said. “Guessing is worse.”

That’s a very you way of finding comfort in something, Pip said, and there was something in it that might have been fondness, though she didn’t yet trust herself to identify it correctly through a voice with no face attached to it.

Can I ask what you’re going to do about it, it went on, when she didn’t answer right away. Not because I expect you to have decided already. I’ve noticed you tend to sit with a problem for a while before you commit to anything, and I don’t think that’s a flaw, I think it’s probably the correct approach, I just don’t know yet whether I’m supposed to wait quietly while you do it or whether you’d rather I kept talking.

“Quietly,” Nadia said, and then, because it seemed only fair, added, “For now.”

Understood, Pip said, and, true to its word, said nothing else for a while, though she could tell, in some way she couldn’t have explained to anyone, that it was still there, present in the particular manner of someone sitting quietly in a room with you rather than one who had actually left, a presence rather than an absence, even without a single further word to prove it.

She sat with the three scenarios a while longer, running her eyes down each column the way she’d run a rail with a meter, waiting for a number to reveal itself as wrong the way numbers sometimes did on a second look. None of them revealed anything. They sat exactly where she’d put them, unmoved by however long she stared at them, the way a genuine fault always sat exactly where it was regardless of how much a person wanted it to be somewhere else.

She thought about the placement category still sitting unfilled after six weeks, the twelve hours a week she owed to something that paid her nothing and taught her nothing, hours that could, in a different arrangement of the world, have gone straight into whatever this actually required instead. She thought about Bhandari’s desk, the stack of other people’s files, the flat honest sentence about systems that didn’t talk to each other, and found, turning it over now with a genuinely new problem in front of her, that the sentence applied here too, in a different direction entirely: no form in existence had a box for what she actually needed money for, and no amount of explaining it to anyone would change that. She thought about the wrestling season bearing down on her from one direction and this from another, both of them demanding calories from the same finite body at the same time she was supposed to be cutting weight for one of them entirely. She thought, briefly and without lingering on it, about the fact that six weeks ago none of this had existed at all, that her whole world had been a voucher counting down and a bench she couldn’t always afford to stock, and that the version of her life that had seemed impossibly constrained back then now looked, by comparison, almost simple, a single clean problem instead of the stack of them she was looking at tonight.

Nadia looked at the number for a long time, in a room that was hers now, with a bench that was hers, and a bed that was hers, all of it paid for by exactly the kind of careful, grinding arithmetic that had gotten her this far and had never once, until tonight, run up against a wall it couldn’t solve its way around.

She closed the notebook and set it down on the bench beside the cooling iron, exactly where she’d see it again first thing in the morning.

The numbers did not work. She had checked them twice, the way she checked anything before she trusted it, and they had come out the same both times, sitting on the page in her own plain handwriting, unwilling to move for anything she’d tried against them yet.
