Chapter 23: Undocumented

The reproduction test resolved on day eight, one day ahead of schedule, and it resolved in a way that told her considerably less than she’d hoped it would.

Breath direction hadn’t mattered. She’d run the held-inhale variant on day three and gotten nothing across forty full cycles, and the held-exhale variant again on day five with the same result, no flicker, no meter movement, a clean negative on a variable she’d half expected to be load-bearing. Posture hadn’t mattered either, weight forward and weight back producing identical silence across two more full sessions. The hour hadn’t mattered. What had mattered, when she finally isolated it properly on day eight, sitting in the alcove for the fourth session in a row with nothing else left to vary, was simply the number of cycles.

Twenty-six. Every single time, within a cycle or two either side, regardless of breath count or posture or the hour of the night, the meter spiked at approximately the twenty-sixth repetition and nowhere else.

She’d run the full confirmation four separate times across the last three days of the nine-day window, once with the six-count breath, once with a four-count, once in the early morning before school rather than her usual late-night slot, once seated rather than in the exact posture the guide specified, and every single variation had produced the same result within a margin of one or two cycles either side of twenty-six. The consistency of it was, in its own way, more unsettling than the original spike had been. A single anomaly could be noise. A number that held steady across four independently varied conditions was not noise. It was a pattern, and a pattern demanded an explanation she did not yet have.

She wrote the finding into her notebook in the same flat hand she’d used for every entry before it, no exclamation mark, no underline, and sat with the result afterward the way she sat with any answer that had technically resolved the question and left a considerably larger one sitting in its place.

“It’s not the breath,” she told Pip, once the entry was written and the meter packed away. “It’s not the posture. It’s just repetition. Twenty-six cycles, minimum, before anything happens at all.”

That’s not a small thing to have found, Pip said.

“It’s not nothing,” Nadia agreed. “But it doesn’t tell me why twenty-six. It doesn’t tell me what happens on cycle twenty-seven, or whether twenty-six is a real threshold or just the number my particular body happens to need this month. I’ve confirmed a symptom. I haven’t found the fault.”

Most people would call a confirmed, reproducible result something considerably better than a symptom, Pip said, something dry in it.

“Most people aren’t trying to build a working piece of equipment out of it,” Nadia said. “A symptom tells you something’s wrong, or something’s possible. It doesn’t tell you the mechanism. I know a board is dead when the power light doesn’t come on. That doesn’t tell me whether it’s the fuse, the regulator, or a cracked trace three layers down. Knowing the light doesn’t come on is where the actual work starts, not where it ends.”

So what happens now, Pip asked. You have your reproducible flicker. Twenty-six cycles, reliable, confirmed four separate ways. Where does that number actually take you next.

Nadia considered the question the whole rest of the walk, turning it over against everything she’d learned in the last nine days and everything she still didn’t know underneath it. “I think it takes me back to the beginning,” she said, finally. “Not Phase One. The whole thing. I’ve been so careful not to contaminate one small experiment that I never once looked at the shape of what I was actually trying to build. I know Phase One works now, empirically, on my own data. I don’t know anything about Phase Two through Seventeen except that they exist and that people spend a decade working through them.”

You’re going to read the rest of it, Pip said, not quite a question.

“I’m going to read all of it,” Nadia said. “Properly, this time. No more stopping myself after four sentences.”

* * *

She held to that resolve the whole walk home, running back over the reasoning one more time the way she checked any decision before letting it stand overnight. Not Phase One in isolation, controlled and careful and uncontaminated by anything that came after it, the discipline that had served her honestly for nine days and had finally done its job. The entire seventeen-step recipe, start to finish, the complete published curriculum that every core-classed practitioner in the province had worked through, some of them successfully, most of them not, across eight to fifteen years of disciplined effort that she did not have and had never pretended she had.

She sat at the bench that Sunday with the archive open across three browser tabs and her own notebook beside the keyboard, and read.

The bay held its usual quiet around her, the compressor on the far side of the wall ticking through its cycle, a grey Vancouver light coming in flat and even through the one window, exactly the kind of unremarkable Sunday that had, over the last year, become the setting for most of the decisions that actually mattered in her life. Nobody watching from outside would have seen anything happening at all. A girl at a bench with a laptop and a notebook. It looked, she thought, distantly, exactly like homework, and in the narrowest possible sense it was.

* * *

It took most of the afternoon, longer than she’d expected, not because the material was dense but because it was the opposite, thin in exactly the places she most wanted it to be thick. Phase One she already knew by heart. Phase Two, next in order, asked for a specific posture held twice daily for a period the guide described only as until the sensation from Phase One becomes reliable, an instruction with no number attached to it at all, no threshold, nothing a person could test against except their own subjective sense of whether reliable had arrived yet.

Phase Three, next, introduced breathing patterns matched to a described mental state the guide called stillness without naming a single physiological marker that might indicate a person had actually achieved it. Phase Four, several pages later, asked the reader to fast for the six hours preceding practice, with a footnote noting that some traditions extended this to twelve and that either was considered acceptable depending on lineage.

She stopped at that specific footnote and read it three times over.

Lineage, Pip repeated, when she read the word out loud. That’s an odd word for a recipe with no theory behind it.

“It’s a tell,” Nadia said, low, marking the footnote with a small pencil star in the margin of her notebook. “If the fasting window actually mattered physiologically, every guide would agree on the number. A footnote that shrugs and says either is fine depending on lineage isn’t describing a physical requirement. It’s describing a disagreement nobody ever resolved.”

Phase Five, further still, described a physical sensation the reader was meant to identify and hold onto, a warmth described only as beginning somewhere and never once located with any precision a person could use to check their own progress against. Phase Six returned to something closer to Phase One’s clarity, a specific duration, a specific posture, no ambiguity in the instruction at all, and she noted the shift with the same attention she’d give a datasheet that suddenly tightened its tolerances halfway through a table for no stated reason. Phase Seven introduced a second breath pattern layered on top of the first, and by Phase Eight the guide had stopped bothering to explain anything at all, simply listing steps in sequence the way a recipe listed ingredients, trusting the reader to already understand why each one mattered.

She read all the way through to Phase Twelve before she finally stopped for the evening, her eyes tired, the light in the bay gone properly dark outside the one window, and sat back from the screen with the specific full-body fatigue of a long research session rather than a physical one, the strange tiredness that came from holding a great deal of unstructured information in her head at once and trying to find the shape underneath it.

You’ve been at this for three hours, Pip observed, gentle. I don’t think I’ve ever seen you read anything for three hours straight before. Not even a full repair manual.

“A repair manual tells you what the part is supposed to do,” Nadia said, rubbing at her eyes. “This tells you what to do to the part and never once says why. It’s a completely different kind of reading. I keep waiting for the sentence that explains the mechanism and it never arrives.”

Does that frustrate you, actually, or am I misreading the situation entirely, Pip asked.

“It used to,” Nadia said. “I think I’m past frustrated now. I think I’m just cataloguing it the way I’d catalogue any fault I already know I’m going to have to solve myself.”

She went back to the archive the following afternoon rather than pushing on through the previous evening’s exhaustion, pulling up a second published version of the guide from a different author entirely, a different community, the same seventeen-step shape underneath entirely different specific instructions in half the places she checked. The fasting footnote from the first guide had no equivalent here at all, no mention of fasting whatsoever attached to Phase Four, and she sat with that particular discrepancy for a long moment before reaching for a third source, and then a fourth, and then, eventually, a fifth.

* * *

She spent the next several hours doing what she’d have done with any two datasheets that claimed to describe the same component and disagreed on a specification, pulling every published variant of the seventeen-step guide she could find, five in total, three from what appeared to be established teaching lineages and two from independent forum contributors with decades of their own practice behind them, and laying them side by side in a spreadsheet she built for exactly this purpose, one column per guide, one row per step.

The pattern that emerged was not subtle once she’d actually assembled it properly.

Some steps agreed across every single version, word for word in places, the same posture, the same breath pattern, the same escalating duration, five independent lineages converging on an identical instruction despite having, as far as she could tell from the biographical notes each author included, no contact with each other at all. Phase One was one of these. So was Phase Six. So, notably, was Phase Eleven, a step that asked for absolute silence and absolute stillness for a duration measured in hours rather than minutes, every guide agreeing on the number to within a margin she found genuinely difficult to explain away as coincidence.

Other steps diverged wildly. The fasting window. A specific direction to face during Phase Four that three guides insisted mattered and two didn’t mention at all. A ritual phrase, spoken aloud, that appeared in one lineage’s version of Phase Nine and nowhere else, phrased differently across the sources that included any spoken component whatsoever, no two of them using the same words.

“Convergence means load-bearing,” she said, mostly to herself, working through the spreadsheet with a highlighter she’d found in the bottom of a drawer. “Five people who never talked to each other landing on the same number by accident isn’t plausible. Divergence means it’s not load-bearing, or it’s load-bearing in a way none of them actually understood well enough to describe consistently. Either way, divergence means I can’t trust the specific instruction, only the general shape of what it’s pointing at.”

She worked through every discrepancy methodically once the spreadsheet was fully finished, cross-checking each divergent row against the biographical notes each author had included about their own path, looking for anything that might explain the disagreement other than pure inherited error. One author, a woman who’d apparently built her core in the nineteen-eighties before any of this had ever been formally taught in a classroom, wrote in a voice considerably more tentative than the others, hedging her instructions with phrases like as far as I could tell and this may simply have been coincidence, an honesty about her own uncertainty that none of the later, more polished guides bothered to preserve. Nadia found herself trusting that voice more than any of the others, not because it was more confident but because it was less, a person willing to say she didn’t fully know rather than papering over the gap with false authority.

Another guide, considerably newer, read like it had been assembled by someone who had never actually built a core themselves, compiling instructions secondhand from older sources and smoothing out every hedge and uncertainty into confident, declarative prose that sounded authoritative and explained nothing. She flagged that one as the least trustworthy of the five almost immediately, the same instinct that told her which online repair forums were staffed by people who’d actually opened the case and which were staffed by people repeating what they’d read somewhere else.

You’re grading the sources like witnesses, Pip observed. The exact way you’d grade a witness at the site, or in a hallway.

“That’s exactly what it is,” Nadia said. “Some of these people did the work and are honestly reporting what they don’t understand. Some of them are just repeating what they were told, confidently, because confidence sells better than honesty. I trust the hedging ones more. A person who admits uncertainty is a person who actually looked closely enough to notice there was something to be uncertain about.”

You’re debugging a spec sheet that five different manufacturers wrote independently for the same undocumented part, Pip said, something almost delighted in it. I don’t think anyone has ever done this before. Not like this. Not laid out in a spreadsheet with a highlighter.

“Somebody must have,” Nadia said. “Forty years of practitioners. Somebody must have noticed the guides disagreed with each other.”

Maybe, Pip said. But if they did, they didn’t publish it, and they certainly didn’t build a testable protocol out of it the way you’re about to. Most people follow the one guide they were handed and never once compare it against anyone else’s. Why would they? It’s not a science to them. It’s a tradition. You don’t cross-reference a tradition. You just do what you were told.

“That’s the whole problem, put into one sentence,” Nadia said, sitting back from the screen, rubbing at her eyes again. “A tradition survives by being followed exactly. Nobody in forty years had a reason to test whether following it exactly was actually necessary, because the one time someone deviated and it went wrong, it went wrong permanently, and that’s a lesson that only needs to happen once before everyone downstream decides never to risk it again. The caution isn’t irrational. It’s just never been tested against anyone who could afford to fail safely.”

Until now, Pip said, quiet.

“Until now,” Nadia agreed, and turned back to the spreadsheet, working through the remaining rows one at a time, marking each step convergent or divergent with the same flat, careful hand she used for anything she meant to trust later. By the time she finally reached Phase Seventeen, the very last entry, the one every guide described in almost reverent terms as the moment the structure first held charge entirely on its own, she had eleven separate steps marked convergent, three marked divergent enough to need individual testing, and three more flagged in a third colour entirely, steps that appeared in only one of the five sources and nowhere else, no corroboration at all, nothing but a single author’s word that the step mattered.

She stared at that third category for a long, quiet time before she let herself write down what she actually thought about it.

Single-source instructions, oddly, worried her considerably more than any of the divergent ones, precisely because there was no way yet to tell whether a single source meant a genuine discovery nobody else had happened to make, or simply one author’s private superstition, carried forward into print with the same unearned confidence as everything else in the archive. She flagged all three for testing dead last, after every convergent and divergent step had already been confirmed or discarded, on the reasoning that by the time she reached them she’d have a far better sense of what a real effect actually looked like on her meter, and would be considerably harder to fool by then into mistaking a coincidence for a discovery.

“These three,” she said, tapping the screen where the third colour sat clustered near the bottom of the table, “I think are the most likely to be nothing at all. One person, writing down something that happened to them once, that they never properly tested, and everyone since has just copied forward because nobody wanted to be the one who removed a step and turned out to be wrong.”

And if one of the three turns out to be real, Pip said. If a single, uncorroborated source turns out to have noticed something nobody else ever did.

“Then I’ll have found something nobody else in forty whole years managed to write down properly,” Nadia said. “That would be worth the wait. I’m just not going to let myself assume it’s true until I’ve properly earned the right to believe it.”

* * *

By evening she had the whole spreadsheet marked, seventeen steps sorted into three rough categories she’d built herself without any external framework to borrow from. Convergent steps, agreed across every source, which she was prepared to trust as genuinely necessary until proven otherwise. Divergent steps, disagreed on in ways that suggested nobody actually understood why the step worked, if it worked at all, which she flagged for individual testing exactly the way she’d tested breath count against the meter. And a third category, smaller, three steps that appeared in only one or two of the five sources and nowhere else, which she suspected, without yet being able to prove it, were pure ritual residue, inherited from whoever had first written the guide down and copied forward by everyone since out of caution rather than evidence.

“A third of it is probably superstition,” she said, sitting back from the spreadsheet, the highlighter capped and set down beside the notebook. “Maybe more. Nobody ever tested any of it properly because testing it wrong causes permanent damage, so everybody just keeps every step, forever, because removing a step you can’t verify is safe feels riskier than keeping one that might be doing nothing at all.”

That’s a very reasonable position for them to take, Pip said, careful. For everyone except you, specifically, given everything we already know about how your body actually recovers from a mistake.

Nadia turned that over, running the arithmetic she’d been circling all afternoon without quite letting herself land on it directly. “Eight to fifteen years,” she said. “Following every step exactly as written, never questioning which ones matter, because questioning them is how you end up with permanent damage instead of a working core. That’s the standard path. That’s what every guide in this archive is actually offering.”

She thought, briefly, about the caseworker’s file sitting in a Ministry drawer somewhere, the three green checkmarks that would need renewing every quarter for years yet to come, the tournament eleven days out, the level bracket she was already carrying that no ordinary practitioner’s curriculum had ever been written to accommodate. Eight to fifteen years assumed a person with an ordinary life to spend them in, school and work and growing older along a curve the guides had never had to think twice about because it was the only curve anyone following them had ever lived on.

And you don’t have eight to fifteen years, Pip said.

“I don’t even have eight to fifteen months,” Nadia said. “The site needs a buffer now, not when I’m twenty-four. My body’s already doing something the standard curve never accounted for. Every guide in this archive assumes a person who levels the ordinary way, slowly, over decades, with a body that isn’t running an Arrears engine underneath everything else it’s trying to do. I’m not that person. Following their timeline exactly as written isn’t actually the safe option for me. It’s just the option that looks safe because it’s the one everyone else takes.”

* * *

She sat with the decision a good while longer before she finally let herself say any of it out loud, turning it the way she turned any conclusion she was about to commit real resources to, checking it one final time for the kind of wishful thinking that could make a bad plan look sound simply because she wanted it to be sound.

“I can’t follow this recipe blind,” she said, finally. “Not because I don’t respect what forty years of practice built. I do. The convergent steps are real, I believe that completely, five independent lineages don’t agree by accident. But I can’t spend a decade keeping every divergent step and every piece of ritual residue just because nobody’s ever had a safe way to test which ones actually matter.”

And you do have a safe way, entirely unlike anyone else who has ever seriously tried this, Pip said, quiet.

“I’m the only person alive who does,” Nadia said. “A botched attempt costs a normal practitioner permanent damage. It costs me calories and a bad week. I can test a step, get it wrong, and recover from getting it wrong in a way nobody else attempting this has ever been able to afford. That’s not talent. That’s not a gift. It’s just an accident of how my body happens to work, and it means I can run an experiment nobody funded institution would ever risk running on a person, because I’m the one person alive who can risk it on myself, over and over, until I actually understand what’s happening instead of just trusting that it does.”

So the model, Pip said. Properly, now, and all seventeen full steps of it, not just the one variable at a time you were already running before tonight.

“The model,” Nadia agreed, quiet. “Every convergent step, I test to confirm it’s doing what I think it’s doing. Every divergent step, I isolate and test individually the way I tested breath count, and I keep only what actually moves the meter. Every step that shows up in one guide and nowhere else, I test last, and I go in assuming it’s superstition until it proves me wrong.”

And if a convergent step turns out not to matter after all this, Pip asked. Five sources agreeing doesn’t guarantee they’re right. It only guarantees they copied from a common origin, or arrived at the same wrong answer independently, which happens more often than people like to admit.

Nadia considered that carefully, and found she didn’t have a clean answer ready for it, which she took as a sign the question was worth actually sitting with rather than dismissing out of hand. “Then I test those ones too, eventually,” she said. “Just last, and more carefully, because the cost of being wrong about something everyone agrees on is probably higher than the cost of being wrong about something only one person ever wrote down. I’m not going to assume convergence means correct forever. I’m just going to trust it enough to start somewhere sensible.”

That seems fair, Pip said. I don’t think you’re going to find that everyone was simply wrong about the fundamentals, since forty years of agreement rarely collapses entirely. But I appreciate that you’re not ruling it out either, not even a little.

“Nothing’s ruled out until I’ve tested it,” Nadia said. “That’s the whole point of doing this the way I’m doing it instead of the way everyone else has always done it.”

* * *

She thought, slowly packing the notebooks away for the night, about what R1 had actually been in the whole chain of reasoning that had brought her here, the plain, unglamorous fact underneath all of it. The guides had never been built for a body running an Arrears engine. She had followed Phase One exactly as written, the same as anyone else, and it had behaved wrong from the very first cycle, twenty-six repetitions instead of whatever ordinary threshold an ordinary practitioner’s body apparently needed, a discrepancy nobody’s guide had a category for because nobody who’d ever written one had a body like hers to test it against.

That mismatch was, in the end, the entire reason any of this existed at all. Not ambition. Not a wish to be exceptional. Simply a standard path that didn’t fit, encountered by a person with no choice but to work out why, because the alternative was following instructions that had never once been calibrated to a body like hers and trusting that would somehow still turn out fine.

* * *

She sat with the reasoning a while longer before letting it settle into something she trusted enough to build on, running the whole chain once more from the beginning the way she ran anything she was about to commit real time to. R1 had started it, a body that didn’t match the standard curve, behaving wrong from the very first test. R4 was why she was allowed to keep going, the one fact that made this entire approach survivable rather than reckless, a failure rate that cost her days instead of costing her permanently. And underneath both of those, the actual decision, made now, on the page, with her eyes open: she was not going to spend a decade trusting a document nobody had ever verified, when she had a way to verify it herself.

You’ve gone quiet again, Pip said, gently, watching her from wherever it watched her from at this hour of a bay gone dark around them both.

“I’m just making sure I actually believe all of it,” Nadia said. “Not just the parts that are convenient. All of it, including the part where this is going to cost me time I don’t really have to spare, on top of everything else already eating my week.”

Do you believe it, actually, Pip asked, quiet.

“Yes,” Nadia said firmly, and there was nothing tentative in the word at all. “I believe it because I checked it three separate ways before I let myself say it out loud, and it held up every single time. That’s the only kind of belief I’ve ever trusted, and I don’t see a good reason to start trusting a different kind now.”

She rebuilt her notebook properly that night, retiring the loose, exploratory pages she’d filled since October and starting a new section with a title at the top she wrote carefully, the way she wrote anything she meant to hold herself to.

The Model. Seventeen steps, tested individually. Keep what works. Discard what doesn’t. No exceptions for tradition.

Beneath it she ruled out a table, seventeen rows, three columns, step number, convergence status, test result pending, the same disciplined structure she’d have built for any board with seventeen suspect components and a limited budget to test them all. It was not a fast plan. Even compressed, even run as efficiently as she could design it, it was going to cost her months rather than years, and months were still a currency she had precious little of to spend, stacked on top of a wrestling season and a ledger she was already managing carefully and a body that hadn’t finished paying off tonight’s ordinary session before she’d even started thinking about tomorrow’s.

It’s still going to cost you something quite real, Pip said, watching her carefully rule the final line of the table straight. Building the model instead of just following the recipe. I don’t think either of us should pretend it won’t.

“I know,” Nadia said. “I’m not pretending any of it is free. I’m just betting it’s cheaper than eight full years, and I’m the only person who gets to make that particular bet honestly.”

She closed the notebook, the table sitting empty and ready beneath its careful heading, seventeen rows waiting for data she hadn’t collected yet, and set it on the shelf beside the one that still, most nights, read no result, and beside the one that tracked a ledger’s balance in the same careful black ink, three separate accounts of the same stubborn refusal to accept an answer she hadn’t verified herself.

She stood at the shelf a moment longer than she strictly needed to, looking at the three of them lined up together in the near dark, three different problems she was solving with exactly the same method, because it was the only method she actually trusted, and the only one that had ever once let her down gently enough to keep using it. A body that didn’t clear on schedule. A form that didn’t reflect what had actually happened. A recipe that refused to explain itself. Three different failures of documentation, and one girl at a bench who had decided, somewhere over the last several months without ever quite announcing it to herself, that if nobody else was going to write the manual properly, she would simply have to write her own.

She finally turned off the bench light and went to bed, the empty table waiting patiently on the shelf for whatever the next several months were about to hand it, seventeen rows, and no exceptions for tradition, and no exceptions for anyone who told her to simply trust that the recipe already knew best.
