Chapter 35: Comparing Notes

Nine days into the sixteen she actually had, and the letter’s clock had already started to feel less like an emergency and more like ordinary weather, the kind of thing she checked without needing to be afraid of it fresh every single morning.

She’d spent most of the week’s free hours on the activity problem rather than on learning how to waste an evening the way Pip had suggested, because a half-solved list bothered her considerably more than an unfamiliar quiet ever had. Three community programmes had come back with waitlists longer than her window. A fourth wanted a parent or guardian’s signature on the intake form before it would even schedule an assessment, a requirement she’d read twice with the same flat, tired recognition she’d given the appeal process a week earlier. She’d crossed it off the list without commentary and moved to the next line, because a fault that couldn’t be worked around got crossed off, not mourned, and mourning it wouldn’t have bought her a single one of the sixteen days back.

Lunch ran the way it had run all week, Hana narrating something about the chemistry group, Tomasz picking at a plate that had finally gone back to a normal size now that his own weigh-in was behind him, and Nadia eating at her usual pace, unhurried, no scale left to answer to, half her attention still on the notebook page from three nights ago that hadn’t finished its list of activities she could plausibly get pre-approved inside a week.

It was Tomasz who started it, the way most things at this table started, without any intention behind it at all.

* * *

“You know what I still think about,” he said, to the table generally, around a mouthful of rice, “is that fire thing. Back before break. I still don’t actually understand what happened.”

“What fire thing,” Hana asked, delighted immediately, the way she was delighted by any story she hadn’t heard yet.

“Equipment room,” Tomasz said. “Outlet behind the space heater, arcing, real close to actually catching. Nadia smelled it before anyone else even knew something was wrong, went straight for the breaker panel, killed the exact right one without checking the label, diagnosed the whole thing by the time the custodian showed up with an actual tester and confirmed she’d called it perfectly.” He shook his head, still visibly impressed months later. “I was standing right there. I watched the whole thing. It wasn’t normal-fast. It was fast like she already knew.”

Nadia kept eating, carefully, the same unhurried pace, and said nothing, because saying nothing had always cost her less than saying something in a room like this one.

“I remember that,” said a boy two seats down whom Nadia only vaguely recognised from the wrestling roster, leaning in now that a story was clearly forming. “Wasn’t there smoke? I heard there was smoke.”

“There wasn’t smoke yet,” Tomasz said, already warming to the retelling the way people always warmed to a story that had gotten better with each pass. “That’s the part that gets me. There wasn’t smoke. There wasn’t a spark you could actually see. She smelled something and just went. By the time the rest of us figured out what was even happening, she’d already killed the power to half the equipment room.”

“Sounds made up,” the boy said, not unkindly, grinning.

“I was there,” Tomasz said again, mild, unbothered, the specific patient insistence of someone repeating a true thing to an audience that hadn’t decided yet whether to believe it. “Ask the custodian. He said it himself. Another fifteen minutes and it would’ve been a real fire.”

The skepticism actually helps you, Pip observed, quiet, once the boy two seats down had shrugged and let the point go. A story people half doubt travels slower than one they believe outright. I don’t think that’s comfort exactly. I just wanted you to have the number while you sat with the rest of it.

“Noted,” Nadia said, under her breath, and kept her eyes fixed on her own plate, the specific discipline of not looking too interested in a conversation that was, technically, entirely about her.

* * *

“Wait,” Hana said, sitting up slightly straighter, the specific quickened attention she gave any detail that connected to a story she already half had. “That’s so weird, because my cousin was at that team dinner in November, the one at my place, and one of the guys on your team asked Nadia straight out if she’d actually fixed the wrestling room scale, like the official one, the one maintenance had supposedly looked at twice.”

“I remember that,” Tomasz said, pointing his fork at her. “That’s real too. Val told the whole team about it back in September, like a joke, maintenance takes two visits and some new girl fixes it in four minutes on her first week.”

“That was such a weird night, actually,” Hana went on, warming to her own memory of it now, the specific pleasure she took in any story with herself somewhere near the middle of it. “My cousin kept bringing it up all through dessert, like it was this whole mystery. Somebody said maintenance had a work order open on that scale for three weeks and it just closed itself out of nowhere the same week Nadia joined the team. Somebody else said no, she actually did it, she watched her do it, four minutes flat, didn’t even open the manual.” She shrugged, cheerful, entirely unbothered by her own retelling. “I didn’t think much of it at the time. Free country, some people are just good with tools.”

“So that’s two things,” Hana said, delighted rather than suspicious, the way a person got delighted assembling a coincidence rather than a case. “The scale and the fire. That’s actually kind of a lot.”

That’s three, if you count the register, Pip said, quiet, careful, present in a way that made Nadia’s pulse pick up half a beat before she’d consciously registered why. Pearce told half the shop about that one. If anyone at this table has ever bought a fuse from him, they’ve probably heard it.

“Nobody here shops at Pearce’s,” Nadia said, under her breath, barely moving her lips, and hated, immediately, how much of a tell the whisper itself probably was.

* * *

“You’re just handy,” Tomasz said, shrugging, entirely unbothered, reaching for his water. “Some people are handy. My uncle can fix any small engine on earth and can’t explain how he knows what’s wrong with it either. He just knows. I don’t think it’s a mystery. I think it’s just a thing some people have.”

“Sure,” Hana said, already halfway back to her own thread, the specific quicksilver attention span that moved on from any topic the instant a better one presented itself. “Still funny though. Nadia’s basically a walking warranty. Anything breaks near her, it’s fixed within the hour.”

“I’ll take that as a compliment,” Nadia said, and heard how flat her own voice came out, and made herself smile at the end of the sentence, a half-second late, the way a person smiled when the joke had actually landed somewhere it hurt.

* * *

Priya hadn’t said anything yet, and Nadia felt the absence of it the way she felt any silence that mattered, a held breath sitting a few centimetres to her left, and when Priya finally spoke it came quieter than either Tomasz or Hana had managed, pitched for the table rather than the whole cafeteria.

“It’s not just fixing things, though,” Priya said, turning her fork over once, thinking out loud rather than accusing anyone of anything. “It’s that she’s always the one standing closest when it happens. The fire. The scale. Probably other stuff nobody’s bothered mentioning because it wasn’t dramatic enough to turn into a story.” She looked up, and her eyes found Nadia’s for a moment, entirely without malice, entirely without suspicion of anything real, just the same careful cataloguing attention she’d turned on Nadia’s plate a day earlier. “You’re either the unluckiest person I know or the luckiest, and I can’t decide which, because you’re always right there when something needs fixing and it always somehow gets fixed.”

“There was that thing with the register at Pearce’s too,” Tomasz added, unhelpfully, entirely without malice of his own. “I only heard about it secondhand, some guy who buys parts there. Something about a coin mechanism nobody could figure out that she fixed before she was even officially hired.”

“See, that’s four now,” Hana said, counting on her fingers, delighted by the tally the way she was delighted by any list. “Scale, fire, register, and whatever else nobody’s mentioned. You should charge admission.”

Priya didn’t laugh along with that one. She kept her eyes on Nadia a beat longer than the joke required, and something in the quality of the look shifted, not suspicion exactly, closer to the particular attention she’d given the empty seat and the too-full tray a day earlier, a person quietly filing a new piece next to several older ones she’d apparently been keeping without announcing it.

“I’m not saying it’s weird,” Priya said, gentler now, the joke draining out of her voice even while Tomasz and Hana kept theirs light. “I’m saying it’s a pattern, and I notice patterns. That’s all. You don’t have to explain it to me.”

* * *

Nadia sat with that a moment longer than she should have, fork resting against the edge of her plate, running the same fast, silent arithmetic she ran on any fault, except this time the fault was her own exposure and the diagnostic had nowhere safe to land.

Four, Pip said, quiet, careful, and she could hear, underneath the single flat syllable, an urgency it was working hard to keep out of its own voice. Scale, fire, register, and whatever Priya’s been keeping on her own private list. I don’t think any one of these four people has enough to actually see the shape of it. I think this table, right now, is the closest anyone has ever come to laying enough of it side by side to start.

“I know,” Nadia said, so quietly it barely disturbed the air in front of her lips, and reached for her water, needing something ordinary to do with her hands while the rest of her worked through exactly how much danger a single lunch table full of people who liked her had just, without meaning to, put her in.

“I notice things too,” she said, finally, choosing the sentence the way she chose anything load-bearing, true enough to survive the room, small enough not to open a door she couldn’t close again. “I grew up around broken stuff. You get fast at spotting it. Doesn’t mean anything’s actually strange about it.”

“Never said strange,” Priya said, easily, and reached for her water, the moment already dissolving the way most moments at this table dissolved, no weight attached to it that anyone but Nadia could feel. “I said lucky. There’s a difference.”

“There is,” Nadia agreed, and let her shoulders come down half a centimetre, the tension she hadn’t noticed carrying releasing slowly enough that nobody at the table clocked it going.

* * *

“Anyway,” Hana said, already onto the chemistry group again, some new logistical wrinkle about the room booking, and the conversation moved on the way conversations at this table always moved on, three separate small stories laid briefly side by side and then folded back into the ordinary noise of a Thursday lunch, nobody at the table carrying anything heavier out of it than a mildly funny observation about a friend who happened to be good with her hands.

Nadia ate the rest of her plate in the same careful, unhurried rhythm, and felt, underneath the calm she was performing, the specific cold clarity of having just watched three unrelated witnesses lay their pieces down next to each other without a single one of them understanding what the completed shape would have looked like if anyone had actually bothered to look at it properly.

That was close, Pip said, once the bell had rung and the table had started breaking apart around them.

“I know,” Nadia said, low. “I built every single piece of that myself. One fault at a time. I never once thought about what it would look like once enough of them ended up sitting on the same table.”

* * *

She walked to her next class slower than usual, letting the corridor’s ordinary noise wash past her while she ran the actual tally, properly, the way she should have run it months ago instead of letting each piece file itself away the moment it happened. Marek, at Kelso House, watching her swap a bearing on a kitchen ring nobody had trusted in months, saying she should charge for that and never mentioning it again. The girl down the hall who’d asked how she’d known, and asked again a week later for a second fix Nadia had turned down because the unit itself was actually dead. Pearce, and the register, and the shop full of regulars who’d apparently heard the story secondhand and passed it along without ever needing the truth to be interesting to keep repeating it. The scale. The ankle she’d packed ice around at a joint practice before either coach had reached the girl, and the rival team’s coach asking, plainly, how she’d known to check for a break first. The equipment room. Tomasz, and the custodian, and Val, watching her with the same particular attention each time, filing something in a private log she hadn’t known existed until it had finally, catastrophically, come due in an equipment room of an entirely different kind.

She thought too, walking, about the ones that hadn’t made a story at all, the unnoticed kind, the youth event in the autumn where nothing had happened because she’d made sure nothing happened, no witness, no thanks, no rumour, a whole evening of careful invisible positioning that had cost her a session’s worth of Arrears and calories and existed nowhere except in her own memory and Pip’s. Those didn’t belong on the same tally. They were the ones that had never once threatened her, precisely because nobody had ever known enough to compare notes about them, and she found, walking, a strange cold comfort in the distinction, proof that the compulsion itself wasn’t automatically a liability, only the witnessed half of it, the half that left a story behind for someone else to retell at a party months later.

That’s seven, by my count, Pip said, once she’d run the whole list out loud, quiet, half under her breath, between one class and the next. Not counting today. Not counting whatever Priya’s actually been holding back and didn’t say all the way out loud.

“Seven,” Nadia repeated, slow, turning the number over the way she turned over any total that mattered. “Seven separate people, none of whom talk to each other regularly enough to ever actually compare all seven at once. That’s the only reason I’m not already a story with an ending instead of a story with loose pieces.”

I don’t think that reason holds forever, Pip said, gently. People change tables. People end up at the same party, or the same shop, or the same coach’s clipboard, the way Hana’s cousin ended up hearing about the scale from someone on your own team. The world’s smaller than the number of people in it makes it feel, and it keeps getting smaller every month you stay in it.

“I know,” Nadia said again, quietly, and this time the two words carried something closer to dread than agreement.

* * *

The rest of the afternoon passed in the specific low hum of a person doing ordinary things while a much larger problem ran quietly underneath all of them, and she was three minutes into last period when the projector at the front of the room gave a sharp electrical stutter, the image flickering, then dying entirely, leaving the teacher standing in front of a blank white rectangle with the specific weary patience of someone who had watched this exact machine fail before.

Nadia felt the pull before she’d consciously registered it, the old familiar itch, a fault in her field of view asking to be looked at, her hand already halfway to raising itself before she caught it and put it back down in her lap.

You want to go look at that, Pip said, careful, neutral, offering the observation rather than a judgment.

“I do,” Nadia admitted, low, watching the teacher jiggle a cable with the specific ineffective motion of someone who didn’t actually know what he was checking for. “It’s probably the lamp driver. It’s an easy fix. I could have it running again in under two minutes.”

You could, Pip agreed. Is that the plan.

She sat with the question longer than she expected to need to, the itch sitting under her sternum the whole time, insistent, entirely uninterested in the conversation she’d just survived two class periods ago. “No,” she said, finally, and made herself look back down at her own notebook instead of at the dead projector. “Not today. Today I’m going to sit here and watch someone else’s technology stay broken, and I’m going to let it bother me the whole time, because bothering me is considerably cheaper than becoming the eighth story on that list.”

That’s new, Pip said, quiet, something that might have been approval sitting underneath the single flat word. I don’t think I’ve ever heard you choose that before.

“I’ve never had a reason to before today,” Nadia said. “Turns out watching four people build half a case against me over lunch is a pretty effective teacher.”

The teacher eventually gave up and switched to writing on the whiteboard instead, the lesson continuing without its slides, and Nadia copied notes from a board instead of a screen for the rest of the period, the small unfixed fault sitting in the corner of her attention the entire time, exactly as loud as she’d expected it to be, exactly as hard to ignore, and she ignored it anyway, all the way to the bell.

Twice more before the period ended she caught her own hand drifting toward the aisle, the reflex arriving faster than the reasoning that was supposed to stop it, and both times she folded her fingers back around her pen instead and kept writing, the small physical correction costing her more concentration than the actual notes did. By the time the bell rang her jaw ached faintly from how long she’d been holding it, an ordinary tension she hadn’t noticed building until the release of the bell let her feel it all at once.

You made it the whole period, Pip said, as she packed her bag.

“Barely,” Nadia said. “I don’t think I want to find out what it costs me to do that every single period, every single day, for the rest of the year. That’s not a supply I trust myself to have.”

* * *

She caught up with Priya at the end of the hallway before either of them reached the buses, matching pace without quite deciding to, and walked half a block in silence before she trusted herself to say anything at all.

“For what it’s worth,” Nadia said, “the pattern isn’t what you think it is.”

“I don’t think it’s anything,” Priya said, easy, unbothered, exactly the same tone she’d used at lunch. “I just notice it. Noticing isn’t the same as thinking I know what it means.”

“I know,” Nadia said. “I just wanted to say it out loud once, somewhere that wasn’t a lunch table with an audience.”

Priya considered that for a moment, walking, rain starting again in its familiar fine mist. “Okay,” she said. “Said and heard. I’m not going to bring it up again unless you do.”

“Thank you,” Nadia said, and meant it, the same uncomfortable gratitude she seemed to keep finding in this particular friendship, offered without a single string attached to it, the way it always seemed to arrive from this direction and no other.

“Don’t thank me,” Priya said, mild. “I just like you. Liking someone doesn’t come with a spreadsheet attached to it. You should try it sometime, from the other direction.”

They reached the bus loop and split off toward different routes, Priya lifting a hand in a small, unbothered wave before disappearing into the crowd around her own bus, and Nadia stood at her stop a moment longer than she needed to, watching the ordinary chaos of the loop, buses idling, kids shouting across the pavement at friends they’d see again in fifteen minutes, none of it carrying any weight at all for anyone standing in it except her.

* * *

The walk from her own stop to the bay ran the same route it always ran, and she let herself think properly, finally, about the shape of the afternoon rather than just managing her way through it one conversation at a time.

“I can’t stop being the person who notices faults,” she said, out loud, once the street had emptied out enough that talking to Pip in the open felt safe again. “That’s not actually a lever I have. I tried sitting on my hands for one projector and it took everything I had just to manage forty minutes of it.”

I noticed, Pip said. You did it anyway. I think that matters more than whether it felt easy.

“It bought me exactly one afternoon,” Nadia said. “One projector, left broken, out of however many faults I’ll walk past between now and June. I don’t actually think restraint is a strategy I can run at scale. I think it’s a rationing plan for something I don’t have an infinite supply of, and I’ve never once been good at rationing something I wanted this badly to spend.”

What’s the actual plan, then, Pip asked. If restraint alone doesn’t hold.

She turned the question over for the rest of the block, the rain steady now, her shoes soaking through at the seam the way they always did in weather like this, and didn’t have a clean answer by the time the shop came into view, only the beginning of one.

* * *

“I think the plan is I stop being surprised,” she said, finally, standing outside the shop’s service door, keys already out. “I’ve been treating every one of these like an isolated incident, cleaned up and filed away the moment it happened, and I never once sat down and counted them the way I counted anything else that mattered. Seven is a number. Numbers have trend lines. I should have been tracking this one as carefully as I track everything else, and I wasn’t, because it never felt like the kind of thing that needed a spreadsheet.”

It does now, Pip said, quiet.

“It does now,” Nadia agreed, and let herself into the bay, the day’s whole slow accumulation of dread finally settling into something closer to a task.

* * *

She sat at the bench with the notebook open to a fresh page before she’d even taken her wet shoes off, and wrote the heading at the top the same way she wrote the heading on any list that mattered: plain, functional, no decoration.

Witnesses.

Marek went down first, then the girl from the hallway whose name she’d never actually learned, then Pearce, then the rival coach whose name she’d also never learned, then Val, then the custodian, then Tomasz, then Hana, then Priya, each entry followed by a single line noting what they’d seen and how likely they were to ever cross paths with anyone else on the list. Nine names. Nine small pieces of a shape none of them could see whole.

“Nine,” she said, out loud, once the list was finished, sitting back to look at the full page the way she looked at any diagnostic once she’d actually run it properly instead of guessing at the result. Nine names, nine dates roughly remembered, and underneath each one a small honest note about exactly how much that person had actually seen, written the same way she’d have logged a fault code, no adjectives, no excuses, just the plain shape of what was true.

I count nine too, Pip said. For what it’s worth, having the actual number in front of you looks worse than the vague dread did. I want to say that plainly, in case it helps you brace for it rather than being surprised twice in one day.

“It helps,” Nadia said. “I’d rather have the ugly true number than the comfortable guess. You know that about me by now.”

* * *

She drew a line down the middle of the page and started a second column, slower this time, thinking properly rather than just cataloguing. Which of the nine talked to which others. Val and the rival coach, both on the same regional wrestling circuit, likely to cross paths at any tournament for the rest of the season even with her own name off the roster. Pearce and Bhandari, both handling paperwork with her name on it, a connection thin enough it might never fire and real enough that she couldn’t rule it out. Tomasz, Hana, and Priya, already proven today to sit at the same table and talk without meaning to. She drew careful lines between the names that could plausibly meet, and watched the page fill up with more connections than she’d expected once she actually forced herself to draw them all the way out instead of leaving them as separate, comfortable islands. Even the unlikely pairs earned a line once she considered them properly: Marek had moved out of Kelso House in the fall, but the house itself was small enough that whoever had replaced him might easily have heard the kitchen-ring story secondhand from someone still living there. The girl from the hallway had never given her name, but a school this size ran on exactly the kind of casual, half-remembered gossip that could resurface a year later attached to a face nobody quite placed.

“Nothing on this page is dangerous by itself,” she said, tracing one of the thinner lines with the tip of her pencil, slow, deliberate, the way she traced a crack to find where it actually started. “It’s only dangerous stacked. One story is a compliment. Two is a coincidence. I don’t actually know what nine becomes, because nobody’s ever handed nine of them to the same person at once, and I’d like to keep it that way for as long as I possibly can.”

She sat back from the page and studied the finished web of it, nine names and considerably more connecting lines than she’d have guessed before actually forcing herself to draw every single one out instead of stopping at the obvious pairs. “It’s more connected than I wanted it to be,” she said, quiet.

I know, Pip said. I think that’s exactly why the exercise was worth doing tonight instead of putting it off. You can’t manage a risk you haven’t actually mapped. You taught me that, technically, the first week you had me running diagnostics on the scooter instead of guessing at what was wrong with it.

“I don’t remember teaching you that on purpose,” Nadia said, setting the pencil down between the two columns.

You didn’t, Pip said. You just did it every single time, until I noticed the pattern and started doing it back to you. That’s most of how I’ve learned anything from you, if I’m honest. Not the words. The habits underneath them.

“That’s a strange thing to owe someone,” Nadia said, “and not even know you owed it until they said so.”

Most of the good things people learn from each other work that way, Pip said. I don’t think either of us noticed this one happening until tonight.

* * *

She closed the notebook eventually, the page full, nine names and a web of thin pencil lines connecting more of them than she liked, and sat with the closed cover a moment longer, one hand flat on top of it the way she’d have rested a hand on a scooter frame after a long diagnostic, checking for a vibration that shouldn’t be there anymore.

“I can’t undo any of the nine,” she said. “I wouldn’t, even if I could. Every one of them was a fault I couldn’t walk past, and I’d make the same call again tonight if the exact same thing happened in front of me tomorrow.”

I believe that, Pip said. I don’t think it’s a flaw, for what that’s worth. I think it might be the best thing about you, sitting right next to the thing that’s eventually going to cost you the most.

“That’s a strange sentence to hear about yourself,” Nadia said. “The best thing and the most expensive thing, both true at the same time, both pointing at the exact same habit.”

Most of the important sentences about a person work that way, from what I’ve read, Pip said. I don’t think you’re unusual in that specific respect. I think you’re unusual in how honestly you’re willing to look at both halves of it at once instead of only keeping the half that feels good.

“Both can be true,” Nadia said, and pushed the notebook to the far side of the table, where it would sit until morning, nine names and a web of pencil lines waiting for whatever the next one turned out to be, and got up to finally take off her wet shoes, the evening’s last small ordinary task waiting for her exactly where she’d left it.
