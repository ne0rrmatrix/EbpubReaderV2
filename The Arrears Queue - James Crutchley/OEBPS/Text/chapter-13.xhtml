Chapter 13: Forty and Three Hundred

By the fourth cycle the whole thing had stopped feeling like an event and started feeling like a shift, in the same flat sense that a Tuesday afternoon at Pearce’s was a shift, or a Thursday practice was a shift, a fixed block of her week with a known shape to it that she no longer had to think her way through from first principles each time she approached it. Even the fear had settled into something more like respect than terror, present without ever quite tipping over into anything that slowed her down.

Two weeks between visits, banked carefully around the wrestling calendar the way she’d worked out in October, a surplus day or two after each weigh-in, a session timed for whenever that surplus had actually accumulated into something safe to spend. She’d run it four full times now since the rhythm settled, and four clean cycles was enough, by her own standard, to stop calling it a hypothesis and start calling it a method she could actually trust going forward.

The rest of her week had settled into its own version of the same rhythm around it. School in the mornings, unremarkable enough that whole days passed without leaving much of a mark on her at all, Priya still talking through most of second period about whatever the current subject was, Hana still stating plans rather than proposing them, the group having settled around her the way a room settles once the furniture finally stops moving. Pearce’s on Tuesday and Thursday afternoons, four hours each, the register humming along without a single miscount since she’d fixed it, the shelves a little better organised each week than the week before in ways she suspected nobody but her had actually noticed.

Wrestling three full afternoons a week, the cut running its own careful arithmetic underneath everything else, water and protein and the specific discipline of a body she’d learned to manage the way she managed a circuit under load, meets now close enough on the calendar that Val had started running the roster through actual live matches rather than pure drilling. Repair jobs filled whatever hours remained, fewer of them now than in September, the twelve placement hours having eaten exactly the margin she’d predicted they would, but enough still to keep the rent paid and the tin in the drawer from ever running properly empty, Duarte and Szabo and a handful of newer names cycling through the parts board with the same steady demand there had always been.

It should have felt like less, all of it stacked together, twelve hours gone to a job that used none of her actual skill, a cut eating calories she needed elsewhere, a site that could only be visited once a fortnight instead of the weekly rhythm she’d once hoped for. It felt, instead, like a life that had finally stopped rearranging itself every single week, and she hadn’t realised how much she’d wanted that particular kind of stillness until she had it. Even the bay felt more settled now than it had in October, the bench worn into its own shape by months of use, tools finding their homes in the totes without her having to think about where each one belonged.

* * *

The fire road had a groove in it now, not literal, but close, a route her feet knew well enough that she rarely thought about the turns any more, past the gate with the slack chain, along the tree line, to the low concrete shape sitting exactly where it always sat. The season had turned properly since her first visit, the trees along the road bare now rather than merely thinning, the gravel underfoot gone hard with an early frost some mornings, the sky already dim by the time she reached the tree line most days, and she’d started dressing for the walk itself the way she dressed for anything predictable, layers she could shed once her body warmed to the pace.

She still carefully counted the rungs going down each time. Twenty-two, every single time, a number that had become less a measurement and more a kind of greeting, the small ritual confirmation that the building was still exactly the building it had always been, whatever else about it might be quietly changing underneath that unmoving surface.

The cold met her at exactly the same distance it always met her, and she stood a moment at the base of the ladder the way she always stood, letting her ears do the work before her eyes had anything useful to report, and then went down the same corridor, past the same office with its collapsed chair, its rusted filing cabinet still holding whatever paper it had earned the right to keep, into the same wide chamber with the stripped control board and the central drain and the valve wheels rusted solid at every joint.

Nothing about the room itself had changed in four visits, the same mineral streaks on the walls, the same silence that swallowed her footsteps before they could properly become sound, and she found, standing there for the handful of seconds it took to orient herself, a strange kind of comfort in exactly how unchanged it all was, a single fixed point in a year that had otherwise rearranged itself around her more times than she honestly cared to count.

It found her the way it always found her, unhurried, incurious, closing the distance the moment she stepped into its path. She’d stopped, somewhere around the third visit, thinking of the opening moment as a surprise at all, the same way a wrestler stopped flinching at the whistle after enough rounds, and she watched it come the way she watched anything familiar, tracking the shift in its weight rather than bracing against an unknown.

The fight ran the way her fights had started running lately, familiar rather than frightening, a rhythm her body executed with less conscious thought each time. Base low. Angle everything. Absorb what she couldn’t redirect. The first exchange came in high and she caught it clean, the second came low and she stepped through it the way Tomasz had once described stepping through a leg sweep rather than retreating from it, and by the third and fourth she’d found the same steady breathing pattern she used on the mat, in through the nose, out through the mouth, the room and the cold and the thing itself all narrowing down to the next half-second and nothing beyond it.

She managed six exchanges clean, felt the seventh clip her at an angle that cost her more than the others, a flat catch across her ribs that she filed immediately as a mistake to review later rather than a crisis to react to now, and disengaged at roughly the point her internal clock told her was safe. She walked the reset backward the way she always walked it, counting the paces the way she always counted them, watching until the patrol resumed its slow route along the same worn groove in the concrete.

* * *

She noticed it on the walk out, more by accident than by any deliberate inspection, a flash of her headlamp catching something on the thing’s foreleg that hadn’t caught the light the same way six weeks ago.

She stopped. She had no real reason to stop, disengagement already well underway, distance already opening between them, and she stopped anyway, because a detail that didn’t match her own memory of an object was exactly the kind of detail she had never once, in six years of repair work, allowed herself to walk past. It was the same reflex that had stopped her hand halfway to a client’s door the first time she’d noticed a hairline crack in a housing she was about to hand back, the same one that had her circling back to a job three streets away because a number in her head hadn’t quite matched a number on a receipt. Some instincts didn’t care whether the moment was convenient.

From this distance, in bad light, at speed, she could only get an impression of it rather than a proper look: a section of the foreleg’s casing that read differently from the rest, less rust, a slightly different grain to the metal, the specific visual signature of a repair rather than an original component. It sat roughly where a wrist joint would sit on something built along more conventional lines, a panel maybe the size of her own palm, catching the headlamp at an angle the surrounding rust simply didn’t.

She filed the impression without stopping to confirm it, because confirming it meant closing distance on something she’d just spent considerable effort putting distance from, and no amount of curiosity was worth that particular trade tonight. She had learned, across four visits and one very bad night on a bathroom floor, exactly how much a moment of misplaced curiosity could cost her, and the lesson had stuck in a way few lessons in her life ever needed repeating twice to teach.

She kept walking instead, the paracord unspooling loose against her leg as she gained on her own trail, and let the impression sit in her memory the way she let any unconfirmed observation sit, present, filed, not yet promoted to the status of an actual fact she’d act on. By the time she reached the ladder her breathing had settled back to something close to ordinary, and she climbed the twenty-two rungs the way she always climbed them now, careful but no longer frightened, a body that had learned exactly how much of itself it could safely spend on a given night.

Did you see that too, Pip asked, once she’d cleared enough ground to feel steady again, or am I imagining a pattern that isn’t there.

“I saw something,” Nadia said. “I don’t know what yet.”

It looked, to me at least, like the leg didn’t match the rest of it. Newer. Or different, at least. I don’t have anything to compare it against beyond what I remember from your first two visits, and my memory of those isn’t exactly reliable, given the state I was in for most of them.

“Neither is mine,” Nadia admitted. “I was a little busy at the time.”

Do you think it’s actually dangerous. The change, whatever it is. I keep running through possibilities and none of them are ones I particularly like.

She considered the question properly, walking the tree line back toward the fire road with her headlamp off now, saving the batteries, navigating the last stretch by memory and the faint ambient light the city always threw up against a cloudy sky even this far out.

“I don’t know yet,” she said. “Different isn’t automatically dangerous. A board with a different part on it isn’t automatically a worse board than it was before. But I don’t like not knowing where the part actually came from, or why it’s there now and simply wasn’t before.”

That’s a very specific kind of not-knowing to dislike.

“It’s the kind that usually matters most,” Nadia said. “The parts that show up without an obvious source are the ones I end up chasing for hours later, sometimes days. I’d rather chase this one now, on my own schedule, at a distance I choose, than have it chase me later on terms I don’t.”

* * *

She wrote it up properly once she got home, the room still cold on her from the walk back, jacket still on, the way she wrote up anything that presented itself as a fact and refused to resolve into a full explanation on the spot. A new page rather than a margin note, because this felt like the start of its own line of inquiry rather than an addendum to an existing one, the kind of entry that deserved room to grow rather than a cramped space between two unrelated notes.

Visit four, she wrote, and the date beside it, a habit that had never once failed to matter later even when it seemed unnecessary at the time. Foreleg casing shows a section inconsistent with surrounding wear. Possible repair or replacement. Cannot confirm material or extent from available observation distance, roughly four to five metres, poor light, brief exposure. Compare against photographs from visit one if any exist.

None did. She had never once thought to photograph the thing itself, too occupied every time with simply surviving the encounter to spare any attention for documentation, and she noted the gap in her own methodology with the same flat disapproval she’d have given a client who’d never once kept a receipt for a part she was now being asked to warranty. It was, she reflected, a strange oversight for someone who photographed nearly everything else that passed across her bench, board serial numbers, fault locations, the occasional particularly satisfying repair, before-and-after pairs she sometimes kept just for her own reference. The thing on the catwalk had simply never registered, in her own head, as a subject worth documenting rather than a threat worth surviving, and she was only now realising those were two entirely different categories that happened, this whole time, to occupy the same object.

Next visit, she added, underlining it once. Photograph it. Do not get close enough to be worth the risk for the photograph.

The bay was quiet around her, the compressor on the other side of the wall ticking through its usual cycle, Fitch long since gone home for the night, and she let the quiet do some of the work a busier room never could, thoughts arriving with less competition for her attention than they got during a shift at Pearce’s or a practice on the mat.

She sat with the unfinished thought a while longer, chair tipped back against the bench the way she sat when a problem needed more time than a single pass could give it, turning over the two explanations that seemed most plausible against everything she actually knew about the site. Either the thing had always had some capacity to change, slowly, in ways too gradual for two early visits spaced only days apart to have caught, drift she simply hadn’t been looking for at the time. Or something about her own repeated engagement with it was producing the change directly, cause and effect running through a mechanism she had exactly no visibility into yet, no more visibility than she’d had into the threat-held payout before three careful visits had finally cracked it open.

She didn’t like either explanation. The first meant the site had always been stranger than the flat three sentences on the Guild registry suggested, a fact that had simply been waiting for her to notice it rather than something she’d caused. The second meant she was, in some way she didn’t yet understand, actively changing the thing every time she went near it, which raised an uncomfortable question about what exactly four visits’ worth of change might already have produced, and what forty visits might eventually produce given enough time. She wrote both down anyway, because a fact she disliked was still a fact, and disliking a fact had never once made it less true.

* * *

The formalizing of the other thing, the thing she’d actually come home to write up before the foreleg had interrupted her plans, took longer, because it deserved more care than a margin note.

She had enough data now, four visits’ worth, dozens of individual exchanges across all of them, to stop treating the pattern as a working theory and start treating it as something closer to a rule. She wrote it the way she’d write a spec sheet, plain numbers, no hedging past what the evidence actually supported, the same discipline she’d apply to any component she was rating for a client who needed to trust the number without needing to trust her reasoning behind it.

The scale itself had taken her most of a week to build, a private system with no units anyone else would recognise, calibrated entirely against her own recovery time and the residual soreness she logged after each contact, cross-referenced against the caloric cost she was already tracking for entirely separate reasons and, where the two overlapped, sanity-checked against each other until the numbers stopped contradicting themselves. It wasn’t precise in any way a real instrument would have respected. It was consistent, which mattered more, because a number that meant the same thing every time she used it was worth considerably more than a number that happened, once, to be exactly right.

A contact absorbed cleanly, base low, angle correct, weight redirected rather than met head-on, cost her something small. She’d started, somewhere around the third visit, quietly counting the aftermath the way she counted anything, translating the mild residual ache into a number on the scale she’d built for exactly this purpose, and the number that number kept landing on, contact after contact, session after session, sat close enough to forty that she trusted it as a working constant, stable across four wildly different sets of circumstances, tired and fresh, well-fed and mid-cut.

A contact taken flat, no redirection, full weight absorbed straight through rather than around, cost her considerably more. That number, run across the handful of times she’d actually mismanaged an exchange badly enough to measure it properly, the seventh exchange tonight chief among them, landed close to three hundred, seven and a half times the cost of the clean version for what felt, in the moment, like barely more force at all, the difference between the two contacts invisible to anyone watching who didn’t already know exactly what to look for.

She read the whole page back once more carefully before she trusted it, checking each figure against the exchanges she could actually remember clearly, the way she checked anything before she let herself act on it, and the numbers held steady. She underlined the ratio twice. Seven and a half to one. Not a small margin. Not a margin she could afford to treat casually, not against a body that had already proven, twice, exactly how badly it punished her for underestimating the actual cost of getting this wrong. She thought, briefly and carefully, about what that ratio actually meant for a much longer session, a full ninety seconds rather than the twenty or thirty she still kept most visits capped at, and set the thought aside for another night entirely, because the arithmetic of that particular question deserved considerably more care than she had energy left in her tonight to give it.

She sketched a rough table instead, right in the margin, exchanges managed against exchanges badly mismanaged across all four visits, a running tally that made the pattern easier to see at a glance than the paragraph above it managed to. Eighteen clean contacts. Four flat ones, counting tonight’s. The ratio of clean to flat mattered almost as much as the cost ratio itself, she realised, because a body that only mismanaged one exchange in five was a body that could afford, on average, to push considerably further than the caution she’d been building her sessions around so far.

She didn’t act on that thought tonight either. It went into the same page as everything else, filed rather than executed, because a good idea tested too early was, in her experience, indistinguishable from a bad one right up until it wasn’t, and she had far too much riding on this particular experiment to gamble on a hunch that hadn’t yet earned its own line of proper data.

So the wrestling actually matters, Pip said, reading the page over her shoulder in the only way it ever read anything. Not as background. As the mechanism itself.

“It’s the only mechanism I’ve got,” Nadia said. “The System doesn’t mitigate anything. It just logs whatever actually reaches me. What reaches me is entirely on how I stand.”

That’s either an enormous advantage or an enormous liability, depending on how good you are at standing correctly under pressure.

“I’ve had six years of practice,” Nadia said, which was true, and closed the notebook on a page she suspected she was going to reread more often than almost anything else in it.

Pip was quiet for a while after that, the particular quiet that had started to mean something specific over the last several weeks, less an absence than a kind of thinking, and Nadia let it sit rather than fill it, because she’d learned by now that a silence like that one usually resolved into something worth hearing if she gave it room.

Can I ask something slightly outside the numbers, Pip said, after a moment.

“Go ahead.”

Does it bother you that the thing keeping you alive down there isn’t magic, or a weapon, or anything the story of this would usually give a person. It’s wrestling. A sport you already knew before any of this started. I don’t mean that as a criticism of wrestling. I mean it as a genuine question about how it feels to find out the thing that saves your life was already sitting in you the whole time, waiting for a use nobody could have predicted for it.

Nadia thought about the question longer than she thought about most of Pip’s questions, letting it sit for a while before answering, turning it over the whole time the way she turned over an unfamiliar fault she wasn’t yet ready to commit to.

“It doesn’t bother me,” she said, finally. “It feels like the opposite of a coincidence. Six years of getting thrown around by people bigger than me, and it turns out that was practice for something. I’d rather it be that than nothing at all.”

That’s a genuinely good way to think about it, Pip said, quiet, something warm threading through the words. I don’t know if I’d have gotten there on my own. I don’t have six years of anything to draw a comparison from. Everything I know I learned all at once, in one sitting, and I think that makes it harder for me to see the shape of a slow thing turning into a useful thing the way you just described it.

“You’ll have your own version eventually,” Nadia said, and meant it fully, though she couldn’t have said exactly what she thought that version might end up looking like, only that six long years had turned wrestling into something genuinely useful and she saw no particular reason the same wouldn’t eventually be true of whatever Pip was still becoming.

* * *

Pearce’s shop absorbed the following Tuesday the way it absorbed every Tuesday, unremarkable, a trickle of customers and a floor that needed sweeping regardless of how empty the store had actually been. She caught herself, twice during the shift, running the seven-and-a-half ratio through her head while she restocked a bin of washers, the number settling into the same category as any other constant she’d ever memorized off a datasheet, present without her having to consciously reach for it.

A woman came in mid-afternoon after a specific gauge of picture wire, standing at the bin for a long while turning two nearly identical spools over in her hands, and left with the cheaper one after Nadia pointed out, honestly, that the difference between them wouldn’t matter for anything lighter than a mirror. Pearce, watching from the back of the store, said nothing about it at the time and mentioned it later, once the woman had gone, that most kids working a register would have let her buy the more expensive one without a word, and that he appreciated that Nadia apparently hadn’t thought to. She said it hadn’t occurred to her to do otherwise, which was true, and he seemed to find that answer considerably more interesting than the deed itself, the specific quiet interest of a man who’d spent thirty years watching people make the opposite choice without ever once mentioning it out loud.

A regular came through around four with a jammed lock he’d been fighting for a week, describing the symptom in more detail than it actually needed, and Nadia diagnosed it from across the counter before he’d finished the second sentence, a worn pin rather than anything wrong with the key itself, and sold him the small tube of graphite lubricant that would actually fix it rather than the full lock replacement he’d come in half-expecting to need. He thanked her twice, once at the counter and once on his way out, the way people so often seemed to feel the need to, and she still hadn’t worked out, after four months of this trade, exactly why the second one always felt necessary to anybody.

Pearce, closing out the register at the end of the day, mentioned, almost as an aside, that a regular customer had asked after her specifically, a woman with a jammed garage door opener who’d heard, somewhere along the grapevine that ran up and down this stretch of the Drive, that the kid at the hardware store fixed things properly rather than just selling a replacement. Nadia said she’d take the job if the woman wanted to bring it in, and Pearce said he’d pass the message along, and neither of them made anything more of it than that, the reputation accumulating the same quiet way everything else in her life seemed to accumulate lately, one small fact stacked on top of the last without any single moment announcing itself as significant.

He locked the till and pulled the heavy security grate down himself that evening, waving off her offer to help, and told her, on her way out, that business had picked up a little these last few months, enough that he’d actually looked at the books properly for the first time in a while and found something other than a slow decline in them. He didn’t say it was because of her specifically. He didn’t need to say it out loud at all. The math was obvious enough to both of them already that saying it plainly would only have made the moment more awkward than it needed to be.

She walked home along a stretch of the Drive that had grown as familiar to her, by now, as the fire road out past the reservoir, past the same laundromat-turned-nothing, the same hardware store sign with its ambiguous closing time, past a bank of mailboxes somebody had recently repainted a colour that already looked wrong against the building behind it, and let her mind drift slowly over the day’s two separate threads, the ratio in the notebook and the panel on the foreleg, both of them still open, both of them waiting patiently on more data she had no choice but to gather slowly, two weeks at a time, whether she liked the pace or not.

Practice that Thursday ran a technical session rather than conditioning, Val walking the whole room through defensive framing against a bigger opponent, hands and forearms doing the work a smaller wrestler’s legs and hips couldn’t, and Nadia found herself paying an entirely different quality of attention to it than she had three months ago, before any of this had a second application. Val corrected the angle on her forearm twice during the drill, small adjustments, a few degrees each time, and Nadia felt, both times, the difference land the way it had landed on the catwalk, force redirected instead of absorbed, the exact same physics wearing a different uniform.

She drilled it longer than the exercise strictly called for, past the point Val had moved the group on to something else, running the same motion against an imaginary line of force until it stopped feeling like technique and started feeling like the only sensible way a body could ever meet an incoming weight. Her forearm ached faintly by the very end of it, the good kind of ache, muscle memory settling deeper with every single repetition, and she thought, running it one more time before she let herself stop, that this was the closest thing to training for the site she actually had access to, a gym full of strangers unwittingly helping her survive something none of them would ever know existed. Tomasz caught her at it during a water break, asked what she was working on so intently that she hadn’t heard him say her name twice, and she said forearm angles, which was true, and didn’t elaborate, and he nodded like that was a perfectly reasonable thing for someone to be obsessing over three months into a season, because for anyone else in that room, it would have been. He showed her a small correction of his own, something he’d picked up from a coach two schools back, and she filed it away carefully, the same way she filed everything useful, without ever once telling him exactly how much further she intended to actually take it than he could have possibly imagined.

She walked slowly home that evening with the ratio still sitting somewhere at the back of her mind, forty against three hundred, wrestling itself against a machine with no mitigation stat of its own to lean on, every single drill Val ran from here forward now doing double duty whether Val herself ever knew it or not. She thought, without any particular weight attached to the thought, that of all the strange things this year had handed her, being good at exactly one specific, narrow, punishing skill turning out to be the entire reason she was still alive felt, on reflection, like the least strange of all of them, the one piece of this whole impossible year that had actually made sense from the very first day she’d learned it, years before any of the rest of it had ever existed at all.
