Chapter 2: Three Categories

Eight days.

The bus was four minutes late and then sat at the stop for two more while the driver dealt with a farecard that would not read, tapping it against the reader four times before waving the man through anyway, and Nadia stood at the back doors with her hood down because the rain had thinned overnight into something that barely counted, a mist more than a weather. Through the window the Drive went by in the grey particular to a Tuesday morning with nothing in it yet, shops still gated, a delivery van double-parked outside the bakery that had replaced the bakery that had closed.

The letter from the Ministry had come by text three days after she moved into Kelso House, a single line with a date and a file number and an address on Kingsway she had never had a reason to visit before. She had written the date on the inside of her wrist in pen the night before, the way she wrote things she did not trust herself to check twice, and it had mostly worn off by the time the bus doors opened, one stroke of the eight still legible, the rest gone to a grey smear.

The building was five storeys of glass and beige panel that had been built for something larger than what it currently held. Two directories stood in the lobby, backlit, and one of them had gone dark at some point and never been replaced, so that half the building’s departments existed only as names on the working directory, crowded in around the departments that had absorbed them. Family and Youth Services, Transitional Supports, and something called Regional Intake shared a floor and, from the look of the directory, most of a budget line. A fourth line, Community Liaison, had no floor listed at all, just a dash where a number should have been, and Nadia looked at it for a second the way she looked at any fault with no obvious cause and filed it without an answer.

She took the stairs, because the elevator had a queue of four people in front of a single working car, a second car standing open and dark behind an out of service sign that had been printed on the same paper stock as the one at Delacourt’s building, and she had eight minutes and no faith left in signs like that one.

* * *

The waiting room on the third floor had thirty chairs and eleven people in them, and a kiosk against the wall for checking in that had a hand-written sign taped over its screen reading please see front desk. Nadia looked at it for the length of time it took to cross the room and diagnosed it without meaning to: a screen that had gone from unresponsive to blank was a power fault more often than a software one, most likely a dead supply board behind the panel rather than anything wrong with the touch surface itself, and a unit that had earned a handwritten sign instead of a service call had been dead long enough that nobody was coming for it. She filed it and kept walking, the itch to open the housing and look mild and easy to set down, because it was not her building and nobody had asked her.

The front desk had two staff behind it and a third chair, empty, with a nameplate still on the desk and nothing else on it, not even a monitor. She gave her name and her file number, and the woman behind the desk did not look up from her own screen for the first eleven seconds, typing through something that had nothing to do with Nadia, and then did, and told her to take a seat, someone would call her.

She took a seat two down from a man with a toddler asleep across his lap and a stroller folded against his leg, and a stack of paperwork balanced on the empty seat beside him that he kept glancing at and not touching, the particular stillness of someone conserving the one free hand he had. On her other side a woman in a high-visibility vest read something on her phone with the sound off, captions running along the bottom in a language Nadia didn’t recognise. Nobody spoke to anybody. The room ran on the low continuous noise of a building doing several hundred people’s worth of business through one working elevator and eleven chairs’ worth of actual seating for thirty, phones and a photocopier somewhere behind the desk and, every so often, a name called that made three or four heads lift at once before settling back down when it wasn’t theirs.

A name got called near the twenty-minute mark, an older man three seats down who took a while to get up, and came back less than ninety seconds later still holding the same folder, unopened, saying something to the woman at the desk in a voice pitched low enough that Nadia only caught the shape of it and not the words, the specific flat cadence of someone repeating a sentence they had already said in this building more than once. The woman at the desk said something back, also low, also flat, and the man sat back down in the same seat with the folder in his lap, and nobody around him reacted, because it was the kind of thing that happened in this room often enough that reacting to it would have taken more energy than anyone in the chairs had spare.

Forty minutes passed by the clock over the check-in desk, which was eleven minutes fast against her own phone, a fact she confirmed twice because it seemed like the kind of thing that would matter to someone eventually and she wanted to already know the answer before it did.

* * *

Bhandari’s office was a cubicle with three walls, not four, on a floor where every other cubicle looked exactly the same, distinguishable only by what had been pinned to the felt divider. Bhandari’s had a school photo of two kids in matching sweaters, curling at one corner, and a printed schedule with a week’s worth of appointment blocks stacked so close together that several of them overlapped by ten minutes on the page, one name’s slot bleeding directly into the next with no gap drawn between them at all.

She had a phone against her shoulder when Nadia sat down, on hold, the hold music the same tinny loop Ferreira’s line ran, which made Nadia wonder for a second whether it was the same system across every office in the province or whether all hold music simply converged on the same four bars eventually, the way every dying capacitor smelled roughly the same regardless of what device it had been sitting in, a convergent failure rather than a shared cause. Bhandari held up one finger, listened for another ten seconds, then hung up without saying anything to whoever had picked up, the way a person hangs up on an automated system rather than a human being, and set the phone face down on top of the nearest stack, where it began ringing again almost immediately, silenced.

She pulled the file. It took a moment to find, not because it was lost but because there were a great many files on the desk and in two trays and stacked on top of a locked drawer, and Bhandari’s system for finding the right one seemed to work by memory of shape and thickness rather than by label. Nadia counted the visible stack without trying to and stopped at around forty, which at whatever the appointment length worked out to be was not a caseload so much as a queue that never emptied, only reordered itself by whoever’s deadline had come due that week.

Bhandari read for a moment with her chin down, the same way Ferreira had, and then set the file flat and said this was a straightforward conversation, the kind she had a lot of, and that Nadia should stop her any time something wasn’t clear.

The transitional benefit, she said, was conditional. It kept Nadia enrolled in school and off the queue for a congregate placement, and to keep it active she needed to log weekly hours across three categories.

School attendance, verified by the district. That one took care of itself as long as Nadia showed up, which the system would confirm automatically once she was enrolled, and enrolment was the second stop on today’s list, not this one.

A supervised activity. Something structured, with adult oversight, that ran on a regular schedule. A team sport counted. A structured after-school program counted. It had to be verifiable by someone other than Nadia, someone who could confirm attendance if asked, a name and a signature the Ministry could call.

And a verified work or training placement. Paid or unpaid did not matter. What mattered was that the hours were signed off by an employer of record, someone whose name and registration number went on a form, someone the Ministry could reach if it needed to reach anyone at all.

Nadia asked whether all three categories carried the same weight, or whether being short on one was worse than being short on another.

Bhandari said the system technically scored them the same, each one either logged or not, no partial credit for effort that hadn’t made it onto a form, but that in practice a missing placement was the one that triggered a review fastest, because it was the category most associated, historically, with kids who’d drifted off the radar entirely, and the algorithm that flagged files for early attention had been tuned around that pattern years ago and never revisited since. She said this last part almost apologetically, as though she found it slightly absurd herself, a machine still reacting to a shape of risk from a decade it no longer accurately described.

Nadia said she worked. She said she had steady clients, repaired mobility equipment and electronics, made more most weeks than the placement category would ever require.

Bhandari said that was good to hear, genuinely, in the tone of someone who did not get to hear it often, and then said it did not satisfy the category, because the category needed an employer of record, and Nadia did not have one. She was self-employed, unlicensed, paid in cash, sixteen. There was no business number a sixteen-year-old without a guardian could register on her own, and no employer to sign a form confirming hours that had never been logged anywhere in the first place.

Nadia asked whether there was a version of the form that covered self-employment.

Bhandari said there was, technically, and pulled it up to check, scrolling through two screens before she found it, and then said the self-employment stream required three months of banking records showing consistent deposits into a registered business account, which required the business number, which required the guardian’s signature, which was the same wall from a different angle. She said this without any particular emphasis, the way a person describes a door that has always been locked rather than one that has just been locked in front of you.

Nadia asked what happened to the money she made in the meantime, whether it needed to be declared anywhere at all if none of the forms could see it.

Bhandari said that below a certain threshold, informally, nobody was chasing it, and that the honest answer was the systems that would care about undeclared cash income and the systems that decided whether a minor stayed out of congregate care did not talk to each other, had never been built to, and that she personally was not about to be the one who made them start. She said it flatly, not as a secret, just as a fact about how the machine was wired, the same way she might have described which floor the photocopier was on, and Nadia filed it the same way, a working note rather than a piece of good news, because a gap two systems had never noticed was still a gap, and gaps closed eventually, usually without warning and never on a schedule anyone published in advance.

So the income did not count. Not because it wasn’t real. Because there was no piece of paper anywhere that agreed it was.

* * *

The deadline was the second week of October, six weeks out, which Bhandari wrote on a slip and pushed across the desk rather than trusting to memory or to the file, which had lost more than one date to the stack it lived in, she said, without making it sound like a complaint, just an observation about a system that had been asked to hold more than it was built for.

If both categories weren’t logged by the review date, the file reopened. Nadia asked what that meant in practice.

Bhandari said it meant a reassessment, and a reassessment for an unaccompanied sixteen-year-old with no guardian on file usually meant a recommendation for congregate care, because congregate care was supervised and documented and closed the exact gap the review would flag. She said this the same way she’d said the thing about the door. Not a threat. A description of what the machine did when the inputs came out a certain way, the same as any other fault path, predictable once you knew the logic behind it.

Nadia asked whether Bhandari could just write down that the income was real.

Bhandari said she believed her, and that believing her wasn’t the same thing as the form having a box for it, and that she was sorry, and that she said that a lot too and it didn’t fix anything either, but she meant it every time she said it, and she thought that mattered even if it changed nothing on the page.

Nadia said thanks. She meant it in the same way she’d meant it with Ferreira, and did not examine any further than that why two different women in two different offices, on two different floors of two different systems, had both landed on almost the same sentence in almost the same week, as though it were something the job taught you to say once you’d done it long enough to know it was the only honest thing left to offer.

She asked who actually did the reassessment, whether it was Bhandari herself or someone else, and whether Nadia would need to be in the room for it.

Bhandari said it would come from a different desk, a regional review officer who worked off the file rather than off any conversation with the person the file belonged to, which meant the version of Nadia that got assessed in October would be built entirely out of whatever paper existed by then and nothing else, no context, no explanation, nothing said in this room today carrying forward unless it had been written down. She said the officer wouldn’t call Nadia in unless the categories were already short, and that most files that made it that far didn’t get a conversation first, only a letter with a decision already in it.

Nadia asked, because it seemed worth asking now that the shape of it was clear, why the whole system ran this close to the edge for everyone, not just her.

Bhandari looked at her for a second in a way that suggested this was not a question she got often, not from someone Nadia’s age, and said the honest answer was that the caseload had roughly doubled in the last six years without the staffing doubling with it, and that most of the doubling had come from exactly the kind of change that had put Nadia here in the first place, thresholds lowered a year at a time across half a dozen programs because it cost less to lower a number than to fund the thing the number used to guarantee, and that nobody who made those decisions sat in this cubicle afterward to watch what it did to the calendar. She said it without any charge in it at all, a fact recited the way a fact gets recited by someone who has stopped being surprised by it, and then said that was probably more than Nadia had asked for, and that the six weeks were the six weeks regardless of why they existed.

She asked what counted as a supervised activity, specifically, because that one at least seemed solvable inside six weeks.

Bhandari said school sports, community league programs, registered youth clubs, anything with a coordinator willing to sign a form once a week. She said the school itself usually had a list, this time of year, because most of the fall programs were still taking registrations, and that if Nadia could get both categories logged before the review, the file never had to reopen in the first place, and this whole conversation would be a formality nobody remembered by November.

She stood to signal the appointment was over before Nadia had finished folding the slip, a motion that had nothing unkind in it, only the shape of someone who had another name to call in ninety seconds whether or not this one was ready to leave.

Nadia folded the slip with the date on it into quarters and put it in the same pocket as the voucher letter, which put two clocks in the same jacket now, running at different speeds toward the same kind of ending.

* * *

The enrolment office at Kitsilano Secondary was two buildings and one SkyTrain stop away, and open until four, which gave her most of the afternoon.

It was a smaller room than the Ministry’s, and busier in a different key, the noise of a building processing several hundred returning students in the space of two weeks rather than several hundred crises in the space of a year. A man behind the counter, a laminated name tag reading Mr. Okafor, took her transfer paperwork and her ID and typed for a while without looking up, the posture of someone doing something he had done several thousand times and would do several thousand more before June.

He confirmed her grade. He confirmed her address, which was Kelso House for another eight days and after that a blank field she left as Kelso House anyway, because there was nowhere else to put and no reason yet to explain why, and because the form had no field at all for an address that hadn’t been decided yet, only ones for addresses that already existed. He scrolled through a screen she couldn’t see the angle of, and somewhere in it, without any change in his expression or his typing speed, was a line that read Class: Logistical Clerk/Courier, F-Tier, because everyone’s file had a line like that, had had one since the classing sweep that ran through every school registry the year she was eleven, and it meant nothing to Mr. Okafor and had never meant anything to anyone who’d looked at hers, because there was nothing on the line worth a second glance.

He asked if she was interested in any fall programs, sliding a printed sheet across the counter without waiting for an answer, the same sheet he’d apparently slid across the counter to everyone in front of her, judging by how worn the top copy already was, the corners gone soft.

She looked at it while he finished the enrolment, mostly out of the six weeks sitting in her pocket rather than any interest of her own. Drama. Robotics, which had a waitlist. Badminton. And near the bottom, wrestling, preseason conditioning starting the following week, a room number and a name, V. Vaillancourt, and a note that said no experience necessary, all weights welcome.

She read the line twice, the way she’d read the multimeter twice that morning on a board that had already told her the answer. It wasn’t the sport that was new. Three schools in four years had meant three different rooms and three different names on three different flyers, and this was only the fourth, a coach she hadn’t met yet running a program in a gym she hadn’t seen yet, which was not the same thing as starting from nothing, only the same thing as starting over. She folded the sheet along its printed crease and put it in the pocket with the other piece of paper, so that the jacket now held three clocks instead of two, all of them running, none of them agreeing with each other about how much time she had.

Mr. Okafor handed back her ID and said welcome back, and she said thanks, and that was the whole of it.

* * *

Her phone buzzed on the walk out, a client from the parts board asking whether she could come by before six, something about a courier’s e-bike controller that had died mid-shift the night before and a route he needed to run that evening.

His name was on the message thread from a job in June, and the address put him three blocks off her route home, so she said forty minutes and meant it.

He met her on the sidewalk outside a basement suite with the bike already turned upside down on a folded blanket, the delivery bag still strapped to the rear rack, a phone propped against the wall behind him showing a countdown to his shift start that he kept glancing at whether he meant to or not. He explained it in the order things had actually happened rather than the order that would have made sense to a technician: it had cut out on a hill, come back for two blocks, then died completely and stayed dead, and he had a shift starting at six he could not afford to lose a second night of pay to, not after last night, which he mentioned without saying what last night had cost him and she didn’t ask.

The rain had picked up again while she knelt on the wet concrete with her toolroll unrolled beside her, and she worked the way she always worked in weather like this, fast and without wasted motion, because a screwdriver dropped in a puddle outside a basement suite was a screwdriver she wasn’t getting back in any usable state. A neighbour came out partway through to drag a recycling bin to the curb, glanced at the bike upside down on the blanket and the girl kneeling over it with a headlamp on, and went back inside without saying anything, the specific incuriosity of somebody who had lived long enough on this block to stop being surprised by what showed up on it.

She had the controller housing open in under two minutes. The connector at the battery lead had cooked itself, the plastic gone soft and slightly deformed around two of the pins, consistent with a bad crimp from the factory finally giving out under six months of a courier’s vibration and weather rather than any one dramatic failure. Nothing about the motor itself was wrong. She told him this while she worked, because he was standing over her the way people did when their income depended on the next ten minutes, and it seemed like the kind of thing that would settle him faster than silence would.

The replacement connector came out of the small parts wallet she carried for exactly this, the kind of failure common enough on ebike conversions that she kept three of the housing in her bag at all times without needing to think about why. She crimped the new one, sealed it with heat-shrink from the same wallet, and had the housing closed again in under fifteen minutes total, start to finish, faster than the walk over had taken.

She reassembled the housing, checked the seal on the gasket before she closed it, because a controller that survived a factory defect only to die of water ingress six weeks later was a callback neither of them needed, and handed it back to him. It started on the first press. He thanked her in a way that had real relief in it, the kind that came from a specific number he’d already been doing in his head, and paid her thirty without being asked, more than she would have quoted, and said if it happened again he was calling her first, not the shop on Fraser that had wanted ninety and two days.

She said it probably wouldn’t happen again, not with that connector, and that if it did, it likely wasn’t the connector at all and he should message her a video of it dying rather than guess, because a video told her more in ten seconds than most people managed in ten minutes of description, and she’d rather have the ten seconds. He was already wheeling the bike toward the street, checking the time on his phone, and said he would, though she doubted either of them believed he actually would remember to.

She put the thirty in with the rest and started walking again, three clocks in one pocket and thirty more dollars in another, none of the numbers touching any of the others, though she noted, the way she noted most things, that an hour of being useful to a stranger had done more for the day than either of the two hours she’d spent in waiting rooms.

* * *

By the time she got back to Kelso House the mist had gone back to real rain, and the linen-store office still had its light on, which meant Ferreira was still at the desk an hour past when she was supposed to be.

Nadia put her head in anyway. She asked, because it seemed like the kind of question that only cost thirty seconds either way, whether the housing file and the Ministry compliance file were the same system, whether anything she’d done that afternoon would show up anywhere useful to the thirty days counting down on the letter still folded in her pocket.

Ferreira looked up from whatever she’d been doing, and it took her a second to place the question, the specific second of someone mentally sorting through two systems she worked inside every day without ever having been asked whether they spoke to each other. She said no, not really, not automatically. The transitional housing program ran through a different intake stream than the ongoing benefit file, different funding, different office, and the two only touched if somebody manually flagged a case across both, which happened sometimes, when a worker had the time to notice a connection worth flagging, and mostly didn’t, because nobody had the time.

She said this the way Bhandari had said the thing about the door, and the thing about the systems that didn’t talk to each other, in almost the same register, and Nadia filed the coincidence without remarking on it, the way she filed most patterns that had no immediate use.

Ferreira asked if there was a specific reason she was asking, a note of something in it that might have been concern, and Nadia said no, just wanted to know how the pieces fit, which was true, and left it there, because it was the whole of what she’d wanted.

She went back up to the room and hung her jacket on the radiator that wasn’t running, and took the three pieces of paper out of the pocket before it could soak through to them: the voucher letter, eight days now instead of eleven, the slip with the October date written in Bhandari’s hand, and the fall programs sheet folded along its crease with a room number and a name circled where she’d circled it without quite deciding to.

She pinned all three to the wall above the bench, in a line, where the bench light would catch them in the morning, and pulled a dead router board out of the totes, one she had been meaning to strip for parts since June, because the numbers on the wall were not going to solve themselves tonight and the board might at least give something back for the hour.

The router had died of nothing dramatic, a swollen electrolytic on the five-volt rail that had finally let go after however many years of sitting in someone’s closet running warm, the same failure as the mobility controller from the morning before, the same failure as half of everything that came through her hands eventually, because heat and time did the same thing to every capacitor built to the same price point regardless of what device it happened to be soldered into. She desoldered the flash chip first, because that was the part worth keeping, then the crystal, then anything else on the board with a footprint common enough to matter, working through it methodically with the totes open on either side of her, sorting each part into the tote labelled for its package size the moment it came free.

By the time she stopped it was past eleven, and the board on the mat in front of her was down to bare copper and a scatter of components sorted into six different containers, and the three pieces of paper on the wall hadn’t moved, hadn’t changed, hadn’t gotten any easier to look at than they had been three hours earlier. She swept the empty board into the recycling bin under the bench, checked the three papers on the wall one more time without needing to, since none of the numbers on them had changed since the last time she’d looked, turned off the light, and went to bed with the smell of solder flux still on her hands and eight days, now seven by the clock on the wall, left on the letter closest to the door.
