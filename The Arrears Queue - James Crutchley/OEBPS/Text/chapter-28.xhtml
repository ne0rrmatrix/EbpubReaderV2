Chapter 28: Minimum Viable

By the new year, Phase Eleven held firmly. Phase Six held too. By the third week of the new schedule she had four more convergent steps confirmed and ruled into her table in the same flat black ink, each one tested on a light day, each one costing exactly what she’d budgeted for it and nothing more, the model behaving the way a model was supposed to behave when the person building it had done the work properly.

None of it produced light. That had never been the point of any single step in isolation, only the cumulative weight of enough of them holding together at once, and she reminded herself of that every time a session ended with nothing visible to show for it, the same discipline she applied to any repair that took longer to diagnose than to actually fix.

The table on the shelf had filled in steadily through the weeks since the reclassification, one convergent step confirmed after another, each entry a small, unglamorous victory that never once felt like the word victory actually implied. Phase Nine held on the second attempt after a false negative the first time out, a scheduling error rather than a genuine failure, and she’d spent an entire evening double-checking her own methodology before trusting the correction. Phase Thirteen took four sessions before she isolated the specific variant that actually moved anything, a posture change so subtle she’d nearly dismissed it as noise before the pattern held steady across three more attempts. None of it produced anything visible. All of it, stacked together, was apparently doing something, because on this particular Tuesday the meter finally told her so.

The light arrived on a Tuesday she hadn’t marked as anything special, four steps into the sequence, twenty-six cycles of the same breath pattern she now ran on reflex, and this time the wrongness in her chest didn’t fade after a fraction of a second.

* * *

Three seconds. She counted them the way she counted anything that mattered, watching the meter’s readout climb and hold and finally drop, a genuine sustained response rather than the single sub-second spike Phase One had given her back in November, and felt, in the same instant the light itself would have shown her something if her eyes had been open to see it, the specific hollow drain of a body that had just spent something real.

She sat down before she’d consciously decided to, her legs simply choosing the concrete floor for her, and stayed there a long moment while the alcove settled back into its ordinary stillness around her, the cold of the stone soaking up through her jeans in a way she barely registered against the larger, stranger fatigue sitting somewhere underneath her sternum.

It wasn’t the same tiredness a hard practice left her with, and it wasn’t the specific hollow burn of a queue climbing toward its cap either. It sat somewhere adjacent to both, familiar in its general shape and entirely unfamiliar in its particulars, the way a repaired circuit could draw current through a path she recognised while producing a symptom she’d never once seen it produce before. She catalogued it carefully, the way she catalogued anything new her body handed her, because a symptom she couldn’t name yet was still data, and data ungathered tonight was data she’d have to reconstruct from memory later, imperfectly, if she ever needed it again.

Are you actually, genuinely alright, Pip asked, careful, the concern in it plain even filtered through its usual restraint.

“I’m fine,” Nadia said, and then, because the sentence had cost Val a straight face months ago and she owed Pip more honesty than she owed a coach who could only read a printed sheet, amended it. “I’m tired in a way I don’t have a category for yet. Give me a minute and I’ll have a better answer.”

You went down fast, Pip said, careful.

“I know,” Nadia said, low, still catching her breath properly. “That cost more than it looked like it should. Three seconds of nothing I could see and I feel like I just ran a full mat session against someone considerably heavier than me.”

The meter genuinely agrees with you, Pip said. Whatever that was, it drew considerably harder than Phase One ever did. I think you just found the first honest price tag on any of this whole project.

She sat with that framing for a moment, turning it the way she turned any observation that reorganised the shape of a problem she thought she already understood. Phase One’s flicker, back in October, had cost her something too, she realised now, thinking back on it properly, a fatigue she’d filed at the time as ordinary exhaustion from a long session, never once suspecting it had a second, more specific cause layered underneath the obvious one. She’d simply never had a clean enough baseline to separate the two costs from each other. Tonight, with nothing else competing for the accounting, the cost stood alone, unmistakable, undeniable, exactly as large as it actually was.

“I think I’ve been underestimating every result so far,” she said, slowly. “Not the meter readings. Those were accurate. The cost. I filed all of it under general tiredness because I never had a night clean enough to isolate it properly.”

That’s an uncomfortable retroactive correction, Pip said. It means the whole model’s been running on a slightly wrong assumption about its own price.

“It means I need to go back through the log and flag every entry where I might have miscounted the cost,” Nadia said, already reaching for her notebook. “Better to find that out now than three months from now when the miscounting’s compounded into something I can’t untangle.”

* * *

The alcove had gone properly cold by the time her legs finally agreed to hold her again, the site’s ambient chill settling into her fully now that the exertion’s brief warmth had burned itself off, and she pulled her jacket tighter without standing, unwilling to rush the recovery any faster than her body was clearly willing to grant it. She’d learned, over months of exactly this kind of aftermath, that pushing too soon only cost her more time in the end, a lesson her body seemed determined to keep re-teaching her every time she thought she’d finally internalised it.

She logged the entry carefully, the way she logged anything, three seconds, sustained, no visible output, cost estimated against the fatigue rather than any instrument she owned that could measure it directly, and sat with the number for a while before she let herself draw the conclusion it was pointing toward.

“It’s not free just because I genuinely can’t see it,” she said, mostly to herself. “I’ve been treating the light as the actual output and everything before it as overhead. It’s the other way around. The draw is the real thing happening. The light, when it finally shows up, is just the cheapest way the field has of telling me the draw succeeded.”

That’s a genuinely meaningful reframe, Pip said. You’ve been chasing the wrong variable for weeks without either of us noticing.

She sat with the implication a moment longer, turning it the way she turned any correction that touched more than the single result she’d been examining when it arrived. If the light was only ever the gauge and never the target, then every entry in the notebook she’d filed as a failure, weeks of no result stretching back to October, might have been recording something else entirely, a draw too small for the light to register at all, present and real and simply invisible to the one instrument she’d trusted to tell her whether anything was happening. The thought unsettled her more than she expected it to. She’d built an entire methodology on the assumption that silence meant nothing, and silence, it turned out, might have meant considerably more than she’d ever given it credit for.

“I might owe some of those old entries a proper second look,” she said, slowly. “Not tonight. But eventually. If the gauge only lights up past a certain threshold, I don’t actually know how many of those no results were true negatives and how many were just too small for the meter to have ever seen at all.”

“I wasn’t chasing the wrong variable,” Nadia said. “I just hadn’t separated it from the thing it was pointing at. That’s different. The light was never the target. It was the reading on the gauge.”

She walked the analogy further out loud, working through it the way she worked through anything until she trusted it enough to build on. A voltmeter didn’t produce the electricity it measured. It simply completed a small circuit of its own and reported back what was already happening elsewhere in the system, cheaply, faithfully, without ever once being the thing worth studying in itself. Light was doing the same job here, an instrument rather than an output, a byproduct cheap enough to convert into that the field apparently offered it up first, before anything more expensive, the same way any circuit bled a little current into its indicator lamp before the rest of the load ever saw a volt.

“The guides never say that,” she added, almost to herself. “Not one of the five I compared. They all describe the light like it’s the achievement. Nobody ever wrote it down as a side effect of something else succeeding.”

Maybe nobody who wrote the guides ever thought to ask the question that way, Pip said. You keep finding gaps like this. Not because the guides are wrong about what they describe. Because they never once asked why.

“That’s the whole problem with a recipe,” Nadia said. “It tells you what happened last time. It never once tells you what actually caused it.”

* * *

The next confirmed step came ten days later, another light evening carved carefully out of a schedule that had grown no more generous since the reclassification, and this one produced something she could actually see, a whitish flicker at the edge of her closed eyes that held, unstable and guttering, for a genuine twenty seconds before it finally gave out.

It cost her considerably more than three seconds had. She sat on the cold concrete afterward for a full ten minutes before her legs agreed to hold her weight again, the specific drained exhaustion she usually associated with the back half of a heavy dungeon session rather than anything she’d expect from sitting perfectly still in an alcove two hundred metres from the nearest actual danger.

She’d watched the flicker itself the whole twenty seconds it lasted, unwilling to close her eyes and lose the one direct visual read she was ever likely to get of the thing she was building, and the memory of it sat oddly in her mind now, unstable in a way that felt less like a technical description and more like an actual physical sensation she’d have struggled to put into words for anyone who hadn’t watched it happen. It had guttered the way a candle guttered in a draft, brightening and dimming in a rhythm that had nothing to do with her own breath count, whitish rather than any single colour she could have named cleanly, and it had left, once it finally died, a faint afterimage against her closed eyelids that took several seconds longer to fade than any ordinary bright light should have.

“It didn’t feel controlled at all,” she said, once she’d finally caught enough breath to talk properly. “Not the way Phase One did. That felt like flipping a switch. This felt like holding a door open against wind that kept trying to push it shut.”

That’s a genuinely useful description, Pip said. I don’t think the meter can tell me anything about wind, not in any language I know how to read from a number alone. I’m glad you can, even if it costs you something to describe it properly.

That’s clearly not a linear cost at all, Pip observed, watching her recover. Three seconds cost you noticeably. Twenty seconds cost you considerably more than six times as much.

“I noticed that,” Nadia said, still catching her breath. “It’s not scaling the way I expected. Something about sustaining it costs more per second the longer it runs, not less.”

She thought about that mismatch the whole walk out of the site that night, the scooter’s headlight cutting a narrow cone through a dark that felt, for once, entirely ordinary compared to the specific field-shaped darkness she’d spent the last hour inside. A circuit under sustained load usually behaved the opposite way, more efficient once it settled into a steady state than during the initial spike of switching on. Whatever she was doing wasn’t settling into anything. It was fighting the same resistance harder with every second she asked it to continue, and that resistance had a shape she still didn’t understand, only a cost curve steep enough that she’d felt it in her own legs for a solid ten minutes after the light itself had already gone out.

She logged the shape of the curve as carefully as she’d logged the duration, three seconds costing roughly what she’d have expected from a single hard round on the mat, twenty seconds costing something closer to the tail end of a full dungeon session, a jump that had no business being that steep if the underlying process scaled the way anything else in her experience scaled. Whatever this cost curve actually was, it wasn’t linear, and a nonlinear cost meant the naive plan of simply pushing for longer duration next time was, she suspected, exactly the wrong instinct to follow.

* * *

She thought about the shape of that curve for the rest of the week, turning it over the way she turned over any relationship that refused to behave the way a simple, well-behaved system ought to behave. A light bulb didn’t get proportionally more expensive to run the longer it stayed lit. Whatever she was doing wasn’t behaving like a light bulb, and she made herself sit with that mismatch rather than paper over it with an assumption she hadn’t earned.

“What if it’s not the duration that’s expensive,” she said, working it out loud in the bay one evening, the twenty-second attempt still fresh enough in her memory to examine properly. “What if it’s the colour. It flickered white the whole time it was unstable. White light isn’t one frequency. It’s every frequency at once, and I never once tried to control which ones I was actually producing.”

Photon energy scales with frequency, Pip said, slowly, following the thread with her. Blue and white cost more per photon than red does. I’ve read that in a hundred contexts and never once connected it to what you’re doing until just now.

“Neither had I,” Nadia said. “Because I was thinking about it purely as an on-off switch. Light or no light. I never once thought about it as a dial with a price attached to every single setting.”

She pulled up every scrap of physics she could remember from school on the subject, most of it half-remembered from a unit two years ago that had covered the electromagnetic spectrum in exactly enough detail to pass a test and no further, and cross-referenced it against a handful of articles she found that evening, checking her memory the way she checked anything before trusting it. Red light sat at the low-energy end of the visible range. Blue and violet sat considerably higher. White light wasn’t a single frequency at all, but every visible frequency arriving together, which meant the whitish flicker she’d produced twenty seconds’ worth of hadn’t been one expensive thing. It had been dozens of expensive things, all at once, stacked on top of each other, and she’d had no idea she was paying for all of them simultaneously.

“I’ve been buying the most expensive version of this by accident,” she said, sitting back from the screen. “Every single time. Not because I wanted white light specifically. Because I never once specified anything at all, and apparently the field defaults to the priciest option when nobody tells it otherwise.”

That’s a very specific kind of costly ignorance, Pip said. I don’t think anyone’s ever described a magical failure mode in quite those particular terms before.

“It’s not a magical failure mode,” Nadia said. “It’s an unoptimised default. I’ve fixed worse than this in appliances built by people who actually knew what they were doing.”

She thought of a specific water heater she’d repaired last spring, factory-set to run its element at full power regardless of demand, wasting most of what it drew because nobody at the plant had bothered to spend the extra hour writing a smarter control loop. She’d fixed that one in twenty minutes with a cheap thermostat kit and a screwdriver. This felt, in its own strange way, like the exact same category of fault, a system defaulting to maximum output because nobody had ever told it a cheaper setting existed, and she was, apparently, the first person who’d ever bothered to write the smarter instruction into it.

* * *

She spent the days leading up to the next light evening preparing the way she prepared for any experiment where the setup mattered as much as the execution, sketching out exactly what she needed to hold in mind and why, checking her own reasoning twice before trusting it enough to spend a scarce session confirming it. Red, she decided, not because red held any special significance in itself, but because it sat at the cheapest end of a spectrum she now understood, at least provisionally, well enough to aim toward deliberately rather than stumbling into by accident.

She wrote the plan into her notebook the night before, three clear sentences describing exactly what she intended to hold in her mind and in what order, because a test she hadn’t bothered to specify in advance was a test she couldn’t properly evaluate afterward, no matter how the result turned out. Specificity first. Intention second. Everything else, she hoped, would follow from getting those two things right.

She tested the theory the following week, deliberately, the way she tested anything once she had a hypothesis worth actually risking a session on, running the same sequence but holding a specific mental image in place of the vague generalised effort she’d been using before, red, dim, cheap, rather than simply willing the field to produce whatever it was inclined to produce on its own.

The result arrived slower than she expected and held longer than anything before it, a small, steady, unmistakably red glow that didn’t flicker, didn’t gutter, didn’t demand the same violent drain the white attempt had cost her. She held it for a full two minutes before she let it go on purpose, checking the meter rather than trusting her own sense of the duration, and found the cost, when she finally checked it against her own fatigue afterward, considerably lower than either of the two shorter, brighter attempts that had come before it.

She opened her eyes partway through the second minute, unable to resist confirming with her actual vision what the meter and her own body were already telling her, and found the small glow sitting in the cupped hollow of her palm exactly where she’d been holding it, steady, patient, throwing barely enough light to redden the lines of her own knuckles. It looked, she thought, distantly, less like a triumph and more like a coal left over from a fire that had mostly burned down, small and quiet and entirely undramatic, nothing at all like the guides’ breathless descriptions of a practitioner’s first successful working.

She closed her eyes again rather than watch it, trusting the meter over her own vision for the remainder of the attempt, because she’d learned months ago not to let a satisfying image substitute for an actual instrument reading, however much the image tempted her to keep looking.

You did it, Pip said, something unmistakably delighted in it. A genuine, stable, repeatable globe. Two minutes.

“It’s dim,” Nadia said, looking at the small red light steady in her own cupped hand, turning it slightly to check it from another angle. “Anyone who saw this would call it a failure. A weak result from someone who couldn’t manage anything brighter.”

Anyone who saw it wouldn’t understand what they were looking at, Pip said. You didn’t fail to make it bright. You chose not to. That’s an entirely different thing, and I don’t think most people would have had the discipline to make that choice on their first real success.

I want to say something else, if you’ll let me, it added, after a moment. I’ve read every account of a first successful globe in the archive, every published guide, every forum thread. Every single one describes it as bright, dramatic, a moment worth celebrating with everyone in the room. Nobody, in forty years of published material, describes doing exactly what you just did on purpose. I don’t think that’s because nobody else was capable of it. I think it’s because nobody else had a reason to want it.

Nadia turned that over, still watching the small red light where it sat cupped in her palm. “I had a reason,” she said, quiet. “I’ve had a reason for almost everything I’ve ever built this way. Cheap because cheap is what I could afford. Cheap because cheap is what actually works down here. Nobody ever handed me the option of doing it the expensive way and I stopped resenting that a long time ago.”

I don’t think you’re resenting it now either, Pip said. I think you might actually prefer it this way, genuinely, and not simply because it happens to be the only version you’ve ever been given a chance to try.

“Maybe,” Nadia said. “I don’t know yet if that’s a preference or just a habit I’ve gotten very good at not questioning.”

She sat with the distinction a moment longer, watching the small light steady in her palm, unwilling to settle the question tonight simply because it had occurred to her at a convenient moment. Some questions deserved more than the first honest answer that arrived. This one, she suspected, was going to take considerably longer than a single evening in a cold alcove to actually resolve, if it ever fully resolved at all.

You don’t have to decide it tonight, Pip said, gently. Some things are allowed to just sit unresolved for a while, quietly, without either of us forcing a conclusion neither of us has actually earned yet.

“I know,” Nadia said. “I’m letting this one sit. I just wanted to notice it was there, and now I have, and that’s all tonight actually needed from me.”

* * *

She sat with the small light a while longer, turning her hand slowly, watching the way it barely touched the darkness around it, a glow closer to a dying ember than anything a stranger would recognise as magic at all. It cost her almost nothing to hold, she realised, running the meter’s numbers one more time to be certain. Whatever the guides’ published curricula had assumed about a practitioner’s first working globe, none of them seemed to have considered that dim and cheap might simply be the correct engineering choice rather than a consolation prize for someone who couldn’t manage anything better.

She thought about every dead board she’d ever repaired using the exact same instinct, replacing a burnt component with the cheapest part that satisfied the actual tolerance rather than the flashiest one available, because a repair judged by how impressive it looked rather than how reliably it worked was a repair built for the wrong audience entirely. Nobody paying her ever asked whether the replacement capacitor matched the original in brand or prestige. They asked whether the appliance turned back on and stayed on, and she’d built six years of a reputation on understanding that distinction better than anyone else on this stretch of the Drive.

The globe, she decided, watching it fade at last under her own deliberate release, was simply the same principle applied somewhere she’d never expected to need it. Minimum viable wasn’t a compromise. It was the entire discipline, correctly practised, in a field that had apparently never once produced a practitioner willing to admit that bright and correct weren’t the same question at all.

“It’s better down here anyway,” she said, mostly to herself, letting the globe finally fade. “My eyes are already adjusted to the dark. Anything bright would ruin that the second it lit up. This barely touches it. I could work by this light for an hour and still see the Elite’s patrol coming from two hundred metres out.”

You didn’t plan for that advantage either, Pip said. You found it after the fact and it turned out to matter anyway.

“Most of the good decisions I’ve made this year turned out that way,” Nadia said. “I pick the cheap option because it’s cheap. The rest of the benefits just come along for free, if I’m lucky.”

She thought about the scooter as she said it, the whole absurd, accidental machine that had never once been designed for the speed it eventually reached, and about the ledger, built purely to solve a scheduling problem and now the only reason she had enough evenings to be sitting here at all. Neither of those had started as anything grand. Both had simply been the cheapest working solution to a problem that refused to wait for a better one, and both had ended up mattering considerably more than she’d ever expected when she first built them.

“I think that’s just how everything I’ve ever made actually works,” she said, slowly, working the thought out as she spoke it. “I never once set out to build something impressive. I set out to solve exactly the problem in front of me, as cheaply as I could survive solving it, and every single time, the cheap solution turned out to be doing more than I originally asked of it.”

That might genuinely be the truest thing you’ve said about yourself all year, Pip said, quiet.

“Maybe,” Nadia said. “Or maybe I’ve just never once had the budget to build anything the expensive way, so I stopped noticing the pattern was a pattern instead of just how things had to be, for me, every single time.”

* * *

She logged the full sequence that night, back at the bench, ruling out the entry with the same careful attention she gave anything she meant to trust later. Rung two, three seconds, unsustainable cost. Rung three, twenty seconds, white, unstable, expensive. Rung four, two minutes, red, stable, cheap, repeatable. Four confirmed data points now, each one building on the last, a curve she was only beginning to understand the shape of but could already see well enough to predict the next test she needed to run.

“Minimum viable,” she said, reading the entry back once it was finished, the phrase settling into place the way the right label always settled once she’d earned it properly. “Not the best globe I could make. The cheapest one that actually does the job.”

I like that phrase better than success, Pip said, quiet. Success sounds like it happened to you. Minimum viable sounds like something you built on purpose, which is closer to what actually happened tonight.

Nadia didn’t answer that directly, closing the notebook instead, the small red glow already fading from memory the way most of her private victories faded once the page had properly recorded them, filed alongside a season, a reclassification, and a body that never once stopped asking more of her than she had readily available to give it, one more quiet number added to a year that had already handed her more of them than she’d ever expected to carry at sixteen.

She sat at the bench a while longer before finally getting ready for bed, running the whole sequence back one last time the way she ran back anything she wanted to actually hold onto rather than simply record and forget. Three seconds. Twenty seconds, whitish, unstable. Two minutes, red, stable, cheap. A ladder with four rungs climbed now, each one earned the same careful way, no rung skipped, no shortcut taken that she hadn’t first tested and confirmed wouldn’t cost her more than it saved.

Five more rungs after this one, if the pattern in the archive holds, Pip said, quiet. You’re not close to finished.

“I know I’m not finished,” Nadia said. “I don’t need to be finished tonight. I just need tonight’s rung to actually hold, and it held.”

It held, Pip agreed. Cleanly, on the meter, and cheaply enough that you’re not sitting on the floor recovering from it right now the way you were an hour ago, two full minutes into a session that cost you a fraction of what twenty seconds of the wrong colour cost you last time.

“That’s the part I actually care about,” Nadia said. “Not that it worked once. That it worked in a shape I can afford to repeat, over and over, for as many nights as the schedule ever actually gives me, without spending a single evening I don’t have spare to give it.”

She turned off the bench light, the small red glow long since faded from both the alcove and her own memory of it, and let herself feel, for exactly as long as she was willing to allow it, the specific quiet satisfaction of a problem that had finally, after months of nothing, started answering back, patiently, on her own terms, exactly the way she preferred anything worth having to arrive.
