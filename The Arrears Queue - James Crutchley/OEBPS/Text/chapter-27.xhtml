Chapter 27: Her Own Rate

[LEVEL UP: 3]

She read the bracket sitting against the alcove wall, the queue drained down further than it had run in weeks, and felt the same flat, unglamorous arrival the number had brought her twice before, no fanfare, no sense of triumph, only a fact that had finally caught up to a body that had been earning it in small increments for months.

She checked the arithmetic before she let herself register anything else. Max HP up again, a genuine number this time rather than the marginal shift Level 2 had given her, the theoretical Window stretching further past the twenty seconds she could actually spend. She still couldn’t touch most of it. That hadn’t changed. What had changed was the size of the gap between what she owned and what she could use, a gap that kept getting wider even as both numbers climbed, and she filed the observation the way she filed anything that refused to simplify no matter how she turned it.

Three, Pip said, quiet, fully present in the alcove’s stillness. That’s three in under six months.

“I know,” Nadia said, and didn’t say anything else about it, because the season physical was in two days and she still had a full evening of testing ahead of her before the schedule let her rest.

She sat with the number a moment longer anyway, unable to entirely dismiss it the way she usually dismissed anything that wasn’t immediately useful. Three levels. Three brackets, each one costing her more than the last, each one adding a debt to the density column she still hadn’t found a way to pay down. She thought about the first bracket, months ago now, the flat unglamorous arrival of Level 2 and the small private satisfaction underneath the caution, and found tonight’s arrival carried less of that satisfaction than she expected, weighed down already by everything she knew was waiting for her Thursday.

You’re thinking about the physical, Pip observed.

“I’m thinking about the physical,” Nadia agreed. “Two days isn’t much runway to prepare for a conversation I don’t actually get to control the terms of, and I don’t like walking into a room without knowing which way it’s going to go before I get there.”

* * *

The physical ran at the school gymnasium on a Thursday afternoon, the whole wrestling team filing through in alphabetical order past three folding tables, height and weight, grip strength, a resting heart rate taken by a volunteer nurse who’d clearly done this circuit for twenty years and could find a pulse blindfolded. Nadia moved through the first two stations without incident, the numbers landing exactly where she expected them to land, and reached Val’s table at the end of the line already braced for the one part of the afternoon she couldn’t fully predict.

The gymnasium had the specific echoing hush of any large room being used for something quieter than it was built for, thirty wrestlers filing through in loose clusters, most of them bored, a few of them nervous in the particular way anyone got nervous around a blood draw regardless of how many times they’d done it before. Tomasz went through two spots ahead of her, complaining under his breath about the grip dynamometer reading low on a day he swore he’d slept fine, and she caught the specific irony of it without saying anything, a boy worried about a number reading slightly worse than expected standing two tables from a girl about to have the opposite problem.

She watched the line move with the same patient attention she gave any queue she was waiting in, cataloguing the room the way she catalogued anything, exits, sightlines, the specific fatigue on the volunteer nurse’s face by the time she reached the front of it, the accumulated tiredness of someone who’d already drawn blood from two dozen teenagers today and had at least ten more ahead of her before the afternoon ended.

Val had the bloodwork in front of her, a single printed sheet from the lab the district contracted for exactly this purpose, and she was reading it with the particular stillness of someone who had read a great many of these sheets before and had never once seen one arranged quite like this.

“Sit down a second,” Val said, not looking up yet.

Nadia sat, watching Val’s face rather than the sheet, reading the specific stillness of a professional working through a problem methodically before committing to a single word about it out loud. Val’s eyes moved down the page a second time, slower than the first pass, the kind of re-check a person only bothered with when the first read had produced a result they didn’t quite want to trust yet.

She’s reading it three times now, Pip observed, quiet, present enough on the gymnasium floor to track exactly where Val’s attention kept landing. She keeps going back to the same two lines, over and over.

Nadia didn’t answer, watching Val’s pen hover over the sheet without actually marking anything at all, the specific hesitation of someone deciding how to open a conversation they’d clearly been dreading since long before this particular Thursday afternoon had ever arrived.

* * *

“Your hemoglobin’s low,” Val said, finally, running a finger down the sheet. “Not dangerously low. Low enough that I’d expect you to be dragging through every practice this month, and you’re not. Your reticulocyte count is high, which tells me your body’s throwing out new red cells faster than it should need to, which usually means it’s losing the old ones faster than it should too.”

“I run hard,” Nadia said, which was true and also, she understood the moment she said it, not going to survive the next question.

“Everybody on this team runs hard,” Val said. “Nobody else’s panel looks like this. Your LDH is elevated. Your haptoglobin’s on the floor. That’s not a training effect. That’s a body breaking down red blood cells faster than the marrow can replace them, and the only thing I’ve ever seen do that outside a genuine blood disorder is repeated blunt mechanical trauma, the kind you’d get from, I don’t know, a car accident on a weekly basis.”

Nadia held very still, running the numbers Val had just handed her against everything she already knew about her own body, the iron hunger she’d stopped questioning months ago, the specific craving that arrived every time a session left her queue climbing hard. Val wasn’t wrong. Val was reading the exact signature of a body absorbing hits it had no medical record of ever taking, and there was no honest version of the explanation Nadia was in any position to give her.

She thought, sitting there under the fluorescent gymnasium lights, about every catwalk exchange the panel was quietly describing, forty points absorbed cleanly here, three hundred taken flat there, a season’s worth of contact that had never once been logged anywhere a doctor could read it, distilled now into four lab values a trained eye could interpret correctly without ever once guessing the actual mechanism behind them. It was, she thought, a strange kind of honesty, a body that couldn’t lie about what had happened to it even when the person carrying it had no intention of admitting anything out loud.

“You’ve gone quiet,” Val said, watching her across the table. “I’m not trying to corner you. I just need you to understand that I’m not going to pretend I didn’t see this.”

“I understand,” Nadia said, quiet, and meant it fully, because pretending Val hadn’t seen it would have been its own kind of dishonesty, one she wasn’t willing to add to everything else already sitting between them.

* * *

“Your creatine kinase is high too,” Val went on, when Nadia didn’t answer. “Muscle breakdown marker. Also consistent with trauma, or with training volume way past what I’ve got you logged for. Your ferritin’s low, which fits the iron loss, and your protein intake, based on what you’ve told me about your diet, shouldn’t be able to keep up with a repair rate this size at all. On paper, Nadia, you should be exhausted. You should be missing practices. You are, by every number on this sheet, a kid who’s taking a beating somewhere I don’t know about, and somehow still walking onto my mat every single day like none of it touches you.”

“I feel fine,” Nadia said, which was true in a way that did nothing to help either of them.

“I believe that you feel fine,” Val said, and there was something underneath the clinical flatness now, something that read, unmistakably, as worry rather than suspicion. “That’s actually the part that concerns me most. A body compensating this well for damage this consistent isn’t a body that’s healthy. It’s a body that’s very good at hiding how much trouble it’s actually in.”

Val set the sheet down flat on the table and folded her hands over it, the specific posture of someone about to say something she’d rehearsed at least once before saying it out loud. “I’ve coached for eleven years,” she said. “I’ve seen kids push their bodies past what they should, kids cutting too hard, kids training through injuries they shouldn’t be training through. I know what that looks like on paper. This isn’t that. The numbers don’t line up with overtraining. They line up with something happening to you that I don’t have a category for, and I want to be honest that not having a category for something scares me more than having one that’s bad.”

“I know it looks strange,” Nadia said, choosing the word carefully, testing how far honesty could stretch before it became a lie by omission instead of by commission.

“Strange is a generous word for it,” Val said, not unkindly. “I looked at four other panels from this team before I got to yours today. None of them looked anything like this. Whatever’s happening to you is happening only to you, as far as I can tell, and I don’t like sitting across from a kid whose body is telling me a story I can’t verify.”

* * *

She thinks it’s an eating disorder, Pip said, quiet, present enough in the gymnasium to have been tracking every line of the conversation. Or something adjacent to it. She’s not looking for the real cause. She doesn’t have a category that includes the real cause.

That’s actually the safest version of this conversation, if it matters, it added. Wrong, but safe. An eating disorder is a category the system knows how to hold without ever once looking for a classed anomaly underneath it. If she’s worried about the wrong thing, she’s worried about a thing that doesn’t point anywhere near the truth.

“It doesn’t feel safe from here at all,” Nadia said, low, under her breath, careful not to let Val catch the words.

I know, Pip said. I’m not saying it should feel safe. I’m saying the actual risk in this room is lower than it feels, which isn’t nothing, even if it doesn’t change how uncomfortable the next five minutes are still going to be for you either way.

Nadia didn’t answer out loud, watching Val flip the sheet over as though a second page might explain the first one, finding nothing there either.

“Is there something going on at home,” Val asked, gentler now, the question arriving with none of the clinical distance the numbers had carried a minute ago. “Or somewhere else. I’m not going to pretend I don’t know your file says group housing before it says a family address. I’m not asking to file a report. I’m asking because I’d like to help, if there’s something to actually help with.”

“Nothing’s going on,” Nadia said, and heard, saying it, how thin the sentence sounded against a panel that told a story this specific. “I eat what I can afford. I train hard. I heal fast. That’s the whole explanation.”

“Nobody heals this fast,” Val said, quiet.

* * *

She let the silence sit for a moment, because there was no honest next sentence available to her, and Val, watching her not answer, seemed to arrive at some private decision of her own about how far to push it today.

“I’m signing your clearance,” Val said, finally. “I want that clear. This isn’t me holding anything back. But I’m flagging the panel for a follow-up next season, and I want you eating more than whatever you’re currently managing, starting today, cut or no cut. Whatever’s doing this to your blood, it needs more protein and more iron than you’re giving it, not less.”

“Okay,” Nadia said, and meant the agreement honestly, because eating more was advice she could actually follow regardless of what Val believed the reason was.

Val signed the form, slid it across the table, and held her gaze a moment longer than the paperwork strictly required. “I’m not going to stop noticing,” she said. “I want you to know that too. I’m not looking for a story I can’t use. I’m just not going to stop watching.”

“I know,” Nadia said, quietly, and stood, gathering her bag from the floor beside the folding chair, buying herself a few seconds before she had to meet Val’s eyes again.

“One more thing,” Val said, before she’d gotten more than a step away. “If there ever is something you want to tell me. Anything. I want you to know the door’s open, no conditions attached to it. I’m not going to promise I won’t worry. I am going to promise I’ll listen before I do anything else.”

“Thank you,” Nadia said, and the word came out steadier than she felt saying it, because there was a version of that offer she genuinely wanted to take her up on and no version of the truth she could hand over that wouldn’t cost her everything the offer was trying to protect. She held the word in her chest a moment longer than she let herself hold most things, turning it over the way she turned over anything unexpectedly kind, before filing it away with everything else this year had taught her not to fully trust.

She gathered the signed form, and walked out of the gymnasium carrying a clearance that had cost her considerably more than the twenty minutes it had taken to obtain.

* * *

The second problem arrived that same evening, in the form of an email from the league office forwarded through Val’s account, a reminder that any wrestler within a specified margin of her registered weight class needed to confirm eligibility before the tournament roster locked, and a link to the reclassification form for anyone who wasn’t going to make it.

She read the email standing in the bay with her jacket still on, the day’s exhaustion settling into her legs the way it always settled after a physical that had asked more of her emotionally than physically, and felt the particular flat dread of a second deadline arriving on the heels of a first one she hadn’t even finished processing yet. One problem at a time had never once been how her weeks actually worked. They arrived in clusters, stacked, each one waiting politely for the last one to finish landing before announcing itself.

She set her bag down, hung the jacket on its hook, and sat at the bench without taking her boots off first, an unusual enough break in her own routine that she noticed it happening and didn’t bother correcting it.

She read the email twice at the bench, the numbers from the last three weigh-ins laid out in her own notebook beside it, and did the arithmetic she’d been avoiding since the density problem had first confirmed itself back in November. The mass wasn’t fat. It wasn’t water. It had climbed with every level, exactly as steadily as the site had climbed with her, and no deficit she was willing to survive was going to move it back down to fifty-two kilos before the roster locked.

You already know what the numbers say, Pip said, quiet.

“I know what they say,” Nadia said. “I’ve known for a week. I just didn’t want to be the one who filled out the form.”

* * *

The form itself was short, three fields and a signature line, a reclassification request that would move her into the next weight bracket up, public the moment it was filed, visible to the league, visible to every coach and wrestler she’d ever face this season, a documented change with her name attached to it and no explanation offered beyond a box that only asked for the new number, not the reason behind it.

She read through the whole form carefully, twice, before touching a single field, the way she read through any document she was about to sign her name to, checking for anything buried in the fine print that might ask for more than the three visible fields let on. A note near the bottom explained that reclassification outside the normal registration window required a coach’s countersignature and, if the change exceeded a certain threshold, a brief written justification submitted to the league office directly, a paragraph rather than a checkbox, the one part of the form that actually invited a story instead of just a number, the exact kind of narrow gate she’d spent the whole evening dreading might be waiting for her somewhere in the fine print.

She read the threshold twice, running her own numbers against it, and found, with a small measure of relief she hadn’t expected to feel about anything on this form, that her jump landed just inside the range that didn’t require the paragraph. A checkbox and a signature. No story owed to anyone.

That’s one fewer place for the paperwork to actually ask a question, Pip observed, watching her confirm the number a third time, then a fourth, before finally letting herself trust it. Small mercies.

“Small mercies,” Nadia agreed, and felt the tightness in her shoulders ease by a fraction, the first genuine relief the whole evening had offered her.

She sat with the form open on her phone for a long time before she started filling it in, running the calculation one more time the way she ran anything before committing to it. Staying at fifty-two meant a cut so severe it would flag on Val’s next panel before the season even ended, a body already breaking down faster than it could repair itself asked to survive on even less than it currently had. Moving up meant a new class, new opponents, a public record of the exact kind of change that invited questions, and precisely zero guarantee any of it would still fit her by the time the next tournament rolled around, if the mass kept climbing the way it had climbed every level so far.

“There isn’t a version of this that doesn’t cost something,” she said, mostly to herself, filling in the first field. “I’m just picking which cost I can actually survive paying.”

For what it’s worth, Pip said, I think this is the right choice. I don’t think the other one was survivable much longer.

“I know,” Nadia said, and finished the form, and sent it, and felt, in the moment after it left her hands, considerably less relief than the decision should probably have earned her.

She sat with the sent confirmation for a good while afterward, the small green checkmark on her screen doing nothing at all to touch the flat unease still sitting underneath it, patient, unresolved, entirely indifferent to how correctly she’d run the numbers to get here. It wasn’t regret exactly. She’d run the numbers correctly, she was certain of that much, and a correctly run number didn’t become wrong just because the outcome felt heavier than she wanted it to. It was something closer to the specific discomfort of watching a decision leave her hands and become real in the world, a private calculation now converted into a public fact that other people would read and react to without ever once knowing the arithmetic behind it.

You did the math right, Pip said, gently. I checked it myself, twice, before you sent it. I don’t think there’s a version of tonight where the feeling matches the correctness of the decision. I think that’s just what a hard call feels like, even the ones that are obviously correct, and even the ones you’d make again without hesitation given the exact same information.

“I know that too,” Nadia said, quiet. “Knowing it doesn’t make the feeling go away. It just means I don’t have to trust the feeling over the arithmetic, which is, most days, the entire difference between a decision and a panic.”

* * *

She didn’t go straight to bed that night. She sat instead with her own notebook open to a blank page, the one number she’d never once written down in six months of meticulous logging, and made herself do the arithmetic properly, the way she made herself do anything she’d been quietly avoiding.

Level 1 to Level 2 had taken most of six weeks. Level 2 to Level 3 had taken considerably less, the curve compressing rather than stretching the way she’d have expected any ordinary progression to behave. Six months, start to finish, Level 1 to Level 3, and somewhere in the public archive she’d read months ago while researching the guides, buried in a paragraph she’d skimmed past without registering its weight at the time, sat the number she made herself look up again now and confirm.

Median classed adult retires at Level 3 or 4. A whole working lifetime, spent, arriving at a number she’d reached in six months, at sixteen, with a level bracket she still hadn’t finished growing into, and no institution anywhere aware that the number had ever moved at all.

She read the sentence a third time, the way she read any figure she needed to actually believe rather than merely register, and made herself sit with the comparison properly instead of letting it slide past as an abstraction. A classed adult, she thought, meant someone who had trained deliberately, who had probably paid for instruction, who had likely spent years building toward exactly this number as a career milestone worth celebrating. She had none of that. She had a depleted site nobody else wanted, a body that ran an engine no guide had ever documented, and six months that had cost her more sleep and more calories than she liked to add up in a single sitting. The comparison didn’t make her feel accomplished. It made her feel, more than anything else, like she’d been quietly running a different race than everyone else this whole time, on a track nobody else could even see.

Do you want to know what the archive says about people who level this fast, historically, Pip asked, careful.

“Not tonight,” Nadia said. “I already know what it’s going to say. Somebody notices. Somebody wants to know why. I don’t need the specifics to know the shape of the answer, and knowing the specifics tonight would just cost me a night’s sleep I don’t have spare.”

* * *

She sat with the number for a long time, turning it the way she turned anything too large to process in a single pass, and found, when she finally reached the bottom of it, that the feeling waiting there wasn’t pride and wasn’t fear in any of the shapes fear usually took.

“Anyone who ever sees this number is going to want to do something about it,” she said, out loud, working through the thought the same way she worked through any conclusion that needed saying to actually land. “A test, a program, a file, an assessment I can’t opt out of. It doesn’t matter whether the thing they’d do is good or bad. It’s time. It’s attention. It’s somebody else deciding what happens to me next, and I don’t have room in this year for that, no matter how it’s dressed up.”

That’s not the reaction most people would have to a number like this, Pip said, careful.

“Most people want to be seen,” Nadia said. “I’ve never wanted that. I just want to be left alone long enough to actually get through the week.”

She closed the notebook on the number, the same flat closure she gave any entry once she’d recorded it honestly, and sat a moment longer in the quiet of the bay, the reclassification form already sent, the season physical already signed, a Level 3 bracket sitting behind both of them that nobody in either room had the framework to actually see.

She thought, before she finally let herself go to bed, about the specific shape of the day she’d just survived, two separate rooms and two separate forms, each one a small, controlled leak of information into a system that had no idea what it was actually holding. Val had a panel she couldn’t explain. The league had a number that would raise no questions at all, precisely because it fell just inside a threshold designed to catch something far more ordinary than the truth. Neither room had the whole picture. Neither room ever would, not from her, not tonight, not for as long as she had any say in the matter.

You survived today, Pip said, quiet, once the light was off and the bay had settled into its usual dark.

“I survived today,” Nadia agreed. “I don’t know yet what tomorrow costs for it. I never do. I just know today’s done, and the forms are filed, and I’m still exactly as unremarkable as I need to be, on paper, where it actually counts, and that has to be enough for tonight.”

* * *

The cafeteria the next day gave her the last piece of it, the craving arriving before she’d even reached the counter, sharper than anything she’d logged since October, a pull toward the darkest cuts of meat on the tray and nothing else that came close to satisfying it.

She built the plate as full as the cut allowed and found, halfway through it, that the cut wasn’t going to allow anywhere near enough. The new weight class bought her room she hadn’t had in months, and she still hadn’t adjusted the numbers properly, still eating against a target that no longer matched the body she was actually running, and she sat with the tray in front of her, hungrier than the food in front of her could touch, and did the only thing she knew how to do with a gap this size.

She ran the new numbers in her head while she ate, the fresh weight class allowing her, in theory, a genuine increase in what she could target every day without risking a single ounce of eligibility, and felt the specific frustration of a solution she hadn’t yet had time to actually implement. The form was filed. The room existed. Her own habits simply hadn’t caught up to it yet, still running on months of careful restriction that no longer matched the constraint actually governing her, a lag between what was true and what she’d built herself around that she was going to have to close by hand, tray by tray, over however many days it took her body to believe the new number was real.

You could eat more right now, Pip pointed out. Tonight. This tray. Nothing’s stopping you except the habit.

“I know,” Nadia said, reaching for a second serving anyway, testing the permission the way she tested anything new, cautiously, one small increment at a time.

She wrote it down, and planned to fix it tomorrow, and ate what was in front of her anyway, because tomorrow’s plan didn’t do anything for the hunger sitting in her chest today.

Priya slid into the seat across from her partway through, dropping her own tray down with the usual unhurried confidence, and glanced at Nadia’s plate with mild surprise. “You’re eating more than usual,” she said, not quite a question, more an observation offered the way Priya offered most observations, lightly, without any particular weight attached to it.

“New season,” Nadia said, which was entirely true in every sense that mattered and explained, deliberately, absolutely nothing real about any of it whatsoever.

“Makes sense,” Priya said, already moving on to whatever she’d actually sat down to talk about, launching into a story about a group project gone sideways with the same unhurried confidence she brought to everything, and Nadia let the moment pass, grateful, the way she was always grateful, for a friend who noticed things and never once needed them explained. She listened to the rest of the story with half her attention, nodding in the right places, the other half still quietly working through a schedule that had, once again, changed shape underneath her without asking permission first.

She noticed, Pip said, quiet, once Priya had drifted into a story about her own week.

“She notices things,” Nadia said. “That’s just Priya. It doesn’t mean anything’s actually at risk.”

I know, Pip said. I just wanted to properly log it. The list keeps growing whether either of us wants it to or not, and I don’t think ignoring that fact makes it grow any slower.

Nadia didn’t answer that, reaching instead for the last of her tray, the hunger still sitting somewhere under her ribs even now, quieter than it had been an hour ago but nowhere near finished, a debt that would still be waiting for her tomorrow no matter how carefully she planned around it tonight, one more column in a ledger that never once seemed to stop growing regardless of how diligently she kept it.
