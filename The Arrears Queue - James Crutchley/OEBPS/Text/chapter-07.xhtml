Chapter 7: Arrears

The evening had passed the way evenings usually passed after a hard day, quiet, ordinary, the router board she couldn’t trust her hands on set aside and a documentary playing on her phone with the sound low while she did nothing in particular, the small unremarkable window between finishing the day’s work and going to sleep. She hadn’t thought about the building or the thing on the catwalk in over an hour. The four lines on the bench under the light had started to feel, by nine o’clock, like a report she’d already closed out.

It started in her shoulder, the one that had taken the first hit, a deep ache waking up under the surface soreness like something had been sitting quiet all evening and had only now decided to announce itself properly.

She was sitting on the edge of the bed when it began, not doing anything in particular, and she pressed two fingers to the spot the way she’d pressed them an hour ago on the tree line, checking for the grinding wrongness of a fracture. It wasn’t there. Not yet. But the ache was deepening under her fingers even as she checked it, climbing from something she could have ignored to something that made her breath catch, in the space of maybe twenty seconds.

She stood up. Standing seemed like the correct response to pain that was getting worse rather than better, though she couldn’t have said why, only that sitting still felt suddenly like the wrong thing to be doing with her body, some instinct older than any diagnosis telling her that whatever was happening wanted her upright and moving rather than folded quietly onto a mattress.

The room, when she looked at it properly, hadn’t changed at all. Same bench, same wall, same three pieces of paper pinned above it that had nothing to do with any of this. It was strange, she thought distantly, how completely ordinary a room could stay while something inside your own body was busy rewriting every rule you thought you understood about it.

The second wave hit before she’d fully gotten to her feet, a flare across the side of her skull where the glancing blow had landed, and this one came with actual heat behind it, a burning that had no business belonging to a bruise, and her vision blurred at the edges for one long second before it cleared, colours pulling apart at the margins the way they did on a screen with a failing driver, the whole room briefly rendering in a version of itself that wasn’t quite true.

She put a hand against the wall to steady herself and found the plaster cool under her palm, an ordinary fact from an ordinary room, and held onto it the way she’d hold onto a known-good ground reference on a board she didn’t trust yet.

She sat back down, hard, on the edge of the bed, and made herself breathe, in through the nose, out through the mouth, the way Val had drilled into the whole team during the worst of the conditioning circuits. It didn’t help much. The ache in her shoulder had become something closer to a burn now too, spreading, and underneath both of them, low in her chest, a third sensation was building that she didn’t have a name for at all, a pressure that felt less like pain and more like something being paid out of an account she hadn’t known she was carrying.

* * *

She made it to the bathroom because the bathroom had a lock on the door and a light that worked, and because some instinct she didn’t examine told her this needed to happen somewhere nobody else in the building would walk in on.

By the time she got the door locked behind her, both hands were shaking.

She sat on the closed toilet lid because the floor still felt too far away to trust, and watched her own hands tremble in the fluorescent light, and tried to run the diagnostic the way she’d run it on anything else, symptom first, then source. Pain, escalating, at the exact two sites that had taken the two clean hits, nothing wrong at the sites that had taken the three she’d managed. A pressure in her chest that had no clear anatomical location at all. A tremor now, spreading past her hands into her forearms.

She made herself list it the way she’d list a fault, out loud, low enough that nobody through the wall would hear her, because saying a thing in order sometimes did what writing it down did, made it smaller by making it structured. Shoulder. Skull. The pressure with no address. The shake. Four symptoms, and not one of them present three hours ago, and every one of them getting worse rather than better in the time it had taken her to say them out loud, her own voice sounding thin and strange to her in the small tiled room, an unfamiliar register she didn’t like the sound of and had no spare attention left to fix.

The fluorescent tube overhead buzzed at the same slightly-wrong frequency as the one in the wrestling changeroom, and under any other circumstances she would have filed that the way she filed everything, mildly, at the back of her attention. Tonight it sat directly behind her eyes like something with a physical weight to it, every flicker of the tube landing as a small separate assault she had no defence against.

The tremor was the part that scared her, and she noted, with the small distant part of her mind that never entirely stopped working no matter what the rest of her was doing, that this was the first time all evening she’d let herself use that particular word about any of it.

The ache in her shoulder had a texture she recognised in an abstract, clinical way from the two collarbones she’d cracked over six years of this sport, the specific grinding pull of tissue actively rebuilding itself rather than simply sitting sore, except compressed, whatever should have taken days happening now inside minutes. She pressed her free hand flat against her sternum and felt her own heart going harder than it should have been going for someone sitting perfectly still, a physical fact that had no business existing next to the physical fact of feeling, underneath the pain, more awake than she had felt all day, some current running through her that hadn’t been there an hour ago.

She had felt something like this before, twice, both times after a wrestling tournament run on too little food and too much water cut, the specific shakiness and fog that came from a body that had spent everything it had and hadn’t been given anything back. This felt like that, except worse, except faster, except arriving on top of an injury that had somehow not been an injury three hours ago and was rapidly becoming one now in real time in front of her.

She stood up to get water from the tap and the room tilted.

Not dramatically. Not a collapse. Just a slow, sickening list to one side that her legs corrected for a half-second too late, and she caught herself against the sink with both hands, knuckles white on the porcelain, breathing hard through her nose, sweat breaking cold across the back of her neck despite the room being, if anything, slightly too cold.

The edges of her vision had gone grey.

* * *

She got the tap on and drank from her cupped hands, badly, water running down her chin and the front of her shirt, and it didn’t help, not immediately, not the way water should have helped anything.

Her legs went out from under her on the second attempt to stand fully upright, and she went down onto the bathroom floor without quite deciding to, one shoulder catching the edge of the tub hard enough that under any other circumstances it would have hurt considerably more than it registered right now, everything in her narrowing down to the grey tunnel her vision had become and the single central fact sitting inside it: something was wrong with her body at a scale she had no framework for, and she was completely alone with it, on a bathroom floor, with a locked door between herself and anyone who might have helped.

The tile was cold against her cheek, and some very small, very calm part of her, the part that had never once stopped assessing no matter how badly the rest of her was failing, noted that the cold felt good, felt like the only thing in the room that was behaving the way it was supposed to. Her pulse filled her ears, a heavy uneven drum that seemed to be coming from everywhere in her skull at once rather than from any single vessel she could locate. She tried to count it and lost the count twice, the numbers sliding away from her the way numbers slid away from her during the worst minute of the hardest cut she’d ever run, except this was worse than that, worse by an order of magnitude she didn’t have the clarity left to measure properly.

She thought, with a detachment that frightened her more than the symptoms themselves, that this was what it must feel like to actually die of something ordinary, not dramatically, not in a single moment, but in a slow accumulating failure of small systems that had simply run out of what they needed to keep running, and that nobody at Kelso House, not Marek two floors down with his pasta, not Ferreira gone home for the night, had the faintest idea that any of it was happening on the other side of a locked door.

There was no one coming. There had never been anyone coming, for anything, and tonight was simply the first time that fact had ever mattered quite this much.

She thought, distantly, that she should call someone. The thought arrived and then simply sat there, unactioned, because the second half of it arrived close behind the first: called who, and told them what. That she’d been attacked by a piece of animated machinery in a sealed municipal building she had no legal reason to be inside of, and that whatever it had done to her wasn’t showing up as any injury a paramedic would know how to treat. The sentence didn’t survive its own construction. There was no version of calling for help that didn’t end with her secret handed to a stranger in an ambulance, on a night when she couldn’t even reliably form the sentence to explain it.

She thought of Ferreira, two doors down and gone home hours ago. She thought of Bhandari, whose number was somewhere in her phone and whose entire relationship to her ran through forms and categories that had no box anywhere for this. She thought, absurdly, of Val, who had handed her a signature sheet three weeks ago and would have absolutely no idea what to do with a sixteen-year-old calling her at midnight from a bathroom floor. Every name she could think of led to the same dead end, a version of the truth that would cost her more than the crisis itself, and she set the whole idea down the way she set down a repair that wasn’t solvable with the tools she had on her, not because it stopped mattering but because dwelling on an unsolvable problem while a solvable one was actively killing her was, by any measure she trusted, the wrong use of what little clear thinking she had left.

Her hands were shaking too hard now to hold her phone steady even if she’d had anything to say into it, the screen swimming in and out of a focus that had nothing to do with the phone itself and everything to do with whatever was happening behind her own eyes.

The pressure in her chest had become something she could no longer separate from her own heartbeat, a hard, uneven hammering that felt too fast and too loud in a body that otherwise felt like it was shutting down floor by floor, and she recognised, from somewhere underneath the panic she was very deliberately not calling panic, the shape of what this actually was. Low blood sugar. A crash, the kind she’d felt the tail end of twice before and was now living through the genuine, unmanaged middle of, except stacked on top of whatever the fight itself had done to her, except so much worse than anything a hard tournament had ever put her through that the comparison barely held.

She needed sugar. She needed it now, in the next several minutes, or the grey at the edges of her vision was going to finish closing in on the middle.

* * *

She crawled. There was no dignity available to her and she didn’t look for any, dragging herself across the bathroom tile to the door, fumbling the lock with fingers that no longer wanted to cooperate with anything as precise as a small mechanism, three attempts before the bolt actually gave, and out into the hallway on her hands and knees because standing was no longer a plan her body was willing to execute.

The hallway light overhead was the same one that had been dead since before she arrived, and for one disoriented second she registered its absence as another symptom, another system failing, before the more sensible part of her caught up and reminded her it had simply always been out. She used the wall instead, dragging one shoulder along the paint the whole way, leaving, she vaguely registered, a faint damp mark where sweat had soaked through her shirt.

The stairs down to the kitchen felt like the longest distance she had ever covered in her life. She took them sitting, one at a time, lowering herself down each step rather than trusting her legs to hold her upright on the way, a controlled slide from tread to tread that jarred every ache in her body fresh with each landing, her vision narrowing further with every one, the stairwell light two floors down swimming in and out of focus like something viewed through water. Twice she stopped entirely, forehead against her knees, breathing in a ragged four-count she remembered from somewhere in Val’s conditioning drills, and made herself start again before the stopping turned into something she couldn’t restart out of.

The kitchen was empty, mercifully, at this hour, the good steel counter and the deep sink lit only by the small light over the stove that somebody left on overnight out of habit. She hauled herself up against the counter using the handle of the oven, her legs barely holding her weight even braced, knocked a box of something off the shelf reaching for the cupboard above it and didn’t stop to see what it was, the clatter of it hitting the floor sounding, in her narrowed hearing, much louder and much further away than it actually was. In the cupboard Marek used for his own things she found a jar of honey she recognised because she’d watched him use it in tea more than once, and beside it, which she registered only after, a box of the crackers he sometimes had with soup, which she grabbed too, some animal part of her brain already reaching past the immediate crisis toward whatever came after it.

She didn’t measure it. She didn’t use a spoon. She got the lid off with hands that had stopped being steady enough for fine motor work entirely, dropping it once, and ate it directly from the jar with her fingers, three, four mouthfuls, more than she would ever have taken from someone else’s food under any circumstances that weren’t this one, honey running down her wrist in a way that would have embarrassed her on any ordinary evening and didn’t register as anything at all tonight. She got through half a sleeve of the crackers after that, chewing without tasting them, mechanical, the way she’d eat between rounds of a tournament when eating was a task rather than an appetite.

She sat on the kitchen floor with her back against the cupboard door and waited, the jar still open beside her, for something to change. She kept both hands pressed flat against the cool tile, grounding herself against it the way she’d once been taught to ground herself against a mat when a room started spinning after a hard takedown, and counted her own breaths instead of her pulse, because her pulse kept sliding away from her count and her breath, at least, she could still control on purpose.

It took what felt like a very long time and was, by the clock on the stove when she finally trusted herself to read it, closer to eleven minutes.

The tremor eased first, a fine unsteadiness replacing the violent shake that had put her on the floor to begin with. Then the grey at the edges of her vision began, very slowly, to recede, the kitchen resolving back into full colour and full focus one degree at a time, the good steel counter and the deep sink and the laminated cleaning sheet on the wall all reassembling themselves out of the blur they’d been a few minutes ago. Her heartbeat, when she found it with two fingers against her own wrist, was still fast but no longer felt like something trying to escape her chest, the count holding steady enough now that she could actually finish it.

The ache in her shoulder and the burn along the side of her skull hadn’t gone. But they had stopped climbing. They sat now at a level that felt, comparatively, almost bearable, the kind of pain she could imagine functioning around rather than the kind that had put her on a bathroom floor twenty minutes ago, and beneath the ache, distinctly, something that felt less like injury and more like work being done, a low steady hum of activity in both sites that she had absolutely no medical vocabulary for and no intention of looking away from either.

She ate a second, smaller handful of the honey anyway, and a few more of the crackers, replaced the lid on the jar, and put it back exactly where she’d found it, angled the same way, the cracker box behind it rather than in front, because some habits didn’t switch off even when everything else about the last hour had. She washed the honey off her hands and wrist at the sink, the water running warm this time rather than the cold shock she’d needed it to be twenty minutes ago, watching it swirl pink at first, faintly, from a scrape on her palm she hadn’t noticed getting, before it ran clear, and checked her own face in the dark reflection of the kitchen window. Pale. Steadier than she felt. Nothing that would have told a stranger walking in right now anything had happened here at all.

* * *

She sat on the kitchen floor for a long while after that, not moving more than she had to, letting her body finish whatever it was doing without asking it to do anything else at the same time. The stove clock ticked over in silent minute-long increments she watched more than she needed to, a way of anchoring herself to something external and reliable while everything internal continued doing whatever it was doing without asking her permission.

She caught herself, at one point, running her thumb over the honey jar’s lid where she’d set it back down, checking that it was sealed properly, that there was no ring of stickiness left on the shelf below it, a small compulsive tidiness that felt oddly stabilising, proof that some part of her was still capable of caring about a detail that small. She left a note in her head to pay Marek back for it somehow, a jar of his own honey, unasked, whenever she next had the spare cash for it, a debt she intended to settle even though he would never know it existed.

The pressure in her chest that had felt, an hour ago, like an account being paid down had settled into something quieter now, present but no longer escalating, and when she finally worked up the nerve to check her shoulder again, pressing two fingers into the same spot she’d checked three times already tonight, the ache had a texture to it that felt, for the first time, like something actually healing rather than something actively getting worse. She rolled the shoulder experimentally, a slow full rotation, and it moved cleanly, no catch, no grinding, only the deep residual soreness of the kind of injury that was already most of the way through mending itself rather than one that had happened twenty minutes ago.

She got herself back upstairs eventually, slower than the crawl down had been but under her own power the whole way, one hand trailing the wall out of caution rather than necessity, and sat back down at the bench, the fault report from four hours ago still open under the light exactly where she’d left it, four lines in her own handwriting that had been written by a version of her who hadn’t yet understood what any of it meant.

She picked up the pen.

She wrote it the way she wrote up any repair, cause traced back from symptom, no speculation past what she could actually support. The two clean hits had produced no injury at the time of contact. No blood, no visible swelling, nothing a person watching her walk out of that building would have flagged as anything worse than a hard fall. And then, roughly four hours later, without warning and without anything triggering it that she could identify, an escalating pain response had begun at the exact two sites those hits had landed, and nowhere else, arriving all at once rather than distributed across the time between contact and now the way a normal injury would have distributed it.

She underlined the word timing and sat with it a moment, because timing was the whole shape of the thing. Not that she’d been hurt. She’d expected to be hurt the instant the first blow landed, had braced for it and been confused when it hadn’t come. What she hadn’t expected, what nothing in her experience had prepared her to expect, was that the injury could simply wait. Stack up somewhere out of reach, invisible, unfelt, and then collect on itself hours later, all of it landing in the space of about twenty minutes instead of settling in gradually the way an ordinary body processed an ordinary hit.

She wrote the second half of it more carefully, because this was the part that had nearly killed her rather than merely hurt her. The delay had a cost attached to it beyond the pain itself, a metabolic cost, sudden and severe enough to put her on a bathroom floor with her blood sugar cratering in a way she recognised and had never once seen come on that fast or that hard, faster than the worst cut she’d ever run, faster than made sense for a body that had eaten a normal dinner only a few hours before any of this started. Whatever had been rebuilt in her shoulder and the side of her skull in those twenty minutes had drawn on something, drawn on it hard and fast and all at once, and her body had very nearly not had enough of whatever that something was to spare.

And the last piece, the piece she wrote slowest of all because it was the piece she trusted least and needed most: that the crash had resolved, and resolved quickly, once she’d gotten sugar into herself, honey and crackers eaten off a kitchen floor at eleven at night, and that the pain itself was already fading in a way no injury she’d ever sustained before tonight had faded, hours rather than days, and accelerating even as she wrote the sentence describing it. If this held, if tonight wasn’t some strange one-time reaction to a strange one-time night, then whatever had happened to her body down in that building came with a bill attached, payable later, payable in a currency that looked a great deal like calories and looked, from where she was sitting right now, dangerously easy to underestimate.

She underlined nothing else. She didn’t need to. The pattern sat there on the page in her own plain handwriting, five lines now instead of four, complete enough to work from even though she understood almost none of the mechanism behind it, and she read it back once, twice, the way she read back any diagnosis before she trusted herself to act on it, checking each line against what she actually remembered rather than what would have made the tidiest story.

Damage did not happen when it happened. It happened later, all at once, and it cost more than the injury itself to process, and whatever was doing the processing worked fast enough that by morning, she suspected, there would be nothing left on her body to show for any of it at all. She thought of the three hits she’d managed, the ones she’d based and angled and absorbed into structure instead of flesh, and understood, with the same flat clarity that had gotten her through every other diagnosis she’d ever run, that those three had cost her nothing tonight because there had been nothing there to collect, that the entire crisis on the bathroom floor traced back to exactly two moments where her own positioning had failed her, and that the difference between surviving this comfortably and nearly dying of it on a kitchen floor had come down, in the end, to inches and angles she already knew how to control.

She capped the pen and looked at her own hands, steady now, no trace of the tremor that had nearly put her on a kitchen floor for good, and turned the page over to a fresh sheet, because the notes she’d just written were a record of what had happened and not yet a plan for what came next, and a plan was the part she actually knew how to build.

Whatever this was, she was going to need to understand it a great deal better before she ever went back out there again. She was going to need food she didn’t currently have money for, in quantities she hadn’t yet worked out, and a much better sense of exactly how much a single clean hit actually cost before she let herself anywhere near that building a second time. She was going to need, more than anything else, to be better at the one thing that had made the whole difference tonight, the thing standing between three hits that had cost her nothing and two that had nearly cost her everything, and that at least was a variable she already knew how to train.
