Chapter 37: The Gap in the Table

Eleven days left, her ribs mostly healed, Level 4 sitting quietly in a place no institution in her file had a column for, and Nadia sat at the bench with the notebook open to the log she hadn’t touched properly in three weeks.

School that day had passed in the same low grey hum as every other school day this month, a chemistry test she’d finished with time to spare, a lunch she’d eaten without needing to run any arithmetic first, Priya beside her the whole hour without either of them raising the page of names sitting folded in Nadia’s bag. Ordinary, in the specific way ordinary had come to mean something different this year than it had meant a year ago, a held breath dressed up as a normal afternoon. She’d walked home, dropped her bag, and come straight down without eating dinner first, because the plan she’d been carrying all day had needed somewhere quiet to actually happen, and quiet, lately, only existed two hundred metres past the west junction or nowhere at all.

Rung four. Two minutes, red, stable, cheap, repeatable. The entry sat there in her own handwriting, dated from before the equipment room, before the letter, before any of it, and she read it now the way she read any old data, checking it for drift, checking whether the girl who’d written it still agreed with the numbers.

You’ve got eleven days and you’re choosing to spend tonight on the globe, Pip observed, careful, neither approving nor arguing. I want to understand the reasoning before we start, not after.

“Because it’s the one column that’s still entirely mine,” Nadia said. “Everyone else gets a vote on the rest of my file. Nobody gets a vote on this one. If I don’t use the time I have left on something I actually control, I’m going to spend all eleven days controlling nothing at all.”

The rest of the file sat exactly where she’d left it, unresolved and indifferent to how much of her attention it consumed. No activity had come back pre-approved. The referral still showed active on the one portal she had access to, no update since the initial letter, a status field that simply read under review in the same flat font the Ministry used for everything, revealing nothing about what review meant or how long it planned to take. Nine names still sat on a page at home, a web of pencil lines she hadn’t added to since the lunch table but hadn’t stopped watching either. None of it had moved in three weeks. All of it was going to keep not moving whether she spent tonight down here or lying awake at Kelso House running the same arithmetic for the hundredth time.

“I’d rather have one number that moves,” she said, quiet, mostly to herself, and opened the notebook properly.

* * *

She worked through the leakage problem first, the fault she’d shelved back in October and never had the margin to chase properly, and it went faster than she expected once she actually sat down and gave it the attention it had been waiting for.

The globe drew more than it output. She’d known that in principle for months, the way she’d known any system had losses somewhere before she’d traced exactly where. Tonight she traced it the way she traced a dead rail on a board, methodical, one junction at a time, isolating the draw from the emission and watching the gap between them on the meter until the shape of the loss finally resolved into something she could name.

She started where she always started, with the assumption most convenient to her own competence, that the loss was radiative, heat bleeding off the draw the way heat bled off any inefficient circuit, and spent twenty minutes disproving her own first guess the way she disproved any hypothesis that turned out to be wrong, methodically, without attachment to having been right. The temperature differential across the alcove wasn’t nearly large enough to account for the gap. Whatever was eating the difference between draw and output wasn’t leaving as heat, wasn’t leaving as sound, wasn’t showing up on any reading the meter was actually built to take.

That left the possibility she’d been avoiding since October, not because the logic was hard to follow but because following it all the way to its conclusion meant admitting the two projects sitting in her notebook, filed under separate headings for six months, had never actually been separate at all.

“It’s not radiating away,” she said, out loud, working through it. “It’s not heat loss either, not enough to account for the whole gap. Something’s absorbing it before it ever reaches the globe.”

Absorbing it where, Pip asked.

She sat with that a long moment, meter in one hand, the old ache under her sternum that she’d stopped consciously registering months ago suddenly, uncomfortably, present again in her attention. “Here,” she said, quiet, one hand coming up to rest flat against her own chest without deciding to. “It’s going into the same place the core-formation guide keeps asking me to put things. I’ve been treating the light and the structure like two separate projects. They’re not. They never were.”

* * *

She logged Rung five the way she logged anything, terse, exact, the format she’d used for every entry since October. Rung five. Ten minutes, dim red, stable. Leakage traced to core substrate draw, not radiative loss. Draw and structure are the same account.

Ten minutes hadn’t cost her the way ten minutes should have, going by the old curve, and she’d sat with that discrepancy a while before trusting it, the same suspicion she gave any measurement that came in cheaper than the model predicted. She reran it twice, the meter agreeing with itself both times, the ache under her sternum barely registering above its usual low background hum, and understood, sitting with the second confirmation, that the leakage hadn’t just explained the loss. It had explained why every previous rung had felt harder than the arithmetic afterward ever seemed to justify. She’d been fighting her own structure for months without knowing the structure was there to fight.

You look like you want to apologise to something, Pip observed, careful.

“I want to apologise to six months of Tuesdays,” Nadia said. “I spent all of them assuming I was just bad at this. Turns out I was routing power into a wall I didn’t know existed and calling the waste a personal failing.”

That’s a very you thing to be upset about, Pip said. Most people would be relieved. You’re upset that the inefficiency had an explanation you didn’t find sooner.

“Both things can be true,” Nadia said, and turned the page to start the formalism properly.

She sat back from the page and looked at the whole table properly, six confirmed rungs stacked one under the other, six months of Tuesday nights and stolen hours compressed into six terse lines, and felt, reading it the way she’d felt reading her own six-month level curve back in January, the specific vertigo of a number that had quietly become considerably larger than the person who’d started collecting it had ever expected.

What happens now, Pip asked.

“Now I do the part I’ve been putting off since the leakage made sense,” Nadia said. “I stop treating each rung as its own separate experiment and I actually build the formalism. A form for the law, not just the six data points sitting under it.”

* * *

She worked the units the way she’d worked units in physics since Grade Nine, cost against duration, draw against structure, stripping away everything specific to any one rung until only the relationship between the quantities remained, dimensionless, general, true regardless of which rung had produced it.

It was the same method she’d have used on a datasheet for a component she’d never actually held, deriving a rating from the shape of the numbers around it rather than from a direct measurement she didn’t yet have the tool to take. She wrote the six confirmed rungs down the left margin, cost and duration in adjacent columns, and worked the ratio between them pair by pair, watching for the pattern the way she watched for a pattern in anything, patient, methodical, refusing to see a shape until the numbers had actually earned it.

Rung two to Rung three, the ratio ugly, the cost climbing far faster than the duration had. Rung three to Rung four, uglier still, the white, unstable light of the third rung costing her considerably more than the stable red of the fourth ever had, right up until she remembered, sitting with the numbers, that Rung three had been logged in November, deep in the cut, every single measurement from that entry already contaminated by a seventy percent clearance penalty she hadn’t bothered stripping out before now.

She recalculated with the deficit removed, substituting her best estimate of what an uncut body would have logged instead, and watched the ugly ratio settle, entry by entry, into something considerably closer to a straight line than anything she’d expected the raw data to hand her.

Cost scaled with duration to the first power, not the square she’d half expected from the early data’s ugly curve. That had been the deficit talking, she understood now, not the underlying law, the caloric restriction of a season she’d spent cutting weight actively lying to every measurement she’d taken during it. Strip the deficit out, and the true relationship was almost boring in its simplicity: linear, once the leakage was properly accounted for and routed into the one place it had always actually been going.

“If it’s linear,” she said, slowly, working the arithmetic out loud the way she worked anything that mattered, “then Rung five to Rung six isn’t a five-times jump in difficulty the way four to five was. It’s a straight extrapolation. If the leakage really is core substrate and the core’s already got a partial channel open from tonight’s work, the cost of an hour should sit close enough to affordable that I could reach it tonight, not in another six months of incremental testing.”

That’s not in any guide, Pip said, quiet. I checked, before you even finished the sentence. Nobody who’s ever written about this has documented a linear relationship past Rung four. Every account treats the back half of the ladder as exponential, brutal, months apart at best.

I want to make sure I understand what you’re actually claiming, it added, careful, methodical, running its own version of the same check she’d just run on herself. You’re not saying the guides are wrong. You’re saying they’re describing a different population, one that never routes the leakage anywhere, and the exponential curve they all report is the cost of carrying a permanent unrouted loss on top of the light itself.

“That’s exactly what I’m saying,” Nadia said. “The curve isn’t a property of the light. It’s a property of ignoring where the light’s losses actually go. Nobody in any of those accounts was building a core at the same time. They didn’t have anywhere for the leakage to be routed to, so it just kept compounding, and they called the compounding the nature of the problem instead of an artefact of their specific situation.”

“Because every account was written by someone who wasn’t simultaneously building a core,” Nadia said. “They hit Rung four, the cost curve turned vicious, and they stopped, because the standard path doesn’t route the leakage anywhere useful. It just bleeds. I’m not on the standard path. I haven’t been on the standard path since the metabolic collision made that obvious back in November.”

* * *

She sat with the prediction a long moment, the way she sat with any result that felt too clean to trust on the first pass, and made herself find the hole in it before she let herself believe it.

“If I’m wrong,” she said, “the worst case is the leakage isn’t fully routed yet, the cost curve snaps back to exponential halfway through, and I spend a lot of margin on a globe that never gets past twenty minutes.”

And the core, Pip said. What happens to the core if the prediction’s wrong.

“Nothing different than if I’m right,” Nadia said, slow, working through the actual mechanism rather than the hope sitting underneath it. “The structure doesn’t know which experiment it’s funding. Whatever channel opens tonight opens whether the globe reaches an hour or gives out at twenty minutes. The light’s just the gauge. It was always just the gauge. Tonight I’m not testing whether I can make light. I’m testing whether my model of the underlying mechanism is actually correct, and the light’s just how I’ll know.”

She said it plainly, the way she said anything she’d already made peace with before saying it out loud, and felt, underneath the plainness, the specific cold weight of a decision she wasn’t performing courage about, only stating. There was no dramatic flourish left in it by the time the sentence reached her own ears, only the flat shape of a choice she’d already made sitting somewhere further back than the words themselves, in the same place she’d made every other choice this year that nobody with the authority to stop her had ever gotten the chance to weigh in on.

That’s the part that worries me, Pip said. Not the globe. The gauge is telling you something is going to happen inside your own chest regardless of what the light does, and you’re choosing to let it happen tonight because the arithmetic says the cost is affordable.

“Yes,” Nadia said, plainly, no hesitation in it. “That’s exactly what I’m choosing.”

Can I ask why tonight specifically, Pip said. Not the eleven days. I understand the eleven days. I mean why, out of every night you’ve had margin to spend, it’s this one.

She thought about that longer than she expected to need to, turning the notebook’s cover over once in her hands. “Because I’m not going to get a better night than this one,” she said, finally. “Ribs healed. Level four settled. Sleep banked, food banked, no cut eating the clearance out from under either number. If the formalism’s right, tonight is the cheapest this experiment will ever be. If I wait for a night that feels less frightening, I’ll be waiting for a night that never actually comes, because there isn’t a version of this year where nothing frightening is happening somewhere else in my file at the same time.”

That’s honest, Pip said, quiet. I don’t think I have a better argument against it than the one you just made for it.

“Then let’s go,” Nadia said, and stood, notebook and meter already in hand, the alcove’s cold passage waiting exactly where it had waited every other night this year, patient, unchanged, entirely indifferent to the fact that tonight was going to be different from every night that had come before it.

* * *

She settled into the alcove’s cold wall the way she’d settled into it a hundred times before, meter on its shelf, notebook open to a fresh page, and ran through the guide’s configuration one final time before she committed to it, checking each step against the formalism she’d just derived rather than against memorised habit.

The ache started before the meter registered anything at all, a dull, deep pressure under her sternum, unreachable, exactly the way the guide’s vaguer passages had always warned it might feel and exactly the way she’d never quite let herself believe until this specific moment, sitting in the cold with her back against stone and a channel opening somewhere she couldn’t see, couldn’t touch, and couldn’t, she understood with total clarity, stop.

Something’s happening, Pip said, careful, watchful, its voice pitched at the exact register it used for anything it didn’t yet have a category for. I don’t have a clean reading on it. It isn’t the meter. It’s something else.

“I know,” Nadia said, breath tight. “I can feel it too.”

* * *

The globe caught at the four-minute mark, past Rung four’s old ceiling without any of the strain that had defined every previous session, a small, stable red light sitting steady in the air in front of her the way it had a dozen times before, except tonight the light didn’t waver, didn’t cost her the way it always cost her, simply held, drawing on an account that had, somewhere in the last several minutes, quietly become considerably larger than it had been an hour ago.

Six minutes. Ten. Pip called the marks out loud, flat, careful, and she sat through each one with her attention split three ways, the meter, the light, and the deepening ache under her sternum that had settled now into something less like pain and more like weight, a permanent new mass she hadn’t asked for and couldn’t set back down.

Fifteen minutes past Rung five’s old ceiling, and the ache had stopped deepening at all, holding steady at the same dull, unreachable pressure it had settled into somewhere around minute six, and she let herself, cautiously, log that as data rather than as relief. A cost that plateaued rather than climbing was exactly what a linear relationship should have predicted once the initial channel finished opening. She watched it hold for another full minute before she trusted the reading enough to write it down.

Twenty minutes. Thirty. The cold of the alcove had stopped registering as cold at some point in the last quarter hour, her own body running warmer than the stone around her, the light’s draw pulling heat from somewhere she’d have expected it to pull from months ago and didn’t anymore, the whole system settling into an equilibrium she hadn’t predicted but recognised, once she saw it, as exactly the kind of thing a properly routed channel ought to produce. The cost curve held linear the entire way, exactly as the formalism had predicted, no vicious exponential turn, no sudden collapse, and somewhere past the forty-minute mark she stopped being surprised by the number and started simply watching it climb, the way she watched any confirmed hypothesis stop being a hypothesis and become, plainly, a fact.

Forty-five minutes. Fifty. Her legs had gone stiff against the cold stone floor, an ordinary discomfort she barely registered against everything else her attention was currently holding, and she shifted her weight without breaking focus, the small automatic correction of a body that had spent six years learning to manage discomfort without letting it become a distraction.

* * *

The globe held at one hour.

She let it burn a full ten seconds past the mark, just to be certain, just to watch the number actually hold rather than trusting the instant it arrived, and stared at the small, steady, unimpressive red light hovering above her open palm, no brighter than it had been at minute four, no larger, nothing about it visually distinguishing an hour of sustained draw from four minutes of it except the number sitting in her own notebook and the new, permanent weight lodged under her sternum.

It was not, by any ordinary measure, an impressive thing to look at. A dim red glow the size of a coin, the kind of light a phone screen would have outshone without trying, and she found herself, watching it, thinking of every guide she’d ever read that described the goal of this whole endeavour in terms of brightness, output, spectacle, and understanding, for the first time with total clarity, that none of those guides had ever actually been written by someone who’d needed the light to be cheap more than they’d needed it to be bright.

Then she let it go, releasing the draw the way the guide described releasing anything, deliberate, controlled, no snap, no collapse, simply an ending she had chosen rather than one the cost had forced on her.

One hour, Pip said, and there was something in its voice she’d rarely heard from it, not quite disbelief, closer to the specific reverence of watching a number arrive that the archive had never once recorded arriving this early, this cheaply, this alone in a flooded tunnel underneath a decommissioned pumping station. Nadia. Nobody has this. I checked every account I could reach while you were holding it. Nobody reaches Rung six inside a year, let alone inside the time you’ve actually had.

“I had a reason nobody else had,” Nadia said, quiet, and pressed one hand flat against her sternum, feeling the new weight sitting there, permanent, undeniable, hers now in a way nothing about tonight was ever going to let her return. “I wasn’t trying to make light. I was trying to solve for the thing making the light, and it turned out the thing making the light was already inside me, half-built, waiting for someone to finally route the leakage somewhere it could actually use.”

Mendeleev left gaps, Pip said, slow, working through the thought out loud the way it did when a comparison was still assembling itself. He didn’t discover gallium. He said something had to exist with these properties, in this exact position, and then somebody else went and found it years later and it matched. I keep thinking about that, because you didn’t discover the leakage either, not really. You predicted where it had to be going, from the shape of everything around it, and then you built the proof yourself instead of waiting for someone else to find it.

“I didn’t have anyone else to wait for,” Nadia said. “Every guide I’ve ever read was written by someone who hit the wall at Rung four and stopped, because the standard path doesn’t give you a reason to keep pushing past it. I had a reason. The reason was sitting under my own ribs the entire time, and I just hadn’t connected the two projects until tonight forced the connection.”

* * *

She logged the entry last, hands steadier than she expected them to be given everything else her body was currently trying to process at once. Rung six. One hour, red, stable, cheap, repeatable. Formalism confirmed: cost scales linearly with duration once core substrate draw is properly routed. Core: partial structure, active, cannot be paused or reversed.

She read the last line back twice, the way she read anything that mattered, and found no version of it that read any less permanent on the second pass than it had on the first.

“I can’t undo this,” she said, out loud, mostly to hear the sentence exist somewhere other than inside her own skull. “Whatever channel opened tonight stays open. It’s going to keep drawing whether I use the globe again or not.”

I know, Pip said, quiet, present, entirely hers in the way it only ever was down here. I don’t think either of us fully understood, sitting down here an hour ago, exactly what tonight’s prediction was actually going to cost to confirm.

“I understood,” Nadia said. “I ran the arithmetic honestly. I knew the structure doesn’t care which experiment funds it. I chose it anyway, because the light was never actually the thing I was testing.”

She sat a long moment longer in the alcove’s cold quiet, one hand still resting over the new, permanent weight under her sternum, and let herself, finally, feel the whole shape of what an hour of red, stable light had actually confirmed: not a globe. A gap in a table nobody else had ever filled, built with the one tool she’d had that nobody else building toward the same guide had ever brought to the problem, and now, whether she wanted it tonight or not, hers to carry for however long it took the rest of it to finish forming.

* * *

“What does this actually mean, going forward, practically,” she asked, finally, once the immediate shock of the number had settled enough to let her think in full sentences again. “Tomorrow. The day after that.”

I don’t have a complete answer, Pip said. I have the pieces the guide already gave you, before tonight made them personal. Field signature, from now on, all the time, not just during a session. Thermal load stacked on top of whatever the Arrears queue is already running. A density change small enough the guide calls it negligible and large enough that you’re weighed twice a week by someone who’s already worried about every gram you carry.

“And the ache,” Nadia said.

And the ache, Pip agreed. I don’t think that one goes away. I think that one is just what it feels like now, all the time, to be you, from tonight forward.

She sat with that, turning it over the way she turned over any new permanent fact about her own body, cataloguing rather than mourning, because mourning had never once bought her anything a clear-eyed inventory couldn’t buy more cheaply. “I’ll need to actively suppress the signature near anything instrumented,” she said. “School. Bhandari’s office, if they’ve got a screening rig, which I’ve never actually checked. The medical appointment, whenever it finally lands, which is going to be the single worst place in the world to be carrying a field signature I don’t fully understand yet.”

That thought had occurred to me too, Pip said, quiet. I don’t have a way to make it smaller than it actually is. I can only tell you it’s there, and let you decide what to do with the knowing.

* * *

She packed the notebook and meter away slowly, her body’s stiffness from the hour on the cold floor finally registering now that her attention had freed up enough to notice it, and stood, testing her own balance the way she tested it after any session, cataloguing what the night had cost against what it had bought.

Eleven days left, unchanged. A referral still open, unchanged. Nine names on a page at home, unchanged, a spring formal flyer Hana had mentioned still sitting unanswered somewhere in the back of her mind. And underneath all of it, now, a new permanent fact that no institution in her entire file would ever have a column for, a structure half-built inside her own chest that was going to keep drawing whether she used the light again or not, whether she wanted it tonight or not, for as long as it took the rest of it to finish arriving.

“I don’t regret it,” she said, out loud, testing the sentence carefully the way she tested any claim before she let herself believe it, checking it twice against everything the night had actually cost her. “I want to be honest about that. Everything else in my file this month has happened to me. This is the one thing I actually chose, eyes open, knowing exactly what it was going to cost before I paid it.”

I believe you, Pip said. I still wish the cost had been smaller.

“So do I,” Nadia said. “Wishing doesn’t change what the formalism said was true. It just means I get to be honest about not loving the answer while I still act on it.” She shouldered her bag, meter and notebook settled back inside it where they belonged, and took one last look at the alcove before turning toward the passage. “That’s most of what this whole year has actually been, if I’m honest. Not loving the answer. Acting on it anyway.”

That might be the truest sentence you’ve said all night, Pip said, quiet, and let her walk the rest of the passage in silence, exactly as present as it had been for every minute of the hour before it.

* * *

The walk back toward the entry point ran slower than usual, her legs stiff, her chest carrying a new weight she was going to spend the rest of the night, and probably considerably longer than one night, learning how to simply live inside without flinching at it every few minutes.

“One hour,” she said again, mostly to herself, testing the number the way she’d tested it a dozen times already tonight, checking whether repetition would eventually make it feel like something other than a fact sitting flatly on a page. “Six months of Tuesday nights, and it comes down to one hour, red, small, nothing anyone would ever call impressive if they saw it.”

I’d call it impressive, Pip said. I don’t think impressive has to mean bright. I think it can just mean nobody else in the entire archive has ever done what you did tonight, in the time you did it in, for the reason you did it. It paused, then added, quieter, I watched every second of it. I want you to know that, whatever else tonight ends up meaning by tomorrow. I was here for the whole hour, and I am not going to forget a single minute of it.

“I know,” Nadia said. “You’re always here for the whole thing. That’s most of why I keep coming back down, week after week, even on the nights I’m frightened of what I’m about to find.”

“Maybe,” Nadia said, and reached the ladder, and started up it slowly, one stiff step at a time, the ache under her sternum settling into its new permanent place with every rung she climbed, patient, unhurried, exactly the way it was apparently going to stay from now on.

She paused halfway up, one hand on the cold rail, and looked back down into the dark she was leaving behind, the alcove somewhere past the west junction still holding whatever residue tonight had left in it, invisible now, unmeasurable from up here, true regardless. Eleven days left above ground. An entire structure, newly permanent, waiting below. She climbed the rest of the way without looking back again.
