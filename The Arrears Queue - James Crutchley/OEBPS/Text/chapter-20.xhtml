Chapter 20: The Ledger

The week after the review ran the way every week ran lately, a schedule with no slack anywhere in it, and she felt the absence of slack most acutely on the Thursday she stood at the entry point with a session in front of her and a clock in her head telling her, with the flat certainty of an instrument she trusted more than her own optimism, that she had ninety minutes before she needed to be back on a bus toward a shift at Pearce’s that would run until closing.

Ninety minutes bought her almost nothing. The Arrears queue took most of the first twenty just to reach the state where a real exchange was worth attempting, mitigation efficiency climbing the way it always climbed, slow and honest, a warmup she couldn’t skip any more than a body could skip the specific chemistry of getting a muscle actually ready to work. By the time she was reading the Elite’s patrol properly, judging angles instead of guessing them, she had perhaps forty minutes of usable session left before the bus schedule made the decision for her, and forty minutes on a partial warmup bought her, at best, two clean exchanges and a queue that had barely begun to test anything real.

She climbed out with the session unfinished, the way she’d climbed out unfinished more times in the last month than she wanted to count, and rode the scooter back through a cold that had gone properly wintry sometime in the last two weeks, running the arithmetic the whole way home, the same arithmetic she’d been running with small variations for days, the numbers refusing, no matter how many times she rearranged them, to add up to anything that actually worked.

School took thirty hours a week and could not move. Wrestling took twelve, non-negotiable, verified, the one category she’d never once considered touching because touching it meant touching Val’s trust and her own actual conditioning both, and she needed both too badly to treat either as a lever. Placement took twelve more, verified by Pearce’s signature on a form that lived in a drawer at the bay, dated and countersigned in his careful, slightly shaking hand. Sleep took whatever she could still afford it, which was less than it should have been and had been for weeks. What remained, after all of that, was the actual number that mattered, the hours she had left over for a body that needed uninterrupted stretches to do anything real underground, and that number kept coming out smaller than the number the site actually required of her.

You’ve run that same calculation four times since we got back, Pip observed, present now that they were home, the bay’s low light doing more for its attention than the bus ride had. The answer isn’t changing no matter how many times you check it.

“I know,” Nadia said, setting the scooter against the wall with more force than it strictly needed. “I keep hoping I did the arithmetic wrong somewhere. I never did the arithmetic wrong.”

What does the number actually say, exactly, Pip asked.

“It says I need somewhere between six and eight more hours a week than I currently have,” Nadia said. “Not next month. Now. The cut’s getting harder, the level’s costing me more to recover from than it used to, and every category eating my week is a category I built myself into a corner defending, because every one of them is real and every one of them matters and none of them are the thing that actually keeps me alive.”

The site is the thing that keeps you alive, Pip said, quiet, choosing the words carefully. In the specific sense that matters, yes. I understand why the arithmetic bothers you as much as it does.

She didn’t answer, and stood in the middle of the bay for a long moment, running the numbers one more time anyway, because a number that refused to change on a fifth check was still worth checking a sixth time, the same stubborn discipline that had kept a hundred boards from going back out the door with a fault she’d talked herself into believing wasn’t there.

She thought about cutting sleep further, the way she’d already cut it twice this season, and rejected the idea almost as fast as it arrived, because sleep wasn’t a category she logged for anyone, it was the raw material every other category ran on, and she’d watched what happened to her own reaction time on the four occasions she’d tried to borrow against it anyway. She thought about asking Val for a modified schedule, fewer drills, lighter contact, and rejected that too, because a coach who started asking why would be a coach who started watching more closely than she could currently afford, and Val had already noticed her stance shifting once this season without an explanation she’d been willing to give her. She thought, last, about simply telling someone the truth, Bhandari, Val, anyone, and rejected that fastest of all, because there was no version of the truth short enough to fit inside a form or a conversation that wouldn’t end with a stranger deciding what happened to her next.

Every honest option she turned over cost her something she genuinely could not afford to lose. That was, she understood, sitting with the whole list a second time before letting it go, the actual shape of the corner she’d built herself into, not a failure of effort but a geometry problem, three fixed points and a fourth requirement that didn’t fit inside the triangle they made no matter which side she tried to stretch.

* * *

The shift at Pearce’s that evening ran its ordinary course, a trickle of customers, a till that hadn’t miscounted once since she’d fixed it, a floor that needed sweeping regardless of how the day had actually gone. She found herself, partway through a quiet stretch near closing, restocking a bin of washers nobody had asked her to restock, the same reflex she’d had for weeks now, hands wanting something to do whenever the shop went quiet and her own thoughts got too loud to sit with unoccupied.

She stopped with a handful of washers still in her palm, struck by the specific, small clarity of a fault finally presenting itself after weeks of looking in the wrong place for it.

She’d been doing this for months. Restocking without being asked. Staying an extra twenty minutes to finish a bin rather than leaving it half sorted. Coming in on a slow Sunday afternoon once, back in October, because Pearce had mentioned offhand that a shipment needed unpacking and she’d had nothing better to do with the hour. None of it logged. None of it claimed. Extra labour handed over for free, the same instinct that had her filling silence with a wrench in her hand rather than a conversation, and she had never once thought to count it as anything other than a habit.

She counted it now, standing at the bin with the washers still cupped in her palm, running back through weeks of shifts the way she ran back through anything she needed an honest total for. An extra twenty minutes here. A whole unlogged Sunday there. Small amounts, individually, easy to dismiss on their own, and she added them anyway, the way she added anything, refusing to let a number feel unimportant just because each piece of it had felt unimportant at the time.

The total, by the time she’d worked back through everything she could remember clearly, came to something over thirty hours. Unclaimed. Unlogged. Real labour Pearce had genuinely received, that the compliance form had never once reflected, because the form only ever asked what the week required and she had never once thought to report more than what the week required.

You’ve gone very still, Pip said, quiet, watching from wherever it watched her from in the low evening light of the shop.

“I think I found the six hours,” Nadia said, low, setting the washers down carefully rather than dropping them, the way she set down anything she needed both hands free to think about properly. “I think I found more than six hours. I think I’ve had them the whole time and never once thought to look at them the right way.”

She walked the shop floor for a few minutes afterward, ostensibly finishing the close, actually reconstructing the last three months the way she reconstructed any history she needed an honest account of, starting from the most recent and working backward, cross-checking one memory against another wherever two of them touched. The Sunday in October came back to her first, clearest, because it had been raining hard enough that the walk over had soaked through her jacket, and Pearce had made her a cup of tea afterward that she still remembered because he’d never once done it before or since, a small unremarked kindness attached to a shift that had never once appeared on any form.

There had been a Wednesday in September too, a supplier’s truck arriving two hours before the store technically opened, and she’d come in early without being asked because she’d already been awake and the bus that got her there early was the same bus that got her there on time, and unloading forty boxes of stock alongside Pearce had felt, in the moment, like nothing worth mentioning to anyone, a favour exchanged between two people who trusted each other rather than a transaction either of them needed to record.

She found four more instances like it once she started actually looking, each one small on its own, each one dismissed at the time as simply what a person did when a shop needed something and she happened to be standing there with two working hands and nothing better to do with them. None of it had felt, in any of those moments, like labour worth claiming. It had felt like the ordinary cost of being somewhere she was trusted, paid without a second thought because the cost had never once seemed like it added up to anything.

It added up to something now.

You’re recalculating the same total a second time, Pip observed, gently. I already watched you do the arithmetic once. It came out the same both times.

“I know,” Nadia said. “I just don’t trust a number this large the first time I find it. Large numbers are usually where the error is hiding.”

This one isn’t an error, Pip said. You checked it twice and it held twice. I think you’re allowed to believe it now.

Nadia let the number sit for a moment longer, turning it the way she turned any figure she was about to build a decision on top of, checking it once more for the specific kind of error that only showed up when a total felt too convenient to be entirely honest. It held. Thirty-some hours of real, unclaimed labour, sitting in her own history the whole time, waiting for her to notice it had a use.

* * *

She built the ledger that night at the bench, in a fresh notebook rather than one of the ones already crowded with field-meter readings and no result written in her own hand, because this thread deserved its own page and its own honesty, kept separate from everything else she was tracking. She chose a notebook with a plain cover, no logo, the kind sold in bulk at the dollar store two blocks from the bay, deliberately unremarkable, the same instinct that had her keeping the field-meter logs in a notebook nobody would look at twice if they ever found it lying open.

She ruled the first page carefully, using the metal edge rather than trusting a straight line to her own hand, because a ledger with wandering columns was a ledger she wouldn’t trust three weeks from now when the numbers actually mattered, and precision, applied here the same way she applied it to a schematic, felt like the one part of this she could still control completely.

Three columns. Date. Hours actually worked. Hours claimed on the compliance form. A fourth column for the running balance, the number that mattered most, the one she’d refuse to let go negative under any circumstance she could currently imagine, because a balance that went negative wasn’t banking anymore, it was simply lying, and she drew a hard line in her own head between the two long before she’d finished ruling the columns.

She worked back through three months of shifts from memory, cross-checked against the dates on Pearce’s signed forms still sitting in the drawer, and filled in what she could reconstruct honestly, leaving a blank rather than a guess wherever her memory wasn’t certain enough to trust. The total held, even conservatively, even discounting every hour she wasn’t fully sure of. Twenty-six hours banked, real labour performed and never claimed, sitting in the ledger like a balance she hadn’t known she was carrying.

I want to make sure I understand the plan, Pip said, careful, present enough in the bay’s quiet to make the carefulness land. You’re going to start claiming hours on the form that you don’t work in the week the form says you worked them. Backed by real hours you already worked and never claimed, on different days.

“That’s the plan,” Nadia said, not looking up from the ledger, ruling the balance column straight with a metal edge the way she ruled anything she needed to trust later.

The total stays honest, Pip said. The days don’t.

“The days were never the part that mattered,” Nadia said. “Pearce doesn’t care what day I show up, as long as the shop gets covered and the hours add up over a month. The Ministry doesn’t actually care either, not really, they just need a form that says a specific number happened on a specific week because that’s the only shape their system knows how to read. I’m not stealing anything from anyone. I’m just telling the form what it wants to hear instead of what it doesn’t know how to ask for properly.”

That’s a very careful way of describing fraud, Pip said, gently, no accusation in it, just the flat statement of a fact it didn’t think she should skip past.

Nadia set the ruler down and sat with that for a long moment, the ledger open in front of her, the balance column filled in clean black ink she’d already decided she was never going to let run into debt.

“I know what it is,” she said, finally. “I’m not pretending it’s something else. I’ve helped plenty of people get away with things they didn’t want looked at too closely, and every single time I told myself the rules covered it, because it always cost me something and it was never free and I never once did it for nothing. This is the first time I’m doing it for myself. I know that’s different. I’m doing it anyway, because the arithmetic says I don’t survive the next six weeks without it, and I trust the arithmetic more than I trust the form.”

I’m not telling you not to, Pip said, carefully. I want that on the record too. I just don’t think either of us should pretend it’s the same shape as everything before it. I think you should get to know the difference, even if you decide the difference doesn’t change what you do next.

“I know the difference,” Nadia said, quiet. “I’m keeping the ledger honest specifically because I know the difference. If I stop caring whether the balance goes negative, that’s the day I’ve actually become the thing the form is supposed to be catching. Right now I’m not that. I’m just moving hours from a day nobody’s watching to a day I need them.”

She thought about the Fixer pattern while she said it, the shape of it she’d never once put a name to but had run a dozen times without thinking twice, a witness who couldn’t prove what they’d seen, a favour that cost her something real at exactly the moment she could least afford to pay it. Every one of those instances had a person on the other end of it, someone she’d chosen to protect, a reason that pointed outward even when the cost landed on her alone. This pointed nowhere but at herself, and she sat with that difference for a long moment, turning it over the way she turned over any fault whose symptoms didn’t match a pattern she already had a name for.

“I keep trying to find the version of this where it’s the same shape as everything else I’ve done,” she said, finally. “Somebody I’m protecting. Some cost that buys somebody else something. I can’t find it. It’s just me, buying myself six hours, because I decided I was worth spending the risk on.”

Is that a hard thing to admit, Pip asked, careful, or an easy one. I genuinely can’t tell from your voice.

Nadia considered the question honestly, the ruler still in her hand, the ledger’s first page nearly filled with dates and small careful numbers.

“Both,” she said. “It should be easy. Everyone else gets to just want things for themselves without running an audit on whether they deserve to. I don’t think I know how to do that part without checking first. So it’s easy to decide, and it’s hard to sit with, and I don’t think those two things are actually in conflict the way they sound like they should be.”

I don’t think they are either, Pip said, quiet. For what it’s worth, I think wanting something for yourself and checking whether you deserve it are two different questions, and you’re allowed to answer the first one without fully resolving the second.

“I’ll work on that,” Nadia said, in a tone that suggested she had no immediate plans to, and went back to ruling the ledger’s columns straight, the pen steady in a hand that had spent six years learning not to shake over far smaller decisions than this one.

* * *

She raised it with Pearce two days later, on a slow Tuesday afternoon with the shop empty and the compressor in the back ticking through its usual cycle, framing it the way she’d rehearsed framing it, plainly, without asking for anything that would sound, to a man who trusted her completely and had no reason yet to wonder why, like more than it actually was.

“Would it mess anything up for you if I moved some of my hours around week to week,” she asked, restocking a shelf she didn’t need to restock while she said it, keeping her hands busy the way she kept her hands busy through anything that mattered more than her voice let on. “Some weeks I’ve got more time than others. I could front-load a bit when things are slow for me, and take a shorter week when they’re not, as long as the month evens out.”

Pearce considered it for about as long as it took him to finish totalling the till, which was not very long at all. “Doesn’t matter to me what day you’re here,” he said, “long as the shop’s covered and you’re not skipping out on me without warning. You’ve never once left me short. I don’t see why that’d change.”

“It wouldn’t,” Nadia said, and meant it in the specific narrow sense that the shop would, in fact, never be short a single hour of actual labour, because the labour existed, banked and real, sitting in a ledger at the bay that would balance itself against every claim she ever made against it.

“Fine by me,” Pearce said, and went back to the till, the whole conversation closing itself in under a minute, exactly as unremarkable as she’d needed it to be, a man who had never once needed a reason more complicated than trust to grant her almost anything she asked for.

He paused before she reached the door, though, tally sheet still in hand, and added, almost as an afterthought, that she should let him know if the hours ever stopped working for her too, that the shop had survived plenty of odd schedules over thirty years and one more wouldn’t break anything, that he’d rather bend the roster than lose the best set of hands he’d had behind that counter in a decade to a schedule that stopped making sense for her.

“I will,” Nadia said, and meant it in the sense that she would genuinely never let the shop go short, which was, she reminded herself walking out into the cold a moment later, a different promise than the one the sentence sounded like she was making.

He didn’t ask a single follow-up question, Pip observed, once they’d stepped back out onto the street.

“He never does,” Nadia said. “That’s not a flaw in him. That’s just what trust looks like from the inside, when you’ve earned enough of it. I know exactly what I’m spending, using it this way.”

* * *

The first real test of it came the following Thursday, the compliance form already filled out for the week, four hours logged against a shift she had no intention of actually working, backed by a Sunday afternoon eleven days earlier that had genuinely happened, unpaid, unclaimed until this exact moment, sitting now in the ledger’s balance column with a line drawn through it in the same black ink, spent.

She rode the scooter out to the site instead, the cold sharp enough now that her breath hung visibly the whole ride, streetlights strung out along the empty highway one after another like beads on a wire, and descended with four clear hours in front of her rather than ninety rushed minutes, the first uninterrupted block she’d had underground in longer than she wanted to admit.

The warmup ran its full honest course this time, mitigation efficiency climbing the way it was supposed to climb when nothing was rushing it, and by the time she was properly reading the Elite’s patrol, judging real angles rather than guessed ones, she still had close to three hours left rather than the twenty rushed minutes a partial session usually left her. She worked the whole block methodically, testing angle after angle against a queue she could finally afford to let build properly, and felt, for the first time in weeks, the specific satisfaction of a session that had actually been allowed to become what it was supposed to become.

She tried three separate approaches to the same recurring exchange, an angle Val had never once needed to correct because it wasn’t a wrestling angle at all, purely a site problem, the Elite’s heaviest telegraphed swing arriving from a quarter she’d never had the clean minutes to properly map before tonight. The first approach cost her too much, absorbed at a fraction wrong enough to register closer to the flat three hundred than the well-managed forty. The second did better, and the third, adjusted a further few degrees off the first two, brought the number down to something she was willing to write into her notebook as a technique worth repeating, the kind of incremental, unglamorous improvement that never once felt like progress in the moment and only ever looked like progress laid out afterward against a page of older numbers.

That third variation is meaningfully better than anything you were running a month ago, Pip said, watching the meter with the closer attention it only ever had the bandwidth for down here. I don’t think you’d have found it on a twenty-minute session. It needed the repetitions.

“It needed the time,” Nadia agreed, resetting for another pass. “Which is the whole point of tonight.”

She worked the exchange four more times before her legs told her, in the specific hollow way they always told her, that she was drawing close enough to the edge of what the session should responsibly ask of a body still recovering from a level bracket it hadn’t finished adjusting to. She logged the final number, checked it twice against the meter’s own readout, and began the climb back toward the entry point with a queue drained further than she’d managed in weeks and a page of real, useable data behind her.

This is what six hours buys you, Pip said, quiet, underground and fully present the way it only ever was down here. I wanted you to notice it properly. Not just feel the relief of it. Actually notice what it cost to get here.

“I noticed,” Nadia said quietly, resetting her stance for the next exchange, the Elite’s slow patrol rounding back toward her along its same familiar circuit, unbothered as always by anything happening on the catwalk above it. “I’m not going to pretend I didn’t notice. It’s the best session I’ve had in a month.”

And the ledger, Pip said.

“The ledger’s clean,” Nadia said. “Balance still positive. I checked it twice before I left tonight.”

I know you did, Pip said. I watched you do it. I just wanted to hear you say it out loud, down here, where it’s harder to tell yourself something that isn’t true.

“Down here everything’s harder to lie about,” Nadia said, quiet. “That’s kind of the whole point of the place, whether anyone ever designed it that way on purpose or not.”

I’ve noticed, Pip said. I think that might be part of why I like it down here better than anywhere else you take me, even with everything else about the place working against it.

She didn’t answer that immediately, working through the next exchange instead, absorbing a hit at the angle Val had spent a season correcting, the queue climbing at a pace she could actually manage rather than one she was constantly racing a bus schedule to outrun. It was, she thought, distantly, exactly the trade she’d described to Pearce two days ago without either of them quite naming it for what it was, hours moved from a day nobody was watching to a day she genuinely needed them, a system exploited rather than broken, a form told exactly what it wanted to hear.

She knew it was more than that. She’d known it since the night she ruled the first column straight and decided, on her own, without anyone forcing the decision, that the balance would never go negative on her watch. That decision was hers, made with her eyes open, the first thing in longer than she could remember that she had chosen to do wrong rather than been backed into doing by a system that had never once asked what she actually had room for.

* * *

She surfaced two hours later than the old ninety-minute limit would ever have allowed, the queue drained down to something manageable, the session logged in her own notebook against numbers that had finally moved somewhere real, and rode home through a cold that had gone fully into winter sometime in the last hour without her noticing the transition.

You’re quiet, Pip said, once they were back above ground and its attention began its usual thinning.

“I’m thinking about the balance,” Nadia said. “Not the queue. The ledger. I already know it’s going to run out eventually. Thirty hours isn’t infinite, and I’m not restocking bins fast enough to keep pace with what I actually need going forward.”

So you’ll need a second source, Pip said. Real labour, banked somewhere else, the same way you found this one.

“I don’t know where yet,” Nadia said. “I’ll find it. I always find it, eventually, the same way I found this one, by looking somewhere I’d already stopped bothering to check.”

I believe you, Pip said. I also want to say, since we’re down to plain honesty tonight, that I don’t love watching you build a second set of books just to survive a system that was supposed to be helping you in the first place, however sensible the reasoning behind it turns out to be.

“Neither do I,” Nadia said, quiet, watching the last of the strip malls slide past in the dark. “I don’t love any part of this. I just haven’t found a version of survival yet that didn’t ask me to do something I didn’t love. This is just the first version where the something was actually mine to choose.”

Pip didn’t answer that, and let the silence hold for most of the remaining ride, the two of them moving through the dark in the particular companionable quiet they’d built up over months of exactly this kind of ride, nothing needing to be said simply because it had already been said honestly once.

“Can I ask you something,” Nadia said, eventually, as the bay’s low silhouette came into view at the end of the street.

Always. Every single time.

“If I ever do let the balance go negative,” she said. “If it ever actually gets there. Will you tell me.”

Yes, Pip said, without any hesitation, the single flattest, most certain word it had offered her all night. I’ll tell you the moment I see it happening, whether or not you want to hear it. That’s not a threat. It’s just the one promise I think actually matters here.

“Good,” Nadia said, quiet, letting the promise settle somewhere safe she could find it again if she ever needed it, and turned the scooter into the driveway, the ledger sitting closed in her bag, balance positive, the form in the drawer already signed for a Thursday she had never once set foot in the shop for, exactly as she’d planned it, exactly as she’d chosen it, and no clock anywhere counting down against the choice at all.
