Chapter 16: Louder Than the Dungeon

Priya said it on a Tuesday, between second and third period, in the specific offhand way she said most things that turned out to matter more than she seemed to realise while saying them: that Nadia was basically her best friend at this point, obviously, and did she want to come to the thing on Saturday, Hana was doing a whole dinner for her birthday and it wasn’t really a party, more just people, and Nadia had to come because it wouldn’t be right without her.

She said it the way she said everything, mid-motion, already reaching into her bag for a textbook while the sentence was still leaving her mouth, the specific delivery of someone who had never once needed to weigh a claim like that before making it, because to her the claim had apparently always been obvious, a fact rather than a declaration, the kind of thing you mentioned in passing on the way to a locker rather than something you sat someone down to say properly.

Nadia said okay, because saying okay had always been easier than the alternative, and because the sentence had arrived and finished and moved on to the next thing before she’d had time to do anything with it except agree. The hallway carried on around them, the same crush of bodies moving between the same rows of lockers, and Priya was already three sentences into something else entirely by the time the bell rang, the birthday dinner and the best friend and all of it folded seamlessly into a stream of talk that never once seemed to require a response for any single piece of it to be considered heard.

You went very still just then, Pip observed, arriving a beat late the way it always arrived in a crowded hallway, half its attention still somewhere else. I don’t think Priya noticed. I noticed.

Nadia didn’t answer, walking into third period with the sentence still sitting somewhere behind her sternum, obvious, basically my best friend, a fact stated so casually that it had clearly never once occurred to Priya it might need saying more carefully.

* * *

She ran the arithmetic of it the way she ran anything that unsettled her, later, alone, at the bench, though the arithmetic here refused to produce a number the way it did for calories or currency.

A cafeteria lunch had a fixed shape. Bell to bell, one clean hour, an obligation that ended itself whether or not anything inside it had gone well. She had learned to survive that hour the same way she’d learned to survive anything with an edge to it, by finding the edge and holding on until it arrived.

A dinner had no edge. Nobody told you when a dinner was over. It ended when the people in it decided it had ended, on some collective signal she had never once been able to read reliably, and until that signal arrived she was expected to be present in a way that required more than her body simply sitting in a chair. Questions arrived without warning. Silences meant things. A person could ask how she was doing and actually want an answer, rather than the ritual non-answer a hallway question usually settled for.

She had tried, over the years, to build herself something like a checklist for it, the way she built a checklist for anything unfamiliar, and had never once gotten past the first few items before the whole exercise collapsed under its own weight. Smile when something is funny, though funny arrived on a timing she couldn’t always predict. Ask a question back, though the question had to be the right one, and she rarely knew which one that was until well after the moment for asking it had passed. Match the room’s volume, though the room’s volume shifted constantly and without any signal she could reliably read in advance. Every rule she wrote for herself turned out to have exceptions she couldn’t enumerate ahead of time, which made the whole checklist worse than useless, because a checklist that failed silently was worse than no checklist at all.

There had been a birthday party in grade seven, a different girl, a different kitchen, that she still filed under things that had gone wrong without ever being able to say precisely where the failure had occurred. She had followed every rule she had at the time. She had smiled when the room smiled. She had asked a question back. And somewhere in the second hour the girl’s older sister had asked her, not unkindly, whether she was having fun, in a tone that made clear the question wasn’t really a question, and Nadia had understood, too late to fix it, that the whole room had apparently been reading her as unhappy for the better part of an hour without her ever once being aware of it. She had gone home early that night and never entirely trusted a checklist again, not because the checklist was wrong exactly, but because it had failed her without giving her any warning that it was failing, which was, she’d decided since, the worst kind of failure a system could produce.

The site had never once done that to her. Whatever else it cost her, it told her when it was costing her. A mitigation window either closed in time or it didn’t, and the difference showed up on the meter where she could see it, not in some delayed verdict delivered by a stranger’s older sister three years later in her memory.

The site, by contrast, had exactly the opposite problem, and she found herself, standing at the bench with a job she wasn’t really working on any more, genuinely grateful for it. A hit either landed or it didn’t. She either managed the angle or she didn’t. There was no version of a session where she did everything technically correctly and still walked away having somehow failed the room.

She thought, setting the wrench down harder than the job required, about the last time something had genuinely frightened her, properly, in the specific physical sense the word actually meant. Five hits on a catwalk. A hard flat contact she hadn’t managed to redirect. A night on a bathroom floor with her blood sugar cratering and her hands too unsteady to hold a phone.

None of it matched what Saturday was already doing to her, and she sat with that fact for a long moment, because a fact that refused to make sense was still a fact, and she had learned not to discard one just because it embarrassed her to hold it.

Can I ask you something, Pip said, quiet, the bay gone still around them both.

“Go ahead.”

Why does this frighten you more than the thing on the catwalk does. I’ve watched you walk toward that thing four separate times now without so much as a change in your pulse until the contact actually starts. I’ve never once seen you this unsettled by a dinner invitation before tonight.

Nadia considered the question honestly, turning the wrench over in her hand without using it.

“The catwalk has rules,” she said, finally. “I know what it wants. I know what it costs. There’s a clock and a boundary and a number I can estimate, even when I get it wrong. A dinner doesn’t have any of that. I don’t know what anyone wants from me at a dinner. I don’t know when it’s safe to stop paying attention. I can’t base low against a question I didn’t see coming.”

That’s a genuinely precise way to describe it.

“It’s the only way I’ve got,” Nadia said, and picked the job back up, because the job, at least, had rules too.

* * *

Saturday arrived the way Saturdays arrived regardless of what she wanted from them, and she spent most of the afternoon doing the same thing she did before a session, running a mental checklist for a threat she couldn’t quite name in the same clinical terms she reserved for anything with actual physical stakes.

She showered properly rather than efficiently, longer than the water bill really justified, standing under it until the small bathroom fogged the way it never did when she was only rinsing off a shift at Pearce’s. She put on the one shirt she owned that didn’t have a permanent solder-flux smell worked into the collar, the good one, kept folded at the bottom of the drawer for exactly the handful of occasions a year that called for it. She practiced, silently, in the small mirror over the bay’s sink, the specific shape her face made when a question landed and she needed a second longer than felt natural to answer it, adjusting the angle of it slightly, testing whether a pause that long read as thoughtful or simply strange, because she had learned, over years of exactly this kind of evening, that the difference mattered and that she had no reliable way of knowing which side of it she usually landed on.

She packed a small amount of food into her jacket pocket too, out of a habit that had nothing to do with tonight’s actual danger and everything to do with the fact that she no longer left the bay for anything without a fallback tucked somewhere close, a reflex that had started at the pumping station and had, somewhere in the last two months, simply become how she moved through the world generally.

The week leading up to it had done its own quiet damage, a low background hum she hadn’t fully named until Saturday actually arrived and the hum stopped, replaced by the sharper, more immediate thing sitting in her chest now. She had thought about the dinner at odd moments all week, waiting for a kettle, halfway through a set of reps in the wrestling room, the invitation surfacing uninvited and running its small circuit of questions before she managed to set it back down. Tomasz had asked her Thursday, between drills, whether she was coming Saturday, and she’d said yes, and he’d said good, easy, unweighted, and gone back to the drill without either of them saying anything else about it, and she had spent the rest of that session grateful he hadn’t asked anything further, because she didn’t yet have an answer for the part of the question he hadn’t asked.

You’re preparing for this the way you prepare for the site, Pip said, watching from wherever it watched things from. I don’t think that’s a bad strategy. I just want to make sure you know I noticed.

“It’s the only strategy I’ve got,” Nadia said, which was becoming, she noted distantly, something close to her answer for everything lately.

Hana’s apartment sat above a bakery two blocks off the Drive, small, warm, crowded with more people than Nadia had braced for, eight or nine rather than the four or five she’d quietly hoped the word people had meant, string lights looped along one wall and a table already crowded with food before anyone had properly sat down at it. Music played low under the noise of everyone talking at once, a specific layered volume that had no single source she could locate and therefore no edge she could find to hold onto, voices overlapping and rising and falling in a pattern that never once settled into anything she could predict two seconds ahead of time.

She found a spot near the kitchen doorway within the first two minutes, not deliberately, only because standing near a doorway had always felt marginally more survivable than standing in the middle of a room, close enough to an exit that some old animal part of her catalogued the distance without her having consciously asked it to. She stayed there for most of the first hour, offering short honest answers to whatever questions drifted her way and asking almost nothing back, because asking nothing back had never once, in her experience, been read as unusual so long as she looked like she was listening, and looking like she was listening was a skill she had built, slowly, over years, out of the same raw materials she used for everything else, close observation and careful repetition.

A girl she half recognised from a different class asked how she knew Priya, and Nadia said school, which was true and also the entire answer she had available, and the girl seemed to expect more and didn’t get it, and moved on to someone else within a minute, which Nadia registered as a small failure she filed rather than one she let herself feel anything about. Somebody else asked whether she was the wrestler, and she said yes, and that seemed to satisfy the question fully enough that no follow-up ever arrived, and she was grateful for it in a way she didn’t examine too closely.

A boy she didn’t recognise at all, tall, holding a plate he hadn’t started eating from, planted himself near the doorway a few minutes later with the specific look of someone about to attempt small talk as a project rather than a habit, and asked her, apropos of nothing, whether she’d ever considered that the whole city smelled different depending on which way the wind came off the water. She said she hadn’t, honestly, and he seemed pleased rather than deflated by that, and spent the next several minutes explaining a theory involving the paper mill upriver and the tide schedule that she didn’t follow closely enough to evaluate but nodded along to anyway, grateful for a conversation that required nothing from her except the occasional confirming sound. He drifted off eventually toward the food table, apparently satisfied, and she catalogued the exchange as a rare success, a full conversation completed without a single question landing anywhere near her.

* * *

Dinner itself ran long, plates passed around a table too small for the number of people trying to use it, elbows brushing, someone’s chair backed into the wall behind it, a bowl of something with too much garlic in it circling twice before it finally emptied. Conversation looped through topics she had no footing in, a show she hadn’t watched, a teacher three of them shared and she didn’t, a summer trip Priya and Hana had apparently been on together years ago that came up twice with the specific ease of a story that had been told enough times to have worn smooth, every retelling slightly different from the last in a way none of them seemed to notice or mind.

She tracked the conversation the way she’d track a signal she couldn’t fully decode, catching enough of each thread to nod in the right places without ever having the full picture any of them were actually working from, three separate private histories running under the surface of a single dinner table that she had no access to and no polite way of asking for. It was, she thought, distantly, the social equivalent of being handed a board with half its silkscreen worn off, expected to trace a circuit whose labels had faded past legibility.

She caught fragments and tried, out of old habit, to reconstruct the whole from them, the way she’d reconstruct a fault from a handful of symptoms. The summer trip had involved a missed ferry and a night in a parking lot that had apparently become, over several years of retelling, the single funniest thing that had ever happened to either of them, though nobody at the table ever explained what had actually been funny about it, only referenced the parking lot itself as though the word alone carried the whole joke. The shared teacher had a nickname nobody used to his face and a rule about phones that three different people quoted with three slightly different wordings, and she filed the discrepancy the way she’d file a schematic that didn’t match the board in front of her, unable to tell which version was the original and which had drifted. She said nothing about any of it, only nodded when the table nodded, and reminded herself that a circuit she couldn’t fully trace wasn’t necessarily a circuit that was broken, only one she didn’t have full documentation for yet.

She ate steadily, the way she ate anything, and used the eating the way she’d used it in the cafeteria for months, a legitimate reason to have her mouth occupied rather than contributing, though it worked less well here, because a cafeteria tray never once asked her a direct question and Tomasz, three seats down, apparently had no such reservation.

“You’ve been quiet all night,” he said, not unkindly, the specific observation of someone who’d spent enough hours across a mat from her to notice a pattern shifting. “Everything okay?”

“I’m fine,” Nadia said, which was the honest sentence available to her that cost the least to say, and felt, saying it, the particular strain of a sentence she’d used so many times it had started to feel less like a lie and more like a piece of load-bearing infrastructure she didn’t dare examine too closely for cracks.

He seemed to accept it, mostly, and the conversation moved on without her having to add anything further, and she registered the small relief of that the way she’d register a fault that had resolved itself without her having to trace it properly. It never did, not really, not on its own, but the reprieve while it lasted was real enough that she let herself have it without questioning the mechanism behind it too closely.

For what it’s worth, Pip said, quiet enough that it barely registered over the table noise, I’m fine is doing a great deal of work in that sentence. I counted at least four things it isn’t telling him.

“It’s not lying,” Nadia said under her breath, mouth mostly hidden behind her glass. “I am fine. Fine just doesn’t mean what he thinks it means.”

I understand the distinction. I’m only noting that from where I sit, it looks like a fairly large gap between what the word says and what it’s standing in for.

“Every word’s doing that,” Nadia said, and let the table’s noise close back over the exchange before anyone nearby had a chance to notice she’d been talking to no one.

Someone further down the table asked her, loudly enough that a few other conversations paused to listen, whether it was true she’d fixed the wrestling room scale, the story having apparently travelled further than she’d ever intended it to, and she said yes, and the follow-up question, how, arrived before she’d finished answering the first one. She gave the same three words she always gave, mostly just looking, and this time it produced a small ripple of something closer to amusement than satisfaction, someone joking that she must see the whole world like that, all faults and fixes, and she said maybe, which wasn’t quite agreement and wasn’t quite denial, and the table moved on to something else before she had to decide which one she actually meant.

That was a reasonably graceful deflection, Pip observed, arriving late to the exchange the way it always arrived late in a room this loud. I’m impressed. I don’t think anyone at that table noticed you hadn’t actually answered anything.

I noticed, it added, a beat later. For what that’s worth.

Priya, beside her, asked something quieter, under the general noise once the table’s attention had moved on, whether she was having an okay time, an actual question rather than a rhetorical one, delivered with a directness that made it harder to deflect than Tomasz’s had been, or the table’s question about the scale, or any of the dozen smaller questions the evening had already thrown at her.

Nadia looked at her for a moment, weighing the honest answer against the sentence that would end the question fastest, and found, for reasons she didn’t examine in the moment, that she gave the honest one instead.

“It’s a lot,” she said. “I’m okay. It’s just a lot.”

Priya didn’t push, and didn’t look hurt, which was its own small surprise, a version of a difficult answer landing without any of the fallout Nadia had braced for. She said only that she got it, that big groups exhausted her too sometimes, though Nadia suspected, without saying so, that whatever Priya meant by that word wasn’t quite the same thing she meant by it, a difference in kind rather than degree that she didn’t have the vocabulary to explain and didn’t particularly want to try explaining tonight.

“You don’t have to stay the whole time,” Priya added, quieter still, glancing down the table to make sure nobody else was listening in. “If it gets to be too much. Nobody’s going to think anything of it if you head out early.”

The offer landed somewhere Nadia didn’t have a ready response for, a permission she hadn’t known she needed until it was handed to her, and she said thanks, and meant it more plainly than she’d meant almost anything else all evening, and went back to her plate, the food giving her, for a moment, exactly the legitimate reason to stop talking that she’d been relying on all night.

She didn’t take the offer, not that night, though she filed it carefully somewhere she could find it again, a standing exception carved out for her specifically, usable whenever she needed it and not only tonight. It occurred to her, chewing slowly through a piece of bread gone slightly cold, that nobody had ever offered her that particular permission before, not at school, not in any of the group placements that had cycled through her childhood, and that Priya had extended it without making her ask for it first, without making her explain why she might need it, which was, in its own small way, more than the invitation itself had been.

* * *

The evening didn’t end so much as it eventually thinned, people peeling off in twos, someone’s ride arriving, the noise gradually dropping to something she could actually parse into individual sentences rather than a single undifferentiated wall of sound. She stayed longer than Priya’s offer had strictly required her to, not out of any renewed enthusiasm for the room but because leaving early felt, once the option was actually in front of her, like it would draw more attention than simply riding the evening out to its natural, unremarkable close.

Somebody put on a slower playlist once the table had cleared, and the conversation broke into smaller clusters around the apartment, two or three people at a time rather than the whole table trying to hold one thread together, and Nadia found this version of the room considerably easier to survive, close enough to eavesdrop on without being expected to contribute, and she let herself sit with it for the better part of an hour, present in the way a piece of furniture is present, tolerated and unremarked upon.

She ended up on the arm of a secondhand couch near the window, half listening to Priya and two others debate, with real intensity, the correct order to watch a set of movies neither of the other two had actually agreed on, and found the argument oddly restful precisely because nothing about it required her opinion. Someone asked her once, lightly, which side she was on, and she said she hadn’t seen any of them, which produced a round of mock outrage that dissolved into laughter within seconds and asked nothing further of her, the conversation folding itself back together and moving on without her the way water closes over a dropped stone. She watched the room from the couch arm the way she’d watch a floor from the edge of a mat, cataloguing where everyone stood in relation to everyone else, who was tired, who was still working the room, who had already mentally left even though their body hadn’t yet, and found some of the same satisfaction in it she found in reading a fight before it started, a pattern with a logic to it even when the logic wasn’t hers to act on.

She left when Hana walked her to the door, an unremarkable gesture that Hana seemed to extend to everyone equally and that still landed, for Nadia, as more attention than she’d wanted directed at the specific fact of her leaving. Hana said thanks for coming, easy, unweighted, already half turned back toward the room before the sentence had finished, and added, almost as an afterthought, that Nadia should come to more of these, that she was good to have around even when she didn’t say much, which was a sentence Nadia turned over twice on the stairs down and still hadn’t fully unpacked by the time she reached the street.

She had braced, walking down, for the sentence to mean something it probably didn’t mean, the way she braced for most compliments, checking it for a hidden condition the way she’d check a repaired board for a cold joint before trusting it back into service. She found, running it again on the second flight, that she couldn’t locate one. Hana hadn’t asked her to be different at the next one. Hadn’t asked her to talk more, or smile more, or perform any particular version of enjoying herself more visibly than she had tonight. The sentence, as far as she could tell, asked for nothing at all beyond her simply showing up again, which was either the easiest condition anyone had ever set for her or one she hadn’t yet found the catch in, and she wasn’t sure, reaching the street, which of those two possibilities she believed more.

Nadia said happy birthday, a beat late, and let herself out into the cold.

The street outside was quiet, streetlights throwing the same familiar orange cones onto wet pavement, a bus passing two blocks over with its engine the only sound for a moment, and she stood there a moment longer than she needed to, letting her own pulse settle the way it settled after anything that had asked more of her than she’d had readily available to give. Her shoulders ached in a way that had nothing to do with wrestling or the site, a tension she hadn’t noticed accumulating until the room that had caused it was finally behind her.

You survived it, Pip said, present again now that the room’s noise had fallen away, its attention returning the way it always returned once the competition for it had thinned, a difference in the quality of its voice that she’d have missed entirely six weeks ago and had learned, since then, to notice immediately.

“I survived it,” Nadia agreed, starting to walk.

For what it’s worth, from what I could follow, you did better than you think you did. Priya seemed genuinely glad you were there. Tomasz didn’t seem to think anything was wrong with you. Nobody looked at you the way people look at someone who’s failing at something.

“It didn’t feel like that from in here,” Nadia said, tapping two fingers against her own temple without quite meaning the gesture literally.

I believe you. I’m just telling you what it looked like from the outside, in case the two views are worth comparing. They don’t always match, in my experience. I don’t think they’re supposed to.

“I don’t think I was failing,” Nadia said, walking now, the cold air doing the work it always did, clearing something out of her chest that she still didn’t have a better word for than tired. “I think I was just working the whole time. It’s different from failing. It’s just harder than it looks like it should be.”

That’s a distinction worth keeping, Pip said, quiet. I’ll remember it.

She walked the rest of the way home in the kind of silence that had stopped, months ago now, feeling like an absence, past the same shuttered storefronts, the same hardware store sign with its ambiguous hours, past Duarte’s dark window and the paper-covered gap where the phone kiosk had briefly existed and failed, and let herself into the bay with the particular relief of a door closing behind her that asked nothing further of her at all.

The room greeted her the way it always greeted her, bench and bed and totes exactly where she’d left them, and she stood in the doorway a moment just taking that in, the specific pleasure of a space that made no demands of her whatsoever, before she moved.

She sat on the edge of the bed without turning the light on, still in the one shirt without a flux stain on the collar, and ran the whole evening back once, the way she ran anything, checking it against what she’d expected going in. Priya’s question at the table. Hana’s comment at the door. The scale, the ripple of amusement it had produced, small and warm and entirely unlike anything the site had ever given her. None of it fit cleanly into the checklist she’d tried to build for herself years ago and abandoned, and she found, turning it over now with the whole room dark and quiet around her, that she didn’t mind the checklist’s failure quite as much as she usually did.

It had cost her more than four visits to the pumping station combined. She didn’t have a number for it, not the kind she trusted, only the flat unmistakable fact of exhaustion sitting in her the way it never sat after a session, deeper somehow, harder to name, and she thought, before sleep finally took the thought away from her entirely, that whatever else this year had taught her about her own body, this particular kind of tired was the one she still had no real instrument for measuring at all.
