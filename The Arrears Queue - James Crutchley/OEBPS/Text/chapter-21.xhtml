Chapter 21: The Flicker

The alcove she’d chosen sat two hundred metres off the Elite’s patrol line, a collapsed service junction where a maintenance corridor had partially caved decades ago, close enough to the site’s main structure that the field density read the same on her meter as it did anywhere else down here, far enough from the patrol that she could spend an evening without one ear permanently cocked toward a threat she wasn’t here to manage tonight.

She’d found it three sessions ago, on one of the newly freed evenings the ledger had bought her, and had spent the first of those three sessions simply confirming it was safe, mapping the Elite’s circuit against the alcove’s position the way she’d map any sightline, before she’d let herself treat it as anything other than a threat she hadn’t yet ruled out.

The collapse itself told its own story if she looked at it the way she looked at anything, a maintenance corridor that had run somewhere useful decades ago, before whatever budget had funded it dried up the same way every other budget in this city seemed to dry up eventually, leaving a slab of ceiling half down and a room nobody had bothered clearing because clearing it would have cost more than the depleted site was worth to anyone keeping the ledgers that mattered. She’d traced the collapse pattern on her second visit, checking the exposed rebar and the angle of the fallen slab against her own read of the structure’s remaining load, and satisfied herself that nothing above her was going anywhere else without considerably more provocation than a girl sitting quietly against one wall for a few hours at a time.

Tonight was the fourth session in the alcove, and the eleventh overall since she’d stopped simply logging baseline readings and started, deliberately, trying to move the meter herself, a shift in method she’d made the same week she’d finished reading through the entire public archive of Phase One material and concluded, with the specific flat certainty of someone who had run out of new information to gather, that there was nothing left to learn from reading. The only way forward from here was attempting it and measuring what happened, the same conclusion that ended every research phase of every repair she’d ever done, the point where theory stopped being useful and the only remaining question was what the bench actually showed you.

The guide’s own text sat open on her phone, propped against a chunk of fallen concrete, the same procedural language she’d already catalogued as useless for explaining anything and useful only as a literal set of instructions to be followed and measured rather than trusted. Phase One, the guide called it, in the same flat, undocumented tone every phase in the archive used. Settle the breath to a four-count. Hold the following posture. Direct attention inward, toward the sternum, without straining for a sensation that has not yet arrived. Repeat daily. Results vary and are not guaranteed within any specific timeframe.

No mechanism. No explanation of what settling a breath to a four-count was supposed to accomplish, physically, biologically, in any sense a repair tech could trace to a cause. Just a recipe, exactly as Pip had promised her back in the fall, forty years of published material with nobody having ever built the underneath.

She’d read past Phase One weeks ago, the first night she’d sat with the archive open properly, and had made herself stop there deliberately, closing the later phases without reading them the way she’d close a schematic she wasn’t ready to trust yet. Reading ahead risked contaminating the test, planting an expectation of what Phase Two was supposed to feel like before she’d honestly earned Phase One, and she’d watched enough repair techs talk themselves into a fault that matched what they already expected to find rather than what the board was actually telling them to trust her own judgement on that point completely.

So Phase One sat alone on her phone, four sentences and a disclaimer, and she had spent eleven sessions treating those four sentences with the same rigor she’d have given a datasheet for a component she’d never worked with before, reading them exactly as written, refusing to infer anything the text didn’t explicitly say.

She followed it anyway, because following it exactly, without deviation, was the only way to isolate whatever the recipe actually depended on from everything else she might otherwise be tempted to add or subtract without meaning to.

* * *

You’ve run this exact protocol eleven times now, Pip said, fully present the way it was only ever fully present down here, its attention entirely hers with nothing else in the world competing for it. I want to make sure I’m tracking the variable you’re isolating tonight, because it’s different from last time.

“Breath count,” Nadia said, settling into the posture the guide described, cross-legged against the alcove’s cold concrete wall, meter propped within sight on a flat stone she’d levelled herself two sessions ago. “Last time I ran a four-count exactly as written. Tonight I’m running six, holding the exhale longer, everything else identical. Same posture, same hour, same distance from the patrol, same amount of sleep behind me, same three hours since I last ate.”

And if six does nothing either, Pip said.

“Then I try eight next time, and then I try varying the posture instead of the breath, and I keep changing exactly one thing at a time until either something moves or I run out of variables the guide actually specifies,” Nadia said. “It’s the same method I’d use on a board that won’t power on. Isolate, test, discard, retest. Eventually you either find the fault or you rule out everything the schematic says could be the fault, and then you have to start looking at things the schematic doesn’t mention at all.”

That’s eleven sessions of no result, Pip said, not quite a question.

“Eleven sessions of no result is data,” Nadia said. “It’s not nothing. It’s ruling things out. I’d rather have eleven clean negatives than one result I couldn’t trust because I changed three things at once and don’t know which one mattered.”

I remember when you explained this the first time, Pip said. Back in October, at the bay, the same week you started the second notebook. I think I understood the words then and not the patience actually behind them. I understand the patience better now, having watched eleven whole sessions of you sitting very still and producing nothing at all.

“It’s the same patience as the scooter,” Nadia said. “Or the register at Pearce’s. Or any fault that doesn’t announce itself the first time you look at it. You don’t get to be impatient with a problem just because it’s important. If anything the important ones are the ones that punish impatience hardest.”

Do you ever wonder if the guide’s vagueness is doing some of that work for you, deliberately or not, Pip asked. Forcing a patience nobody would otherwise choose to have, simply by refusing to explain anything faster than it already does.

Nadia considered that, turning it over the way she turned over anything Pip offered that hadn’t occurred to her first. “Maybe,” she said. “I don’t think it was designed that way on purpose. I think it’s just what happens when nobody ever built the theory and everyone since has just been copying the last person’s notes without questioning them. But maybe the patience is the only part of it that actually works reliably, and everything else is just ritual sitting on top of that one real thing.”

That’s a fairly uncomfortable thought to have about something you’re about to spend the next hour doing exactly as written, word for word, Pip said.

“Most useful thoughts are uncomfortable ones,” Nadia said, and settled further into the posture, working her shoulders back against the cold concrete until the angle matched what the guide’s single accompanying diagram had shown, as closely as she could judge it without a mirror to actually check herself against.

She closed her eyes, the meter’s small readout glowing faintly at the edge of her vision even through closed lids, and began the breath.

* * *

Four counts in, nothing. Four counts out, nothing. She held the position through six full cycles, the cold from the concrete seeping up through her jeans, the alcove silent except for her own breath and, somewhere distant enough to ignore, the faint mechanical hum of the Elite’s patrol working its slow circuit two hundred metres away.

By the twelfth cycle her mind had done what it always did eventually, wandering toward the ledger’s balance, toward the shape of the next fault she’d need to solve once this evening’s freed hours ran out, and she caught the wandering the way she caught anything that threatened to compromise a clean test, pulling her attention back to the sternum, to the specific instruction the guide had given without ever explaining why it mattered, direct attention inward, without straining for a sensation that has not yet arrived.

Nothing arrived on the fifteenth cycle either. Nor the twentieth. She kept counting, patient in the specific way six years of fault-tracing had built into her, the patience of someone who had learned that a fault which refused to present itself on the tenth test sometimes needed the fortieth, and impatience only ever cost more time than it saved.

Her body settled into the repetition the way it settled into any long, monotonous physical task, wrestling conditioning drills, a shift restocking shelves, the slow methodical work of stripping a board down to its bare components one solder joint at a time. The cold from the concrete had stopped registering as discomfort somewhere around the eighth cycle and become instead a kind of background constant, present but no longer worth attending to, the same way the ache in her hips from Val’s drills had become background weeks ago, one more fixed cost of a body that kept asking more of her than it used to.

She thought, somewhere around the eighteenth cycle, about the ledger sitting closed in her bag, the balance she’d checked twice before leaving the bay tonight, and felt the specific low hum of a debt she was managing rather than a debt that was managing her, a distinction that mattered more to her than she suspected it would matter to anyone reading the actual numbers. Eighteen hours banked, six spent, twelve remaining before she’d need to find another Sunday somewhere. She let the thought pass through without settling, the way she let any thought pass through during a held posture, acknowledged and released rather than pursued.

By the twenty-second cycle her mind had gone properly quiet in the way it sometimes went quiet underground and almost never went quiet anywhere else, a stillness she’d first noticed months ago and had since come to recognise as one of the site’s stranger gifts, a place loud enough with danger that her body had apparently decided, somewhere below conscious thought, that ordinary mental noise wasn’t worth the energy it cost to maintain. She held the six-count breath through cycle after cycle, the exhale drawn out longer than felt entirely natural, watching without watching the faint green glow of the meter at the edge of her closed eyes.

Somewhere around the twenty-sixth cycle, with no warning and nothing she could point to as a cause, something happened that she would spend the rest of the night trying to describe accurately to herself and would never quite manage.

It wasn’t light exactly. It wasn’t a sound. It was closer to the specific, wrong sensation of a circuit completing somewhere she had no wires to feel it complete, a fraction of a second where the space in front of her closed eyes seemed to hold something that hadn’t been there a moment before and wasn’t there a moment after, gone before she’d finished registering that anything had changed at all.

Her eyes snapped open.

The meter’s readout had moved.

* * *

She stared at it for a long moment without touching anything, the way she’d stare at an oscilloscope trace that had caught something she hadn’t expected to see, unwilling to disturb the display before she’d recorded exactly what it said.

A single spike, sub-second, timestamped, the field-density reading climbing from its steady baseline to a value nearly four times higher before dropping back to baseline within a window the meter’s own logging interval could barely resolve. Not a glitch pattern. Not the kind of noise she’d learned to recognise and discard from three weeks of calibrating the instrument against its own quirks. A single, clean, real event, exactly the shape a genuine reading should have, and gone almost as fast as it had arrived.

I felt that. I actually felt that, Pip said, and there was something in its voice she hadn’t heard from it before, not quite alarm, closer to the specific attention of someone who had just noticed their own hands doing something without having decided to move them. I don’t know how else to describe it. Something changed, very briefly, very close to you, and it registered somewhere in whatever I use to register anything at all.

“You’re sure of that,” Nadia said, low, still not touching the meter at all, cross-checking the timestamp on its display against her own memory of the exact cycle count, the twenty-sixth, the moment the wrongness had arrived and vanished.

I’m not sure of anything about what it actually was, Pip said, quiet. I’m sure something happened. That’s two independent readings, if you want to count me as an instrument, which I’m increasingly unsure I shouldn’t.

Nadia sat very still, the cold concrete forgotten, running the whole sequence back the way she ran anything she needed to trust before building a conclusion on top of it. She had not seen light. She had felt something that resembled, more than anything else in her experience, the specific non-visual wrongness of a fault finally presenting after a long silent search, and Pip, entirely independently, on whatever internal sense its own field-dependent cognition ran on, had registered something at the exact same moment.

Two instruments. Neither one visual. Both timestamped to the same twenty-sixth cycle.

She ran the sceptic’s version of the sequence next, the way she always ran the sceptic’s version before letting herself trust a promising reading, because a reading she wanted badly enough could be a reading she’d unconsciously bent to fit the shape of what she wanted. Could the meter have spiked on its own, an internal fault, a stray reading from something else entirely passing through the alcove. She’d calibrated the instrument herself, twice, against a known reference, and had logged three weeks of clean baseline data with nothing resembling this pattern anywhere in it. Could Pip’s report be unreliable, a pattern-matching error rather than a genuine independent sensation. She turned that possibility over carefully too, because Pip was, in the end, still hardware, however strange the hardware, and hardware could produce false readings same as anything else.

“Could you have imagined it,” she asked, quiet, testing the question the way she’d test any component she suspected of lying to her. “Not lying. I don’t think you’d lie to me. I mean genuinely misread your own signal.”

I’ve asked myself the same thing in the last ninety seconds, Pip said. I don’t have a clean answer. I’ve never had a sensation like that before, so I don’t have a library of false positives to compare it against. All I can tell you is that it didn’t feel like noise. It felt like something specific happened, close by, briefly, and then stopped. I don’t know how to prove that to you beyond saying it as plainly as I can.

“That’s fair,” Nadia said. “I’m not doubting you because I think you’re unreliable. I’m doubting everything right now because that’s what I do with a result I want to be true. If I don’t doubt it properly now, I’ll trust it for the wrong reasons later, and a result trusted for the wrong reasons is worse than no result at all.”

I understand, Pip said. I think I’d rather be doubted properly than believed carelessly. It’s a strange thing to be grateful for, but I think I am.

She sat with the two readings a while longer, letting the scepticism run its full course rather than cutting it short just because it was uncomfortable to sustain, and found, at the end of it, that neither explanation for dismissing the spike actually held up against the data in front of her. The meter’s calibration was sound. Pip’s report, however novel, had arrived independently and at the exact timestamp, with no opportunity for either of them to have influenced the other’s account before comparing notes. Whatever this was, it had happened, once, briefly, and she had no explanation for it that didn’t require believing something in her had, for a fraction of a second, done what the guide’s four flat sentences had spent eleven sessions failing to describe clearly enough for her to reproduce on command.

* * *

She did not celebrate.

She thought about celebrating, distantly, the way she thought about most reactions other people would have had immediately and automatically, and set the impulse aside the same way she set aside anything that wasn’t yet earned. One data point was one data point. A single spike, however real, however clean, proved only that something was possible, not that she understood what had produced it or could produce it again on purpose.

She opened her notebook instead, the one that had held weeks of no result in her own increasingly cramped handwriting, and wrote the entry carefully, exactly as she’d have written up a repair that had finally, after a long search, shown its first real symptom.

Cycle twenty-six. Six-count breath, held exhale. Posture per guide, unmodified. Meter spike, sub-second, four times baseline, timestamp logged. Not reproduced within the same session, attempted cycles twenty-seven through forty with no further result. Cause unknown. Pip reports independent sensation at the same timestamp, unverified against any instrument, noted for the record.

She underlined nothing. She drew no exclamation mark, no star, none of the small unconscious flourishes she caught herself adding to a page when something had genuinely gone well. The entry sat on the page exactly as flat and exactly as careful as every no result that had come before it, because a single anomaly, however promising, was still an anomaly until it had been reproduced under conditions she controlled and understood.

She read the entry back once, checking her own handwriting against itself the way she checked any record she meant to trust later, and found, reading it, that the flatness of it pleased her more than a celebration would have. A page of careful, unremarkable prose describing exactly what had happened and nothing more was a page she could still trust in six months, when the memory of the moment itself had inevitably softened and reshaped itself the way memories always did. An exclamation mark would have told her only how she’d felt. The numbers told her what had actually occurred, and the numbers were the only part of tonight she was willing to build anything on top of.

You’re not going to say anything about it, Pip said, something almost gentle in the observation.

“What is there to say,” Nadia asked, closing the notebook. “It happened once. I don’t know why. I don’t know if I can make it happen again. Getting excited about a single data point is how you end up building a theory on noise.”

I think most people would allow themselves at least a moment, Pip said. After eleven sessions of nothing.

“Most people aren’t trying to build something that has to actually work later, underground, when it matters,” Nadia said. “If I get excited every time the meter twitches, I stop being able to tell the difference between a real signal and something I wanted badly enough to see.”

You let yourself feel things about the site, Pip pointed out. You let yourself feel something about the ledger, and about Bhandari, and about the dinner at Hana’s. I’ve watched you do it, even when you didn’t call it by name. I don’t understand why this gets a different rule.

Nadia considered that for longer than she’d expected to need, turning it over the way she turned over anything Pip said that landed somewhere she hadn’t already mapped. “Because those things already happened,” she said, finally. “Whatever I feel about them doesn’t change whether they happened. This is different. This is the start of something I still have to build, and if I let myself get attached to the feeling of it working before I actually know how to make it work, I’ll start making decisions based on the feeling instead of the data. I’ve watched that go wrong for other people. I’m not going to let it go wrong for me over something this important.”

That’s a very controlled way to live, Pip said, not quite a criticism.

“It’s the only way I know how to survive something this uncertain,” Nadia said. “I’m not against feeling things about it eventually. I just want to earn the feeling first, the same way I want to earn the result. One flicker isn’t earned yet. It’s just a door that isn’t locked.”

I’ll hold the feelings for both of us in the meantime, then, Pip said, something almost warm in it. Someone should be allowed to be pleased about this tonight, even if it isn’t going to be you.

“Fine,” Nadia said, and something in her own voice, despite everything she’d just said, came out closer to a smile than she’d ever admit to out loud in words. “You be pleased. I’ll be careful. We’ll compare notes once it actually reproduces.”

* * *

She spent the remainder of the freed evening designing the next phase of the test rather than repeating the one that had just, however briefly, worked. Repetition without a plan was how a person burned through data without learning anything from it, and she had exactly as many freed evenings as the ledger’s balance allowed her, a number she was not going to waste chasing a feeling instead of a method.

She worked out the shape of it methodically, the way she worked out any test plan, listing every variable the single successful cycle had held constant and considering which ones needed to be tested next to narrow down what had actually mattered. The six-count breath. The held exhale specifically, rather than a held inhale, which she hadn’t yet tried. The twenty-sixth repetition, which might mean nothing or might mean something about cumulative effect building slowly across many attempts rather than any single attempt being special on its own. The exact hour, close to midnight, when her own fatigue was at a specific point in its daily curve that she hadn’t previously controlled for.

Four variables, each requiring its own isolated session to test properly, each session costing her one of the freed evenings the ledger had bought her at a cost she was keeping careful account of in a different notebook entirely, one column of hours spent underground against a balance that only ever moved in one direction once she’d claimed it.

She weighed the order of the tests carefully before settling on one, because testing them in the wrong sequence risked wasting a session on a variable that turned out to depend on another variable she hadn’t isolated yet, the same trap that had cost her a full afternoon once on a board with two faults masking each other. Breath direction first, since it was the cheapest to test and the easiest to isolate cleanly. The cumulative-repetition question second, since it would take the longest to confirm either way and was worth ruling in or out early. Posture and hour last, in whichever order the first two results suggested made more sense to chase.

“Four more sessions, minimum,” she said, mostly to herself, doing the arithmetic against her own schedule, the wrestling calendar, the shifts at Pearce’s, the freed hours she had left in the ledger’s current balance before she’d need to bank another Sunday somewhere to keep the whole account from running dry. “Call it nine days, if I can get the spacing right and nothing else eats into the schedule before then.”

Nine days to find out if it actually reproduces at all, Pip said, quiet.

“Nine days to find out if I was right to build the model this way instead of just following the guide blind for eight years like everyone else does,” Nadia said. “One flicker doesn’t prove anything yet. It just means the door isn’t locked. I still have to find out if I can actually open it on purpose.”

What happens if none of the four variables reproduce it, Pip asked. If nine days from now you’re still sitting on exactly one unexplained spike and nothing else new to show for the whole nine days.

Nadia thought about that honestly, the way she thought about any failure mode before she’d actually reached it, because a plan without a contingency wasn’t a plan, it was a hope wearing a plan’s clothing. “Then I widen the search,” she said. “I go back to the guide and check whether I missed a variable it specifies that I assumed didn’t matter. If the guide’s exhausted, I start looking at what I changed by accident rather than on purpose, something about that specific night I haven’t thought to control for yet. Diet. Sleep debt. How close the Elite’s patrol was at the time. I keep narrowing until either I find it or I run out of things left to narrow.”

And if you run out entirely, Pip said. If nine days becomes ninety, and then longer still, and the spike never once comes back no matter what you try.

“Then it was noise after all, and I was wrong, and I go back to logging baselines until something else gives me a reason to try again,” Nadia said, plainly, no defensiveness in it at all. “I’d rather admit that honestly than keep chasing a ghost because I liked the way it felt to have found something once. That’s the whole discipline, the entire reason it’s worth doing carefully instead of just hoping. It only works if I’m genuinely willing to lose.”

I don’t think you’re going to lose this one, Pip said, quiet.

“You don’t know that,” Nadia said, not unkindly. “Neither do I. That’s exactly why I’m not celebrating yet, whatever else either of us might privately suspect about how this ends.”

She thought, packing the last of her gear together, about the ledger’s balance sitting in a different notebook entirely, twelve hours remaining after tonight, four sessions needed to test the variables properly, the arithmetic tight but not impossible if nothing else in her week shifted unexpectedly. It was, she reflected, exactly the kind of budget she’d gotten used to running on lately, everything costed precisely, nothing spared that hadn’t been accounted for first, a life measured out in columns the way her whole week had started to feel measured out in columns since the night she’d first ruled a ledger’s edges straight with a metal ruler and decided the balance would never go negative on her watch.

Do you want to know what I think it means, Pip asked, quiet, as she began gathering the last of her things. Not what the data says. What I actually think, if you want it.

Nadia paused, the meter halfway already into her bag. “Go ahead, then.”

I think you just proved something the guides have never once admitted out loud in forty years of published material, Pip said. Not that the recipe works, everyone already knew the recipe works, eventually, for some people, given enough decades. I think you proved it can be approached like a real experiment instead of an act of faith, and that the approach itself changes what’s possible on a timeline nobody else has ever tried to compress. I don’t know if that matters to you tonight. I think it might matter more than the spike itself, eventually.

Nadia considered that in silence for a moment, shouldering her bag, the alcove’s cold pressing back in now that she’d stopped moving enough to generate any warmth of her own. “Maybe,” she said, finally. “Ask me again once it actually reproduces properly. Right now it’s still just one number on a page.”

Fair enough, Pip said, quiet. I’ll ask you again in exactly nine days. I’m marking the date, for what it’s worth, the same way you’re marking it in that notebook.

“Good,” Nadia said, quiet. “Then at least one of us won’t forget the exact number, if this ends up somehow taking rather longer than nine whole days to actually confirm properly.”

I don’t forget numbers, Pip said, something dry in it. That’s rather the one single thing I’m reliably good at, whatever else might still be uncertain about me these days.

“I know,” Nadia said softly, and something in her own voice carried more warmth than the sentence had strictly needed to.

She packed the meter away carefully, checked the notebook’s entry one final time against her own memory of the cycle count, and rose from the cold concrete with legs that had gone stiff from holding one position through forty repetitions, the alcove settling back into its ordinary silence around her, the Elite’s distant patrol continuing its slow circuit two hundred metres away, entirely indifferent to the fact that something in the field it scavenged from had, for one unmeasured fraction of a second, answered to her instead.
