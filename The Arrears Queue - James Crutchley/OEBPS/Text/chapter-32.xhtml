Chapter 32: Correct on Every Number

Thursday arrived the way every deadline she’d ever failed to solve in advance arrived, precisely on schedule, indifferent to the fact that she still didn’t have anything to bring to it.

She’d spent the walk to school running the same three sentences over and over, testing each one for how much truth it could survive carrying. She could say she was fine and let Val disbelieve her a second time. She could say she was working on it and let the vagueness buy another day she didn’t actually have a plan to fill. She could say nothing at all and let the silence answer for her, which was, she suspected, the version Val would trust least of the three and the version most likely to be true. None of them fixed anything. She walked the rest of the way turning the three sentences over anyway, because turning a problem over, even one with no working solution left inside it, was the only thing her hands still knew how to do with fear.

Practice itself passed in a blur she wouldn’t be able to fully reconstruct afterward, drills run half a beat behind where her body used to put them, the rest of the team’s voices arriving from somewhere slightly further away than usual. She caught Val watching her twice during warm-ups, not the sharp attentive look from Tuesday but something softer and more final, the specific expression of someone who had already made a decision and was only waiting for the right room to deliver it in.

Val had her wait in the equipment room after practice, the smaller one off the main mat floor where the team kept the extra headgear and the ancient scale nobody trusted for official weigh-ins anymore. It was a room built for storage, not conversation, and the choice of it told Nadia something before Val had said a single word: whatever was coming wasn’t meant for the team to overhear.

“Sit,” Val said, not unkindly, and pulled the second folding chair around to face her rather than standing over her the way a coach delivering a lineup decision usually would.

Nadia sat, and took in the room properly for the first time in months, the way she only ever really noticed a familiar space once something about it had changed its meaning. Headgear stacked three deep along the back shelf, a box of unmatched kneepads nobody had sorted since October, the old scale in the corner with its needle stuck a half-kilo off true, a fact the whole team had long since learned to correct for by memory rather than trust the machine itself. None of it had moved. It only felt different now, sitting across from a coach who had clearly decided, before Nadia had even walked in, exactly how this conversation was going to end.

* * *

Val had a folder with her, the kind of plain manila folder that could have held anything, and she set it on her knee without opening it, like she wanted the conversation to happen first and the paper to arrive only once the words had already done their work.

“I want to walk you through what I’m seeing,” Val said. “Not because I think you don’t already know it. I think you know most of it better than I do. But I need to say it out loud, in order, so you understand this isn’t a guess I’m making off one bad weigh-in.”

“Okay,” Nadia said. Her voice came out smaller than she wanted it to.

“October’s panel. Low haemoglobin. Low ferritin. I read that as a female athlete not eating enough iron, common enough on this team I don’t even blink at it anymore. I told you to eat more red meat and left it there.” Val’s eyes stayed level on her. “Then December. Same low numbers, plus LDH climbing, haptoglobin dropping. That combination isn’t a diet gap anymore. That’s a body breaking something down faster than it can replace it. I sat with that one a long time before I said anything, because it’s not a pattern I like landing on.”

Nadia said nothing. There wasn’t a true sentence available to her that wouldn’t make this worse.

* * *

“And then this month,” Val went on, “I watch you reclassify down a weight class in the middle of a season that was already asking a lot of you. I watch you drag through practice in a way you never used to. I ask for an outside clearance because I want someone with a licence telling me I’m wrong, and instead of bringing me that, you spend nine days getting visibly worse in front of me while insisting you were fine.” She opened the folder now, turned it so the top page faced Nadia without actually handing it over. “CK’s up again. Significantly. That one worries me more than the others, because sustained elevation like this, on top of everything else, starts putting real strain on the kidneys if it isn’t addressed.”

“I know the numbers,” Nadia said, quiet.

“I believe you do,” Val said. “I don’t think that’s the part you’re missing.”

“Do you want to know why the kidney part worries me most?” Val asked, and didn’t wait for an answer before she gave one. “CK is a marker for muscle breakdown. A little elevation after a hard practice is normal, every athlete on this roster runs high the day after a heavy session. Sustained elevation, week over week, on top of a body that’s already running a deficit it can’t seem to close, means the kidneys are filtering more of that breakdown product than they’re built to handle for very long. It doesn’t announce itself. It just quietly gets worse until one day it isn’t quiet anymore.” She said it plainly, without theatrics, the same tone she used walking a rookie through a takedown for the first time, and the ordinariness of the delivery made it land harder than a raised voice would have. “I’m not trying to frighten you. I’m telling you why I stopped being willing to wait for a form to come back on its own timeline.”

* * *

She let that sit a moment before she said the rest of it, and when she did, her voice had softened, not into doubt, but into something closer to grief.

“I’ve coached for eleven years,” Val said. “I’ve had three athletes on other teams go through exactly this shape of thing. Not the same numbers, every case looks a little different, but the same shape. A kid who’s smart, who’s disciplined, who’s good at controlling things, running a caloric deficit that’s gone past cutting weight and turned into something the deficit is doing on its own now, whether she’s still choosing it or not. Sometimes it’s about the sport. Sometimes the sport’s just where it happened to show up first.” She closed the folder gently. “I’m not saying I know exactly what this is for you. I’m saying I know what it looks like from the outside, and it looks like that.”

“I’ve been keeping a log since October,” Val added, before Nadia could answer. “Not officially, just my own notes, the kind I keep on every athlete I’m worried about. Weigh-ins. How you looked at practice. That afternoon in September you nearly went down in the parking lot and told me it was low blood sugar before I’d even asked. I believed you then. I went back and reread that entry last week with everything else sitting next to it, and it doesn’t read the same way it did in September. Almost nothing on that list reads the same way anymore, now that I know what to line it up against.”

The parking lot. She’d nearly forgotten that one herself, an early Arrears queue she hadn’t yet learned to manage draining faster than she could disguise it, five months and a hundred careful excuses ago. She hadn’t known, sitting on the curb that afternoon telling Val a lie about breakfast, that she was making the first entry in a file that would eventually read, correctly, as a pattern.

She thought about how many other entries must be sitting in that private log alongside it, small moments she’d written off at the time as nothing, a missed lift here, a slower mile there, a week in November she’d blamed on a cold that never actually produced a cough, a Sunday she’d claimed a headache to skip conditioning rather than admit the queue from Saturday’s session hadn’t finished draining yet. Val hadn’t needed a lab to start noticing. She’d needed eleven years of watching athletes come apart in slow motion and the patience to keep quiet notes on one who kept insisting, every single time, that she was fine.

“I wasn’t trying to hide it from you specifically,” Nadia said, the closest thing to an apology she could offer without an explanation attached to it. “I know that’s probably hard to believe right now.”

“I believe it,” Val said. “I don’t think you were hiding it from me. I think you were hiding it from everyone, including yourself, and I happened to be standing close enough to notice anyway. That’s not a comfort. I know it isn’t meant to be one.” She let the silence sit a moment before she went on. “I wish you’d trusted me with whatever this actually is a long time before it got here.”

Nadia’s throat worked once before anything came out. “It’s not what you think it is,” she said, and heard, even as she said it, how useless the sentence was without anything true behind it to replace what Val already believed.

“Tell me what it is, then,” Val said, gently, and waited, and the waiting was the worst part, an open door Nadia had no honest way to walk through.

She thought, in the silence, about every version of the truth she could offer that would satisfy the shape of Val’s question without handing over the substance of it. She could say it was stress. She could say it was the reclassification, the schoolwork, the general grinding weight of a year that had asked more of her than any one sixteen-year-old should reasonably be asked to carry, and every word of that would even be true, just not true in the specific way that would actually explain a haptoglobin reading a lab tech had flagged twice. Val wasn’t asking for a mood. She was asking for a mechanism, and the only honest mechanism Nadia had came with a queue and a Window and a hard cap she couldn’t say out loud in a room with a door that didn’t lock.

* * *

She sat with the silence longer than she meant to, running through every version of an answer she could give without handing Val a truth that would put them both in more danger than a wrong diagnosis ever could. A body that healed faster than any clinic chart had a column for. A queue that drained substrate no lab test could see. A wallet, spent all in one place because two requirements had turned out to be mutually exclusive and she’d tried to fund both anyway. None of it was a sentence she could say out loud in this room, to this person, no matter how much she wanted to.

“I can’t explain it the way you’re asking me to,” she said finally, and it was the truest sentence available to her, even wrapped around everything it wasn’t saying.

Val studied her for a long moment. “I know you can’t,” she said. “I’ve known that about you since October, if I’m honest. There’s always been a door I don’t get to walk through with you. I’ve tried to respect that, because everyone’s allowed one. But I can’t coach a door. I can only coach what I can see, and what I can see is a kid whose bloodwork keeps getting worse no matter what I ask her to change, and I’ve run out of room to wait for the door to open on its own.”

* * *

“I’m disqualifying you,” Val said, and the words landed with a plainness that made them somehow worse than if she’d dressed them up in anything softer. “Effective today. No practice, no conditioning, no Saturday. Not until I have an actual medical clearance in front of me, from an actual doctor, that tells me this isn’t what it looks like.”

“The tournament,” Nadia said, and hated how small her voice sounded saying it, hated that of everything in the room right now, some old reflexive part of her had reached first for the loss she was allowed to grieve out loud instead of the one she wasn’t.

“I know what the reclassification cost you,” Val said. “I watched you build it. I’m not doing this because I don’t think you earned Saturday. I’m doing this because I think you might not survive earning the next one if I don’t.”

The word landed harder than Val could have meant it to, a plain coaching phrase that happened to be, in Nadia’s case, considerably less figurative than either of them was allowed to say out loud in this room. She thought of the site, the hollow weakness on the catwalk two days ago, a queue draining slower than it should have because the deficit had already spent the margin meant to clear it. Val didn’t know how literal her own warning was. That didn’t make it wrong.

“Nine weeks of reclassification,” Nadia said, mostly to herself, the number arriving before she’d decided to say it out loud. “The forms. The extra conditioning. The whole autumn built around one Saturday.”

“I know,” Val said again, and this time there was nothing coach-flat in it at all. “I’m sorry. I mean that as plainly as I know how to mean anything. I wish there were a version of this where I got to keep both things true, you healthy and you on that mat Saturday. There isn’t one. I checked, more than once, before I called you in here.”

Nadia looked down at her own hands, at knuckles that had healed clean and fast all year in a way no coach was ever supposed to notice, at skin that carried none of the ordinary evidence of a body running on empty the way Val believed it was, and found she had nothing left to argue with.

A second thought arrived underneath the first, colder, arriving in the particular flat voice she used for compliance arithmetic rather than grief. Wrestling wasn’t only a season. It was the supervised-activity line on a form Bhandari reviewed every quarter, the specific category that had kept one whole third of her file boring since September. Losing the tournament was a loss she was, at least in theory, allowed to have out loud. Losing the category underneath it was a second problem stacked directly on top of the first one, and this one didn’t care how she felt about it. It only cared whether the line still had a signature on it the next time someone checked.

* * *

“I’m not trying to punish you,” Val said, quieter now, the coach’s flatness gone out of her voice entirely. “I want you to hear that clearly, because I don’t think it’s going to feel that way to you for a while. I’m trying to make sure there’s a you left to come back to this mat once whatever this is gets handled properly. That’s the whole reason. I don’t have a second one.”

“I understand,” Nadia said, and the terrible thing was that she did, completely, every piece of Val’s reasoning tracking clean and correct off numbers that were, individually, entirely real. It was only the shape underneath them that Val had gotten wrong, and there was no way to hand her the right shape without handing her everything else stacked behind it, the queue, the Window, a body that had spent nine days trying to fund two accounts out of one and lost both bets at once.

* * *

Val didn’t say anything for a while after that, letting the silence do whatever work silence was going to do, and Nadia sat in the folding chair with the ancient scale watching her from the corner like it had an opinion of its own about how this had gone.

“There’s something else,” Val said, finally, and something in the careful way she said it made Nadia’s stomach drop before the sentence had even finished forming. “Findings like this, in a minor, they don’t just stay between a coach and an athlete. There are people I have to tell. I don’t have a choice in that part, and I want you to hear it from me before it comes from somewhere else.”

Nadia felt the floor of the room shift under a threat that had nothing to do with gravity. “Tell who,” she said, and already knew, before Val answered, exactly which office the answer was going to lead back to.

“The school has a protocol,” Val said. “I’m required to report a suspected case. I already started the paperwork this morning, before I called you in here, because I needed to know I was going to follow through with it before I said any of this to your face.” She held Nadia’s eyes, steady, apologetic, and entirely certain. “I’m sorry. I know that’s going to open up more than a bench for Saturday. I don’t see a version of this where I look away from what I’ve been watching for months and still call myself someone who takes care of the kids on this team.”

* * *

Nadia sat with that a long moment, the equipment room’s fluorescent light humming overhead, the whole conversation collapsing down into a single sentence she couldn’t say and a single form already halfway filled out on someone else’s desk.

“Okay,” she said, because there wasn’t anything else left to say that wouldn’t make the next part harder than it was already going to be.

Val reached over and, for just a moment, put a hand on her shoulder, the same brief, steady contact she gave any athlete walking off the mat after a loss that had cost more than the scoreboard showed. “You’re going to be angry at me,” she said. “That’s fine. I’d rather have you angry and alive than agreeable and not. Go home. Rest. I’ll call you when I hear back about next steps.”

“What happens to my spot on the roster,” Nadia asked, the question arriving before she could decide whether she actually wanted the answer.

“It’s held,” Val said. “Nobody’s replacing you. This isn’t a cut from the team. It’s a hold, until there’s a clearance that actually satisfies what I’m looking at.” She hesitated, just slightly, before she added the rest of it. “I want to be honest with you about the shape of that condition, though. A clearance that satisfies me is going to need to explain the whole picture, not just tell me you’re eating better now. If whatever’s underneath this doesn’t change, the numbers won’t either, and I won’t sign off on numbers that haven’t actually changed just because I want to.”

“I understand,” Nadia said, and meant it in a way that had nothing to do with agreement and everything to do with the plain, exhausted recognition that Val’s condition was, on its own terms, entirely reasonable, and entirely impossible for her to meet with anything but a lie. A clearance built on a story instead of the truth would have to be a good one, careful, load-bearing, and she had no idea yet what shape it could possibly take.

Nadia stood, legs unsteady in a way that had nothing to do with the cut anymore, and walked out of the equipment room without trusting herself to say another word.

* * *

She made it two blocks from the school before she stopped walking altogether, standing at the edge of an empty parking lot with her bag still slung over one shoulder, staring at nothing in particular while the afternoon went on around her as though the last twenty minutes hadn’t happened at all.

Are you all right, Pip asked, careful, the question it saved for moments it already suspected the answer to.

“No,” Nadia said, flat, and found, saying it, that the single honest syllable was the first entirely true thing she’d managed to say to anyone all day.

Tell me what happened, Pip said. All of it. I only got pieces through the implant feed, and half of what came through was your pulse doing things I didn’t like.

She told it. The whole conversation, in order, the panel numbers Val had listed off one by one, the parking lot entry from September that Val had gone back and reread with new context, the disqualification, the hand on her shoulder, the sentence about a report already halfway filled out on someone else’s desk before Val had even called her in. She kept her voice level through most of it, the same flat recitation she’d have given any other after-action report, and only felt the level tone start to crack somewhere around the word report.

* * *

A report, Pip repeated, and something in its voice had gone very still, the careful stillness it usually reserved for the closest calls at the site. Nadia, do you understand what that word does inside the system you’re already flagged in.

“I understand,” she said. “I understood it the second she said it. That’s a school protocol talking to a Ministry file. That’s Bhandari getting a call that isn’t about a placement form or a supervised-activity signature. That’s a caseworker opening a folder that has an actual medical concern written on it instead of a compliance checkbox.” She pressed the heel of her hand briefly against one eye, hard enough to leave a faint ache behind when she took it away. “I spent a year keeping my file boring. She just made it interesting, and she did it because she’s trying to save my life, and I can’t even be angry about that part, because she’s not wrong about anything except the one thing that actually matters.”

She’s not wrong about the danger, Pip said, quiet. She’s just pointed the danger at the wrong door. I don’t know what happens when whoever reads that report walks up to your actual door instead.

“Neither do I,” Nadia said, and started walking again, because standing still in an empty parking lot processing a sentence like that wasn’t a posture she trusted herself to hold much longer without something in her giving out.

* * *

She walked the rest of the way home slower than usual, legs still unsteady, the cut and the deficit and nine days of trying to solve an unsolvable problem all still sitting in her muscles like debt that hadn’t finished coming due. The tournament had already receded somewhere behind her, a loss she could feel but couldn’t currently afford to grieve properly, crowded out by a much larger fear that had just opened a door she had spent a year keeping welded shut.

For what it’s worth, Pip said, somewhere past the second intersection, I don’t think you did anything wrong today. I watched you try to give her an honest answer and run out of one you could actually say. That’s not the same as lying to her.

“It felt the same from where I was sitting,” Nadia said.

I know, Pip said. I don’t think that makes it true.

She didn’t answer that, and let the rest of the walk pass in silence, turning the word report over and over the way she’d turned over every other unsolvable problem this year, looking for an angle that might make it smaller, and not finding one.

* * *

The bay held its usual after-school quiet when she let herself in, the kind of quiet that had always felt like relief before and today just felt like a room waiting for her to fall apart in it without an audience. She dropped her bag by the door instead of hanging it up, sat down hard on the edge of the bed, and let herself, finally, stop holding her spine in the careful, composed line she’d kept it in for the last forty minutes because Val had been watching and then Pip had been listening and there hadn’t been a single moment in between where falling apart would have been safe to do.

“There’s a second problem under the first one,” she said out loud, once she trusted her voice to hold steady again. “Wrestling wasn’t just wrestling. It was my supervised-activity line. Val signed that form every month since September. If I’m disqualified, that signature stops meaning anything, and I don’t have a second activity sitting ready to slide into the slot.”

I was doing that math too, Pip admitted. I didn’t want to say it while you were still standing in a parking lot. Three categories. School, supervised activity, verified placement. You lose one and you’re carrying the whole file on two legs instead of three, and Bhandari’s next review is close enough that two legs might not be enough of a cushion to explain why the third one just vanished with a medical report attached to it.

“I know,” Nadia said. “I did the arithmetic walking home. I just needed to hear someone else run it and get the same number.” Two legs instead of three, she thought again, and wondered how long a file could stand upright like that before someone with the authority to notice finally did.

* * *

She made herself eat something, eventually, mechanical about it in a way that had nothing to do with hunger and everything to do with the specific discipline of not skipping a meal on a day her body had already spent more than it had. The plate in front of her held more than the cut plan would have allowed a week ago, and she noticed, cutting into it, a strange hesitation in her own hand before the first bite, muscle memory arguing with a rule that technically no longer applied. The cut was over. Nobody was weighing her in on Saturday now. Some part of her hadn’t caught up to that fact yet, and sat there at the bench turning a fork over a plate like eating past the old boundary was still something that needed permission she hadn’t been granted.

She ate it anyway, slowly, without tasting most of it, and told herself the calculation had changed even if her body hadn’t finished believing it yet. It should have felt like relief, one column finally allowed to stand open without a ceiling pressing down on it, and instead it felt like nothing at all, a permission granted for exactly the wrong reason, arriving from exactly the wrong direction, too late to have changed anything that mattered this week.

* * *

What do you want to do tonight, Pip asked, once the plate was empty and the bay had settled back into its ordinary evening quiet. I don’t think there’s a version of tonight where you fix any of this. I just don’t want you sitting alone with it if there’s something smaller I can actually help with.

“I want to know what happens next,” Nadia said. “Not the parts I can’t control. The parts I can. When does a school report actually reach a caseworker. How long before Bhandari calls. Whether there’s anything I can do between now and then that isn’t just waiting to find out.”

I can pull what’s publicly documented about the reporting timeline, Pip said. It won’t be much. This kind of process doesn’t usually publish its own clock, and most of what governs it lives in internal procedure rather than anything a search actually surfaces.

“Pull what you can,” Nadia said. “I’d rather have a bad estimate than nothing. I’ve never once in my life done better sitting still and hoping the number showed up on its own.”

On it, Pip said, and for a while after that the bay held nothing but the quiet, patient sound of it working, and Nadia sat with her hands flat on the table, waiting for whatever came next.

The evening wore on slower than any evening had in weeks, no cut to track, no cafeteria maths to run, no last calorie to fit into a shrinking column before bed. She noticed the absence of it the way she’d have noticed a sound stopping, a background hum she hadn’t realised she’d been listening to until it went quiet. In its place sat a different kind of arithmetic entirely, one she had far less practice running: how many days before a school report became a phone call, how many phone calls before Bhandari’s next review stopped being routine, how much of the careful, boring file she’d spent a year constructing was still salvageable once someone actually opened it and started reading what was inside.

I’ve got a rough range, Pip said, eventually, quiet. It’s not good news. It’s not the worst version either. Do you want it tonight, or do you want to sleep on it first and hear it tomorrow with something in your stomach and a clearer head to run it through?

Nadia looked at the empty plate in front of her, at her own hands still flat on the table, at the ordinary bay that had, in the space of one afternoon, become the last quiet room she was reasonably sure still belonged only to her.

“Tonight,” she said. “I’d rather know. Waiting until morning just means I lie here running the same numbers you already have.”
