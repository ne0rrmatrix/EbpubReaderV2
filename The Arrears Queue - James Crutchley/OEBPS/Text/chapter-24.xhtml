Chapter 24: One Wallet

She started with the convergent steps, exactly as the plan specified, and the first three weeks went well enough that she let herself believe, quietly, that the model might simply work the way she’d designed it to, clean data arriving on schedule, no surprises, a fault-tracing exercise that behaved like every other fault-tracing exercise she’d ever run.

Phase One reproduced reliably now, twenty-six cycles, the meter spiking every time within a margin she trusted. Phase Six, the second convergent step she tested, took longer, four separate sessions before she isolated the specific posture variant that actually moved the needle, but it moved, and she logged the result in the same flat hand she used for everything and moved on to Phase Eleven.

Pip had taken to keeping a running count of confirmed steps against the sixteen still untested, a small private tally it mentioned only occasionally, and by the time Phase Six finally cooperated it had started referring to the whole project, half-seriously, as ahead of schedule, a phrase Nadia refused to let herself trust even as some quiet part of her privately agreed with it. Two steps down. Fifteen to go, counting the three unconfirmed ones she hadn’t yet touched. The pace, if it held, would clear the table well inside the season, and she had been careful, deliberately, not to let herself believe in a pace before it had actually been tested against something harder than an easy week.

Phase Eleven was where the numbers stopped agreeing with each other.

She hadn’t expected trouble from it specifically. Phase Eleven sat well down the list, past the steps she’d flagged as riskiest, a convergent instruction with the cleanest agreement of any row in her spreadsheet, five independent authors describing the exact same duration to within minutes of each other despite having, as far as she could tell, no contact across four decades and two continents. If anything ought to have behaved predictably, it was this one. She had, if she was honest with herself, been looking forward to it, the closest thing to an easy win the whole table offered her.

* * *

She ran the test on a Thursday that had already asked a great deal of her, a full wrestling practice in the morning before school, a shift at Pearce’s in the afternoon, and a dungeon session banked against the ledger in the evening, three demands stacked into a single day the way she’d been stacking them for weeks without any particular problem showing up in the seams.

The practice alone had run harder than most, Val pushing the team through a live-round rotation meant to simulate tournament conditions, and Nadia had come off the mat with the specific hollow-legged fatigue that meant she’d genuinely emptied something rather than merely worked up a sweat. The shift at Pearce’s had asked less of her body and more of her attention, an afternoon spent untangling a customer’s hopelessly cross-wired thermostat while the register hummed along without her needing to think about it, and by the time she’d locked up and started toward the site she’d already logged, without quite noticing she was doing it, the particular tiredness of a day that had asked for three different kinds of effort in a row.

Phase Eleven asked for absolute stillness held for a duration measured in hours rather than minutes, the step every guide in her comparison had agreed on to within a margin she’d found genuinely difficult to explain away as coincidence. She settled into it at the alcove, meter propped and ready, expecting the same clean confirmation Phase One and Phase Six had already given her.

Nothing happened. Not a flicker. Not a tremor on the readout. She held the position for the full duration specified, past it, well past it, and the meter sat flat the entire time, a result so thoroughly negative it read less like a failed test and more like an instrument that had simply stopped working.

Her body told a different story than the meter did, and she catalogued that discrepancy too, the way she catalogued anything that refused to match. Her legs had gone leaden somewhere around the ninety-minute mark, a fatigue that had nothing to do with the stillness the posture demanded and everything to do with a day that had already asked three separate versions of maximum effort from a body running on a caloric deficit it hadn’t fully replenished since Tuesday. She’d assumed, sitting down at the alcove, that the tiredness was simply background noise, the ordinary cost of an ordinary heavy day. She hadn’t yet connected it to the meter’s silence. That connection was still an hour away.

That’s strange, Pip said, watching the readout with her. A convergent step, confirmed by five independent sources, and tonight it does nothing at all.

“I know,” Nadia said, low, checking the meter’s calibration against the reference weight she still carried for exactly this reason. The calibration held. The instrument was fine. Whatever had failed tonight had failed somewhere else.

She ran through the usual suspects methodically, the way she’d run through them for any fault that refused to present a cause on the first pass. Posture, checked against the guide’s diagram twice, correct both times. Duration, held well past the specified minimum, ruling out a threshold she simply hadn’t reached. Environment, unchanged from every other session in this same alcove, the field density reading identical on the meter to every prior baseline she’d logged since October. None of it explained the silence. Everything about tonight matched every other night she’d run this exact step, except for the result.

Could it simply be a bad night, Pip offered, careful. Not every reproducible result reproduces every single time. Even convergent steps might have some natural variance nobody’s ever bothered documenting.

“Maybe,” Nadia said, unconvinced, packing the meter away with more care than the disappointment strictly warranted. “I don’t like leaving it there, though. A step that fails once and I never test again isn’t data. It’s just a story I told myself to explain away an inconvenient result.”

* * *

She ran it again two nights later, on a lighter day, no wrestling practice, no shift at Pearce’s, only school and a full night’s sleep behind her, and Phase Eleven produced a result within the first ninety minutes, clean, unmistakable, exactly the shape the convergent guides had described.

She sat with the discrepancy for a long time afterward, turning it the way she turned any two readings that refused to agree, because a step that worked on one night and failed completely on another wasn’t behaving like a step with a fixed threshold. It was behaving like a step competing for something that simply hadn’t been available on the Thursday.

The comparison she kept returning to was a board she’d repaired two summers ago, a controller that ran flawlessly on the bench and dropped out intermittently the moment it was reinstalled in the machine it actually belonged to. She’d spent three frustrating evenings chasing a phantom fault in the board itself before finally testing the machine’s power supply under real load and finding the actual problem sitting there the entire time, a rail that sagged under demand the bench’s clean supply had never once reproduced. The board had never been broken. It had simply been starved, intermittently, by a system that couldn’t always give it what it needed.

She thought about that board for a long while, turning the parallel over from every angle she could find, because a parallel that held up under scrutiny was worth trusting and one that only looked convincing on the surface was worth discarding fast, before it led her somewhere wrong. This one held. Phase Eleven wasn’t broken. It was being starved, intermittently, by a body that couldn’t always give it what it needed either, and the two nights she’d already run it were exactly the kind of contrast a sagging power rail would have produced, clean on a light day, starved on a heavy one.

“It’s not the step,” she said, finally, working it through out loud the way she worked through anything complicated enough to need saying twice. “It’s the budget. Thursday I’d already spent everything on the queue and the shift and the session. There was nothing left for Phase Eleven to draw on, so it didn’t draw on anything, so nothing happened.”

You’re saying it’s competing directly with the Arrears clearance, Pip said, careful. Not metaphorically. The same resource.

“I think it has to be,” Nadia said. “Calories. Protein. Iron. Sleep. Whatever the queue eats to clear itself, the model eats the same things to build itself. I’ve been treating them like two separate accounts and they’re not. They’re the same account.”

That seems like a significant thing to have discovered by accident, Pip said. A convergent step, failing silently, on exactly the kind of day nobody would think to flag as unusual.

“That’s probably why nobody ever noticed it before,” Nadia said, thinking it through out loud. “An ordinary practitioner isn’t running an Arrears queue underground on top of everything else. Their body’s only competing demand is being an ordinary body. Mine has three. The competition was probably always there, in theory, buried under years of practice slow enough that nobody would ever notice a single bad night mattering. I noticed because I’m compressing a decade into months, and because I happen to also be doing something else entirely that eats from the exact same account.”

So the guides were never wrong about Phase Eleven, Pip said. They just never had a reason to test it against a body spending itself three ways at once.

“Right,” Nadia said. “It’s not a flaw in the recipe. It’s a constraint nobody who wrote the recipe ever had to think about, because nobody who wrote it was also running an Arrears Engine on the side.”

* * *

She spent the next several days confirming it properly, the way she confirmed anything before letting herself trust it, running Phase Eleven on a rotation of deliberately varied days, a heavy dungeon day, a rest day, a day with a full practice and nothing underground at all, logging every variable she could think to control for against the result the meter gave her.

The pattern held cleanly across six separate trials. On any day where her Arrears queue had drawn down hard, whether from a session or from practice or from the ordinary wear of being a body that trained six days a week, Phase Eleven produced nothing. On any day where the queue sat light and her sleep had been genuine, it produced a result every single time.

She recorded each trial with the same care she gave any dataset she meant to defend later, date, hours of sleep the night before, calories logged against her own careful tracking, an estimate of the queue’s residual load pulled from the previous session’s notes, and the meter’s verdict at the end of it, positive or flat, nothing in between. The fourth trial nearly broke the pattern, a moderate day that should have sat somewhere in the middle and instead produced a clean positive, and she spent an uncomfortable hour convinced the whole theory was about to collapse before she found the explanation sitting in her own log from three days earlier, a rest day she’d taken without realising how much slack it had actually built back into the account.

That single near-miss did more to convince her than any of the clean results had. A theory that predicted correctly every time she expected it to was satisfying. A theory that correctly explained the one result that looked like an exception, once she went back and found the reason, was the kind of theory she actually trusted.

You look almost pleased, Pip said, watching her cross-check the fourth trial against the earlier rest day one final time.

“I am pleased,” Nadia said. “Not about what it means. About the fact that it held up. I’d rather find out my body is running three demands off one wallet and know it for certain than keep guessing and hope the guessing happens to be right on the nights that matter.”

That’s a very particular kind of relief, Pip said. Being glad to have confirmed something you don’t actually want to be true.

“It’s the only kind of relief that’s ever reliable,” Nadia said, closing the notebook on the sixth trial. “Feelings lie. Confirmed data doesn’t. I’ll take confirmed and unwelcome over comfortable and wrong every single time.”

“Three demands,” she said, laying the six trials out on the bench in front of her, the numbers ruled into neat columns the way she ruled anything she meant to trust later. “The queue clearing itself. The model building itself. And just being a body that’s supposed to be growing, sixteen years old, doing all the ordinary repair a body that age is supposed to be doing anyway. Three separate claims on one wallet, and I’ve been spending from it like it had three separate accounts behind it.”

And the cut, Pip said, quiet.

Nadia looked at the numbers again, the wrestling column, the tightest column of the six, every trial run during a week where her caloric intake had been deliberately, carefully restricted to make a number on a scale. “The cut doesn’t add a fourth demand,” she said slowly, working it out as she said it. “It shrinks the wallet. Same three demands, less money in the account to spend on any of them. That’s why Thursday failed so completely. Heavy day, heavy cut, nothing left over for anything.”

She drew the model out properly on a fresh page, the way she’d have drawn a circuit diagram for anything with this many interacting parts, a single rectangle labelled substrate, three arrows leading out of it toward three smaller boxes, queue clearance, core formation, ordinary growth, and a fourth line, drawn differently from the others, a valve rather than a demand, choking the rectangle’s own input down according to however deep the week’s cut happened to be running.

It was, she thought, looking at the finished diagram, the most honest picture she’d ever drawn of what her body was actually doing to itself this year, three departments fighting over one shrinking budget, and a policy decision from an entirely different part of her life reaching in and cutting that budget further on purpose, for reasons that had nothing to do with any of the three departments and everything to do with a number on a scale twice a week.

That’s a genuinely elegant way to draw it, Pip said, studying the diagram along with her. I mean that as a real compliment. I don’t think I could have laid it out that clearly myself, and I’ve had access to every piece of the underlying data the whole time.

“It’s just a systems diagram,” Nadia said. “I draw one every time I need to see all the pieces of a problem in the same place at once. This one’s just about me instead of a board, which I suppose is the part that makes it harder to look at.”

* * *

She thought about the arithmetic of it properly that night, back at the bay, the six trial results spread across the bench under the good light, and found the shape of the problem considerably larger than she’d let herself notice while she was still in the middle of collecting the data.

Wrestling season ran through February. The cut would only get tighter from here, not looser, every gram she still had to find between now and the tournament coming directly out of a budget that had already proven, empirically, six times over, that it couldn’t currently support both the queue and the model at once. The core-building thread she’d just spent weeks deciding to commit to, the model she’d built specifically because she didn’t have eight years to spare, was now revealing itself to be a thread that could only actually progress on the exact days her body had the least reason to be generous with itself.

“It’s going to slow down,” she said, out loud, mostly to hear the conclusion in her own voice rather than only in her head. “Not stop. Slow down. Every rest day is worth more to the model now than it used to be, and every rest day is also the day I most need for the queue. I’m going to be rationing which one gets the good days.”

She thought about the wrestling calendar taped inside her locker, the tournament date circled twice, the two dual meets scheduled either side of it, every one of them a heavy day by definition, every one of them a day the queue would claim absolutely and the model would get nothing from at all. Ten weeks of season left. She counted the genuinely light days remaining between now and February on her fingers, running the arithmetic the way she ran anything with a hard deadline attached, and the number that came back was smaller than she wanted it to be, smaller by a margin that mattered.

Seventeen. Seventeen light days, at most, assuming nothing else went wrong between now and the season’s end, assuming no additional dungeon session ever ran long, assuming her sleep held and nothing forced an unplanned rest day to be spent on recovery instead of the model. Seventeen days to move through however many of the remaining fourteen steps still needed testing, a number that had felt generous when she’d first built the plan in the fall and felt considerably less generous now that she understood exactly how narrow a light day actually was.

That’s a genuinely difficult trade to be making at sixteen, Pip said, something careful in it that wasn’t quite sympathy and wasn’t quite anything else she had a name for.

“It’s just arithmetic,” Nadia said, though even as she said it she heard how thin the sentence sounded against the size of what it was actually describing. “I budget. I’ve always budgeted. This is just one more thing that needs a line in the budget instead of getting to run free.”

Most sixteen-year-olds don’t have a budget for their own core formation, Pip said. I want to say that plainly, in case it’s gotten lost somewhere under all the columns and the arithmetic. This is not an ordinary problem, even by the standard of everything else that isn’t ordinary about your year.

“I know it isn’t ordinary,” Nadia said. “I stopped expecting ordinary a long time ago. I just need it to be solvable, and solvable means finding the numbers, and I found the numbers. Seventeen light days. That’s what I’ve got to work with. Complaining about the number doesn’t change it. Only the plan changes it, and I already built the plan.”

You built the plan in about twenty minutes, sitting at this bench, with the diagram still wet, Pip said. I watched you do it. I don’t think most people could do that. I think most people would need considerably longer to even accept the numbers, let alone act on them.

Nadia considered that, turning her pen over once in her fingers before setting it down properly beside the notebook. “Most people haven’t had a year of practice accepting numbers they don’t like,” she said. “I’ve had a great deal of practice. It doesn’t make the numbers hurt less. It just means I don’t waste time arguing with them before I get to work.”

* * *

She rebuilt her weekly schedule that night, the same disciplined restructuring she’d have applied to any system with more competing demands than it had resources to meet, blocking out which days belonged to which priority rather than assuming, the way she had for weeks, that everything could simply happen whenever there was a spare hour to put it in.

She started from a blank page rather than editing the old schedule, because a system this fundamentally changed deserved a fresh start rather than a patch layered over assumptions that no longer held, the same instinct that told her, on a board bad enough, that a full rebuild sometimes cost less time in the end than chasing one more intermittent fault through a design that had already proven it couldn’t be trusted.

Heavy wrestling days and dungeon-session days went into one column, protected, the queue’s claim on those days absolute and non-negotiable, because a body that couldn’t clear its Arrears properly was a body that eventually stopped surviving the site at all. Lighter days, the handful scattered through any given week where school ran ordinary and no session was scheduled, went into a second column reserved for the model, Phase Eleven and whatever came after it claiming whatever the queue hadn’t already spent.

A third column, thinner than either of the other two, held the days she genuinely couldn’t predict in advance, a Sunday that might turn into an extra shift at Pearce’s if a shipment came in unannounced, an evening that might get eaten by homework she’d underestimated, the ordinary unpredictability of a life with this many moving parts already in it. She marked those days provisional, neither the queue’s nor the model’s until the day itself revealed which demand actually needed it more, a small flexible buffer built into a system that otherwise had almost none left anywhere else.

She checked the finished schedule against the wrestling calendar one more time, cross-referencing every heavy day against the season’s actual fixture list rather than trusting her memory of it, because a schedule built on a misremembered date was a schedule that would fail exactly when she could least afford it to. The two lists matched. She allowed herself a small, private satisfaction at that, the same satisfaction she felt whenever a repair held up under a second, more skeptical inspection.

She pinned the finished schedule to the corkboard above the bench where the wrestling calendar and the placement-hours reminder already lived, one more sheet of paper governing one more piece of a life that had, somewhere over the last year, become almost entirely a matter of paper and columns and careful, ruled lines. She stood back and looked at the corkboard as a whole for a moment, four separate documents now, each one a different attempt to impose some order on a year that had never once offered any on its own.

It left almost nothing over. She sat looking at the finished schedule for a long moment, three columns and a fourth she hadn’t needed to draw because it was already implied in how thin the other three had gotten, the specific arithmetic of a sixteen-year-old body being asked to fund a wrestling season, a magic model, and its own ordinary growth, all at once, on a budget that a weight cut had just made smaller than it had been a month ago and was only going to keep shrinking.

She thought, looking at the schedule, about the ordinary growth column specifically, the one demand on the diagram that had no clock attached to it at all, no tournament date, no nine-day reproduction window, nothing forcing it to compete loudly for its share the way the other two did. It simply sat there, quiet, patient, doing whatever unglamorous maintenance a growing body needed done, bone density and immune function and everything else that never announced itself the way a level bracket or a meter spike did. She wondered, briefly, what happened to a demand that never got to make noise for itself, whether it simply took what was left after the louder two had finished arguing, or whether it had its own way of eventually forcing the issue that she hadn’t yet been unlucky enough to discover.

You’re thinking about the third box now, Pip observed. The one that isn’t the queue and isn’t the model.

“I’m thinking about what happens to it while the other two are busy fighting,” Nadia said. “It doesn’t get a notebook. It doesn’t get a meter reading. I don’t actually have any way of knowing whether it’s losing, other than the fact that it’s never once complained about it in a language I know how to read.”

That might be the part of this I find hardest to sit with, Pip said, quiet. Not the queue. Not the model. The demand with no instrument attached to it at all.

You didn’t leave yourself anything, Pip said, reading the schedule over her shoulder in whatever way it read anything. Not one day that belongs to just resting, with nothing claimed against it at all.

“I don’t have a day like that to give,” Nadia said. “Not right now. Maybe after the tournament, if the cut eases off.”

And if it doesn’t ease off, Pip said. If Level 3 costs you the way Level 2 already has.

Nadia didn’t answer that one right away, looking at the three tight columns and the schedule’s empty margins, because she didn’t yet have an honest answer that wasn’t simply going to keep running the numbers and hoping the arithmetic found her more room than it currently showed.

She thought instead about the season ahead, February arriving eventually the way every deadline eventually arrived, and what the far side of it actually looked like once the cut finally lifted. Maintenance calories again. A wallet returning to something closer to its full size, three demands still competing but at least competing over a budget that hadn’t been deliberately narrowed on top of everything else. She let herself hold that image for a moment, the first genuinely hopeful thing she’d allowed herself since sitting down with the diagram, and then set it aside again, because hope wasn’t a resource the schedule could actually spend, and the schedule was the only thing standing between her and the next seventeen weeks.

You went quiet, Pip said, gentle.

“I was thinking about after,” Nadia said. “Once the season’s over and the cut lifts. It won’t fix everything. It’ll help. I’m allowed to hold onto that for a second before I go back to the actual arithmetic.”

You are allowed, Pip said. I don’t think you give yourself that permission very often. I noticed, just now, that you gave it to yourself for about four seconds before you took it back.

“Four seconds is what I had time for,” Nadia said, and there was something almost like humour in it, tired but real.

I’ll take four seconds, Pip said. It’s more than you gave yourself last month, and considerably more than you gave yourself the month before that. I keep a running count of that too, if you were wondering.

“Then I find something else to cut,” she said, finally. “I always find something.”

I know you do, Pip said, quiet. I’d just like it, once, if the thing you found didn’t come out of you.

She didn’t have an answer for that either, and closed the notebook instead, the schedule ruled and finished, three demands and one wallet and a cut sitting on top of all of it, exactly as narrow as the numbers said it had to be.

She sat a while longer at the bench before actually going to bed, the compressor ticking through its cycle on the other side of the wall, the finished schedule sitting under the good light where she’d be able to find it first thing the next morning without having to search. She thought about Phase Eleven, the step that had started all of this, sitting somewhere out past the fourteen remaining rows in her table, waiting for whichever light day the schedule finally handed it. It would come. She’d make sure it came. That was, in the end, the entire function of a plan this careful, not to make the numbers larger, only to make sure that whatever room actually existed got spent on purpose instead of by accident.

One more thing, Pip said, as she finally reached for the bench light’s switch. For what it’s worth. I think the version of you that built this schedule tonight is exactly the version of you I’d have wanted running it, if anyone had asked me to choose.

“Nobody asked you to choose,” Nadia said, not unkindly, the light going out under her hand.

I know, Pip said. I’m saying it anyway.

I don’t say most of what I actually think, it added, after a moment. There isn’t always room for it, and you have enough to carry without my opinions stacked on top. But I wanted this one on the record before tonight closed properly.

“Noted,” Nadia said, quiet, and meant it more than the single word let on.

She turned off the light properly this time, and let the bay settle into the ordinary dark it kept most nights, the finished schedule waiting on the corkboard, three demands and one wallet, ready for whatever the morning actually asked of it, whichever column it decided to claim first.

She sat in the dark a moment longer, the schedule’s three thin columns already committed to memory the way anything she’d built carefully enough always was, and let the compressor’s steady tick be the last sound of the night before she finally went to sleep.

Sleep came slower than usual, her mind still working the diagram’s edges even with her eyes closed, the three boxes and the valve cutting into all of them at once refusing to fully settle the way a finished repair usually settled once the last screw went back in. Somewhere in the last stretch before she actually drifted off, she found herself running the arithmetic one more time regardless, seventeen light days, fourteen steps, a wallet that would only keep shrinking until February, and let the numbers carry her the rest of the way down into sleep, the same way they always eventually did.
