Chapter 33: No One to Sign

The call came on Monday, four days after the equipment room, from a number that wasn’t Bhandari’s usual extension.

“This isn’t your quarterly,” the voice on the other end said, brisk, unfamiliar, reading off something rather than speaking from memory. “We need you in for an intake meeting. Wednesday, nine a.m., bring photo ID. Ms. Bhandari will be conducting it.”

“An intake meeting for what,” Nadia asked, already knowing, in the flat certain way she knew a fault before the diagnostic finished confirming it, exactly what the answer was going to be.

“That’ll be covered on Wednesday,” the voice said, and hung up before she could ask anything else.

That wasn’t Bhandari, Pip said, once the call had ended. That was someone reading from an intake script. I don’t like how fast this moved.

“Four days,” Nadia said. “I thought we had longer than four days.”

So did I, Pip admitted. I was wrong about the clock. I’m sorry. I should have told you the estimate was softer than I made it sound.

She’d spent the two days between the equipment room and the phone call doing the only thing she knew how to do with a problem she couldn’t yet see the edges of, which was try to build a map of it in advance. She’d had Pip pull whatever public information existed on how the Ministry classified a school-filed protection report, what timelines governed it, what rights a minor in provincial care actually held once one landed. Most of what came back was thin, procedural, written in the specific bloodless language of a policy manual that assumed a reader who already had a lawyer or a social worker translating it for them in real time. She’d read it twice anyway, highlighting phrases she didn’t fully understand, building the same kind of working folder she’d built for every Bhandari review since she’d aged out, because a folder she’d built herself had never once, in six months, failed to be more useful than simply hoping the system would explain itself to her clearly the first time.

None of it had prepared her for how fast Wednesday’s call would actually arrive, four days instead of the two or three weeks the policy language had implied for an ordinary compliance flag. She understood now, standing in the bay with the phone still warm in her hand, that a protection report didn’t run on the same clock a compliance flag did, and that she’d built her entire two-day map off the wrong document without realising it.

“I read the wrong policy,” she said, flat.

You read the only policy that was publicly available, Pip said. That’s not the same as reading it wrong. I think this whole category of process just isn’t built to be legible from the outside, and I don’t think that’s an accident.

* * *

Wednesday’s waiting room looked the same as it always did, the same plastic chairs, the same corkboard with its curling flyers about tenant rights and food bank hours, and Nadia sat in it with a stillness that had nothing to do with patience, running through the three sentences she’d rehearsed on the bus and finding none of them held up any better in daylight than they had the night before.

She’d dressed carefully that morning, the way she dressed for anything where the room’s first read of her mattered more than it should have, nothing that would draw attention, nothing that would give a stranger reading her file an easy detail to fixate on. She’d eaten a full breakfast, the largest one she’d let herself have in months, not out of hunger but out of a colder calculation entirely, the specific awareness that whoever looked at her in that cubicle today was going to be looking for exactly the kind of body the report described, and she wanted, if nothing else, to hand them one visible piece of evidence that argued the other way, even knowing a single breakfast wouldn’t move a haptoglobin reading anyone had already flagged twice.

Two other people sat in the row of chairs with her, an older man reading something on his phone with the specific studied blankness of someone determined not to make eye contact with anyone in a government waiting room, and a woman about Val’s age holding a toddler who kept trying, without much success, to escape her lap and investigate the corkboard. Nadia watched the toddler for longer than she meant to, some part of her cataloguing, without deciding to, the ordinary unbothered ease of a small child who had no idea what kind of building she was sitting in or what kind of decisions got made inside it.

Bhandari came out to collect her personally rather than calling her name from the doorway the way the receptionist usually did, and the difference in that small procedural detail told Nadia more than anything said so far.

“Come on back,” Bhandari said, and her voice carried none of the easy, unhurried warmth from the last review. It was careful now, measured, the specific tone of someone handling something she wished she weren’t required to handle at all.

* * *

The cubicle looked the same, the same felt walls, the same curling photo of two kids in matching sweaters pinned at the corner, but the file open on the screen was a different colour scheme than Nadia had seen before, a header in red rather than the ordinary grey of a compliance summary.

“I received a report Thursday afternoon,” Bhandari said, not making Nadia ask for it. “From your school district, filed by your athletics department. A minor, in provincial care, presenting bloodwork consistent with malnutrition and possible disordered eating, flagged by a coach who documented her concerns in detail before submitting it.” She turned the screen slightly, not far enough for Nadia to actually read it, just far enough to establish that it existed. “This isn’t a compliance file anymore. It’s a protection concern. Different category. Different rules attached to it.”

“Can I see it,” Nadia asked. “Not the whole file. Just what the report actually says. I’d rather know exactly what I’m responding to than guess.”

Bhandari considered that a moment, then turned the screen fully around, a small procedural courtesy Nadia understood wasn’t strictly required. The report ran three paragraphs, clinical, careful, exactly the shape of language Val would have written, each sentence hedged with words like suspected and consistent with that did nothing to soften the weight of the specific numbers listed underneath them. Near the bottom, under a heading marked Reporting Party Statement, a single line stood out from the rest, less clinical, more plainly Val’s own voice bleeding through the form’s careful structure: I have coached this athlete for two seasons and have never had reason to doubt her honesty. I am filing this report because I am frightened for her, not because I believe she is lying to me. Nadia read that sentence three times before she trusted herself to move past it, and handed the screen back with a small nod rather than trusting her voice for a moment.

“I know what it says,” Nadia said, once she had her voice back under her. “It’s wrong. Not about the numbers. About what the numbers mean.”

“I believe that you believe that,” Bhandari said, gently, and the sentence landed exactly as carefully as it had clearly been built to land. “I need you to understand something before we go any further, and I need you to hear it as information, not as me arguing with you. Once a report like this is filed, on a file like yours, I don’t have discretion to simply decide it isn’t true and close it back up. There’s a process. It runs whether either of us wants it to or not.”

“What does the process actually involve,” Nadia asked. “Concretely. Not the category name. What happens, in order, starting today.”

Bhandari seemed almost relieved by the specificity of the question, the way a form was always easier to answer than a feeling. “Today, I log the intake and confirm you’ve been informed of the finding, which is what we’re doing right now. Within seventy-two hours, a supervisor reviews the file and either upgrades the concern level or confirms it at its current tier. Within two weeks of that confirmation, a medical follow-up gets scheduled with a Ministry-approved provider. Depending on what that follow-up finds, the file either closes with a clearance note, or it escalates into a formal safety plan, which can include changes to housing, increased check-in frequency, and referral to specialised services.” She said it the way she’d clearly said it many times before, to many other kids in many other chairs, a recitation worn smooth by repetition rather than indifference. “I know it sounds like a lot of machinery for three paragraphs on a screen. It is a lot of machinery. It exists because sometimes three paragraphs on a screen are the only warning anyone gets before something much worse happens to a kid nobody was watching closely enough.”

* * *

“Then let me contest it,” Nadia said. “Get a second opinion. Show that the coach who filed it doesn’t actually know what she’s looking at.”

Bhandari was quiet for a moment, and something in the quiet made Nadia’s stomach drop before a single word of the answer arrived.

“You can request an independent assessment,” Bhandari said. “That’s a real option, and I’d support you requesting it. But the process for a minor to formally contest a protection finding runs through a guardian. A parent files the objection, requests the second opinion, sits in the review hearing beside their kid and argues the case alongside her. That’s how the appeal is built. It assumes there’s an adult in the room whose interests point the same direction as the child’s, standing outside the system that made the finding, able to push back against it.”

“I don’t have one of those,” Nadia said, and heard how small the sentence came out, five words carrying the entire weight of the last six months behind them.

“I know,” Bhandari said. “That’s the part I need you to understand clearly, because I don’t think anyone’s ever laid it out for you this plainly before, and I’d rather you hear it from me than discover it three steps into a process that isn’t going to work the way you’re hoping. The Ministry is your guardian of record. That means, legally, the party who would normally advocate for you against a finding like this and the party who made the finding are the same party. There’s no one standing outside this to argue your side. The system is supposed to be your parent right now, and a parent doesn’t appeal itself.”

“That can’t be the whole design,” Nadia said. “There has to be someone whose job is specifically to check the Ministry’s own work. An ombudsperson. A tribunal. Something.”

“There is,” Bhandari said. “The Representative for Children and Youth’s office exists for exactly that reason, and I can give you the number before you leave today. But that office investigates patterns and systemic failures, mostly, and individual complaints go into a queue that isn’t built for a two-week medical timeline. It’s a real avenue. It’s not a fast one, and it’s not designed to pause an active safety process while it works through your specific file.” She spread her hands slightly, a small, tired gesture that carried more honesty in it than anything she’d said so far. “I wish I had a better answer for you. I’ve looked, more than once, for kids in exactly your position. The honest answer is that the system assumes a family standing behind every child in it, arguing on their behalf when the system gets something wrong, and it doesn’t know what to do with a file where that assumption simply isn’t true. You’re not the first kid I’ve watched fall into that gap. I don’t think you’ll be the last.”

* * *

Nadia sat with that for a long moment, the cubicle’s fluorescent light humming the same flat hum it always hummed, and felt the shape of the trap close all the way around her for the first time since Val had said the word report in an equipment room four days ago.

“So there’s nothing,” she said. “No process. No door. Just whatever the Ministry decides is true about me, and no one whose job it is to check whether it actually is.”

“There’s a door,” Bhandari said. “It’s just a narrower one than the one built for a kid with parents. You can request your own hearing once you turn nineteen, or petition for it earlier with legal representation, which I can help you apply to get, though the waitlist for a youth legal advocate runs longer than the timeline on the medical follow-up we’re required to schedule regardless. In the meantime, the finding stands, and the process attached to it moves forward, because a finding like this doesn’t get to just sit unaddressed while a nineteen-year-old’s hearing date arrives.”

“How long,” Nadia asked.

“The medical follow-up gets scheduled inside two weeks,” Bhandari said. “A Ministry-approved physician, a full workup, someone with the authority to either confirm the concern or clear it properly, on the record, in a way a coach’s bloodwork can’t on its own.” She hesitated, the first genuine hesitation Nadia had seen from her all morning. “I want to be honest with you about what happens if that workup doesn’t clear you. It doesn’t stay contained to a doctor’s note. It affects housing. It affects your activity category, which I understand is already sitting open after the disqualification. It potentially affects placement review generally, because a file this flagged doesn’t stay in the same tier of oversight it was sitting in a week ago.”

* * *

“I didn’t do anything wrong,” Nadia said, and the sentence came out rawer than she meant it to, the first crack in a composure she’d been holding since the equipment room.

“I don’t think you did either,” Bhandari said, quiet, and something in her voice made Nadia look up properly for the first time since sitting down. “For what it’s worth, and I know it isn’t worth much right now. I’ve read your file more carefully than almost any other file on my desk, because you’ve never once given me a reason not to trust what you tell me, and every instinct I have says something doesn’t add up between the numbers on that report and the kid who sat across this desk from me in October and told me, straight, that she’d rather hear bad news early than have it arrive as a surprise.” She folded her hands on the desk, the same gesture from the last review, except this time it didn’t precede a question that cost her nothing. “But I don’t get to run this file on instinct. I get to run it on the process the report triggered, and the process doesn’t care what my instinct says.”

“What would you do,” Nadia asked, “if it were your file. Not mine. Yours, with all the same numbers on it and none of the answers I’m not allowed to give you.”

Bhandari was quiet long enough that Nadia thought, for a moment, she wasn’t going to answer at all. “Honestly,” she said, finally, “I’d show up to the appointment, cooperate fully, and hope the version of me that walked in matched whatever the finding was actually built on. I don’t give that advice because I think it’s guaranteed to work. I give it because in four years of doing this job, I’ve never once seen a file improve its position by fighting the process instead of engaging with it. The ones who fight it usually just make the escalation faster.”

“That’s not really an answer to what you’d do,” Nadia said. “That’s an answer to what you’d tell someone else to do, from behind a desk, with the process safely on your side of it.”

Something that might almost have been a smile crossed Bhandari’s face, brief, tired, gone before it fully arrived. “You’re right,” she said. “It is. I don’t think I get to answer the real version of your question honestly in this chair. I’m not sure I’d answer it honestly in any chair. Some questions you only get to ask yourself.”

“Then what do I do,” Nadia said, and the question came out closer to a plea than she’d ever let herself sound in this room before.

“You show up to the appointment,” Bhandari said. “You let the doctor do the workup properly. If there’s an explanation that clears the concern, that’s the fastest, cleanest way it gets cleared, faster than any hearing I could help you request. I know that’s not the answer you wanted. It’s the honest one.”

* * *

Nadia sat with that answer the way she sat with a fault she couldn’t yet see the shape of, turning it over for an angle that might make it smaller, and finding, for the first time in longer than she could comfortably remember, that the problem simply didn’t have one. A medical workup thorough enough to satisfy a Ministry-approved physician would see exactly what Val’s panel had already flagged, run through instruments considerably less forgiving than a school trainer’s basic order, and there was no honest version of that appointment that ended anywhere except deeper inside the exact machinery she’d spent a year keeping herself out of.

“Okay,” she said, finally, because there wasn’t another word left in the room that would have done any more work than that one.

Bhandari studied her a moment longer, the same patient, careful attention from the last review, except heavier now, weighted down by everything sitting on the screen between them. “I know this isn’t the meeting either of us wanted to be having,” she said. “I want you to know I’ll flag every mitigating note I can find room for in this file. I don’t control the outcome. I do control how fairly the record reads going in.”

“Thank you,” Nadia said, and meant it, the same uncomfortable warmth from the last review rising again despite everything else sitting in the room with it, a kindness that cost Bhandari something real this time and was offered anyway.

“One more thing, before you go,” Bhandari said, and reached for a slip of paper, the same small economical motion she’d used to hand across the clinic addresses that had, in the end, never gotten used. “Housing isn’t affected yet. I want to be clear about that specifically, because I know Kelso House is the thing you’re least willing to lose out of all of this, and I don’t want you spending the next two weeks bracing for a call about your placement that isn’t coming today. If the medical follow-up escalates, housing conversations happen after that, not before. You have two weeks where that particular fear, at least, doesn’t need to be the one you’re carrying.”

“Thank you for saying that specifically,” Nadia said, and meant that too, a small mercy inside a morning that had offered almost none, one worry set down, at least for now, out of the several she’d walked in carrying.

Bhandari wrote a phone number on the slip and slid it across the desk, the Representative’s office, the same careful handwriting from the clinic addresses. “Call them anyway,” she said. “Even if it doesn’t move fast enough to matter for the two-week window. Get your name in a system that exists to notice patterns like this one, so that if this ever happens to you again, or to someone else in a position like yours, there’s a record showing it happened before.”

“I will,” Nadia said, and folded the slip carefully into the same pocket where she’d once folded Val’s two clinic addresses, a habit she was beginning to recognise as a particular shape of hope, the kind that came from other people rather than from her own arithmetic, arriving in her hands as paper she wasn’t always sure what to do with, and kept anyway, every single time, because throwing it away had never once felt like the right response to someone trying.

* * *

She walked out past the same corkboard, the same curling flyers, into a grey Wednesday that hadn’t changed at all in the hour she’d spent inside, and stood on the sidewalk a long moment before she trusted her legs to carry her toward the SkyTrain.

The woman with the toddler was still there, out on the sidewalk now, crouched down refastening a small shoe that had come undone somewhere between the waiting room and the parking lot, and Nadia watched them a moment longer than she needed to, the same unbothered ordinariness she’d clocked earlier, a family whose worst morning still had two people in it standing on the same side of whatever came next. She didn’t begrudge them the ease of it. She just felt, standing there, the specific size of the thing she didn’t have, measured against someone else’s small unremarkable Wednesday, and found the measurement heavier than she’d expected it to be.

You’re quiet, Pip said, careful.

“I’m doing the arithmetic,” Nadia said. “Two weeks. A physician I can’t control, in a system I can’t argue with, looking at a body I can’t explain, and no one standing next to me whose job it is to push back if the answer comes out wrong.” She started walking, slow, the cut’s old exhaustion still sitting in her legs even though the cut itself no longer had a reason to exist. “Every other problem this year had a fix if I was clever enough to find it. Bhandari just told me, as gently as anyone’s ever told me anything, that this one doesn’t have a door built for someone in my position at all.”

The cut’s over now, Pip said, carefully, after a moment. If the deficit was really most of what was dragging the numbers down, two weeks of eating properly might move them further than you think before the appointment even happens.

“Maybe some of it,” Nadia said. “Not the resting heart rate. Not the healing. Not whatever a proper set of instruments reads off me that a coach’s basic panel never had the resolution to catch in the first place. I can fix the part the cut broke. I can’t fix the part that was never broken and was never going to read as ordinary no matter what I ate this week or any other week.” She turned the thought over once more, testing it for a crack she might have missed. “Two weeks of eating properly might actually make it worse, from where a Ministry physician’s sitting. A kid whose iron and haemoglobin bounce back that fast on a normal diet isn’t behaving like malnutrition behaves. It’s behaving like something else, and something else is exactly the category I can’t afford anyone official getting curious about.”

So getting better is also dangerous, Pip said, quiet.

“Everything’s dangerous,” Nadia said. “I’m just running out of directions where it’s dangerous in a way I actually know how to manage.”

What do you want to do, Pip asked.

“I don’t know yet,” Nadia said. “I know I have two weeks, and I know I’ve never once wasted two weeks doing nothing while a deadline closed in on me. I just don’t know yet what the first move actually is.”

She stopped at the corner before the SkyTrain entrance and pulled the slip of paper back out of her pocket, the Representative’s number in Bhandari’s careful handwriting, and dialled it standing there on the sidewalk rather than waiting until she’d had time to lose her nerve about it at home.

The recording that answered was pleasant, unhurried, entirely unbothered by the fact that someone on the other end had called it standing outside a Ministry office four minutes after the meeting that had made the call necessary. Thank you for contacting the Office of the Representative for Children and Youth. Our current response time for new intake calls is approximately six to eight weeks. If your matter is urgent, please contact your Ministry caseworker directly. A queue number followed, then a beep, and Nadia stood with the phone against her ear for a long moment before she left a short, careful message with her name and file number, mostly, she suspected, so that it would exist somewhere on record rather than because she believed it would move anything before the appointment Bhandari had already scheduled around her.

Six to eight weeks, Pip said, once she’d hung up. Against a two-week clock. I ran the numbers three different ways hoping one of them would come out kinder. None of them did.

“I know how to read two numbers next to each other,” Nadia said, and put the phone away, the queue number still sitting somewhere in a system that would eventually call her back long after this particular problem had already been decided one way or the other, filed and forgotten by everyone except her.

* * *

She kept walking toward the SkyTrain, fast enough now that the old cut-exhaustion in her legs had to work to keep up with her, and didn’t slow down again until the platform came into view.

I don’t have a fix for this one either, Pip said, quiet, once they were standing on the platform waiting for the next train. I want to say that plainly, because I don’t think pretending otherwise would help you right now. I’ve run every version of this I can think of, and every version ends the same place Bhandari already told you it ends. I don’t like sitting here with nothing useful to offer.

“You offered plenty,” Nadia said. “You told me the truth as soon as you had it, both times, the four days and the six weeks. That’s not nothing. I’d rather have an accurate bad number than a comforting wrong one. You know that about me by now.”

I do, Pip said. I still don’t like it.

Can I ask you something, Pip added, after a moment. Bhandari’s report had a line in it from Val. Did you notice it?

“I noticed,” Nadia said. “She wrote that she’s frightened for me. Not that she doubts me. I don’t think I’d have made it through that page at all if the line hadn’t been sitting there.”

I keep turning that over, Pip said. A person who was actually trying to hurt you wouldn’t have written that sentence. It doesn’t fit the shape of a betrayal, even though I know it felt like one walking out of that equipment room.

“I know it isn’t a betrayal,” Nadia said. “I never thought it was. That’s most of why it’s unbearable. If she were wrong about me on purpose, I could just be angry and let the anger do some of the work. She’s wrong about me for the kindest possible reason, and there’s no version of angry that fits around that shape cleanly.”

She watched the train’s lights appear at the far end of the tunnel, still a long way off, the platform’s cold air pushing ahead of it the way it always did before the train itself finally arrived, a small mechanical certainty in a day that had otherwise offered her almost none.

She thought, standing there, about the toddler’s small unfastened shoe, about a room built for families that had simply never accounted for someone standing in it alone, and about two weeks that were going to pass whether she found a plan to fill them or not. She had built plans out of less before. She didn’t yet know what this one was going to look like, only that she had until the appointment to find it, and that finding it, this time, might mean admitting there wasn’t a clean one waiting to be found at all.

* * *

The train pulled in, doors opening with the same flat mechanical chime they always used, and Nadia stepped on, found a seat near the back, and sat with her bag on her lap the way she’d sat in the waiting room an hour before, still, upright, running arithmetic that refused to resolve into anything she could act on yet.

Home? Pip asked, once the doors had closed and the train had started moving, its voice quieter now than it had been standing on the platform.

“Home,” Nadia said. “I need to sit with this somewhere that isn’t a government waiting room or a train car before I try to plan anything else today.”

That’s fair, Pip said. For what it’s worth, I’ll keep pulling on the legal advocate waitlist in the background. Even if it’s slow. I’d rather have a second option quietly building than none at all.

I’ll also start tracking what a normal, clean two weeks of eating and rest actually does to bloodwork like yours, it added. Not because I think it solves the appointment. Because if it doesn’t, I want us to know precisely how far short it fell, rather than guessing after the fact.

“Do that too,” Nadia said. “I want the actual numbers, whatever they turn out to be. Not the version I’m hoping for.”

She watched the tunnel lights slide past the window in their familiar rhythm, counting them without meaning to, the way she counted anything that gave her hands something to do while the rest of her worked on a problem that didn’t yet have an answer, all the way until the train surfaced again and the grey daylight came back in through the windows, unchanged, indifferent, exactly where she’d left it.
