Chapter 5: Category-0

Zero.

The letter had run out two days ago, and nothing had happened, which was its own kind of information. Ferreira had caught her in the hallway the morning it hit zero and said, without making anything formal of it, that the file was still open on her end, that reapplying took ninety days on paper but nobody was about to put a sixteen-year-old on the street over a number while the paperwork caught up to itself, and that Nadia should just keep doing what she was doing and let her know if anyone official ever actually said otherwise. It wasn’t a policy. It wasn’t written down anywhere Nadia had seen, and Ferreira had said it standing in the hallway with a coffee in one hand and a stack of other people’s files in the other, the way she said most things these days, as though the sentence had to be delivered somewhere between two other tasks or it wouldn’t get delivered at all. It was one tired woman deciding, quietly, not to enforce a rule that had never been built with anyone like Nadia in mind, and Nadia had said thanks and gone to class, and that had been the whole of it.

She checked the room-search tab that morning out of the same habit that made her check anything twice, and it had produced, for the first time in three weeks, a single new listing inside her price range with no age restriction attached at all, a basement suite in Renfrew with photographs too dark to make out much beyond a mattress and a window at ground level, which she’d messaged within a minute of seeing it and heard nothing back from since, a silence she recognised by now as its own kind of answer.

Saturday had happened and ended the way she’d expected it to, cake and a yard and voices she mostly let wash past her, Hana introducing her to four people whose names she’d retained for about as long as it took to be introduced to the next one, and by Sunday nobody had mentioned it again, which suited her. Priya had sent one photo afterward, blurry, half the frame taken up by someone’s shoulder, and Nadia had reacted to it with the single symbol that seemed to close that kind of message fastest and moved on.

The job today was out past the North Shore reservoir road, further than she usually went, a trail camera and a gate sensor on a property that backed onto watershed land, the kind of job that paid for the trip out as much as for the actual work.

She’d taken the SkyTrain and then a bus, and the bus was the part of the day that told her the most, the way a long transit ride always did if she paid attention to it. It ran once an hour out this far, a schedule posted on a laminated card that had gone illegible with sun and hadn’t been reprinted, and the driver seemed to know most of the dozen people who got on it, nodding at two of them by name without either party treating it as remarkable. The buildings thinned as the route climbed, a strip mall with two of its six units papered over giving way to a stretch of newer townhouses giving way, eventually, to nothing built at all, just trees on both sides of a road that had stopped bothering with sidewalks somewhere around the point where it stopped needing to serve anyone on foot.

She got off at the last stop that still counted as a stop, more a widened shoulder with a bench nobody used than any kind of terminal, and walked the last twenty minutes along a road with no sidewalk at all, gravel giving way to grass giving way to gravel again wherever a driveway crossed it. A hand-lettered sign nailed to a fence post advertised eggs, cash only, ring the bell, in a hand that had faded almost as much as the reservoir schedule had, and a dog somewhere behind a hedge announced her passing without ever showing itself.

Houses thinned out the further she went until the last few sat on lots big enough to have their own tree cover, mailboxes standing alone at the end of long gravel approaches, the city receding behind her one storey at a time until there was, by the time she reached the Henderson gate, no city left in view at all, only the green wall of the watershed lands rising up beyond the last row of fences.

* * *

The Hendersons had a gate sensor that had stopped registering vehicles reliably, leaving the gate either stuck open for hours or refusing to trigger at all, and a trail camera further back on the property that hadn’t sent an image in three weeks, mounted on a fence post at the edge of their land where it backed onto a strip of undeveloped watershed property behind a second, older fence that wasn’t theirs.

Mrs. Henderson met her at the gate itself, a woman in her fifties with mud on her boots and the specific brisk friendliness of somebody who had a great many things to do today and was glad this was one she could hand off entirely. She explained the sensor’s history at more length than the fault actually required, the gate sticking open twice last month and once nearly letting a delivery truck through onto the grass, and then admitted she had no real idea how any of it worked beyond which button reset it, which Nadia had heard some version of from almost every client she’d ever had, the specific relief of a person handing a problem to somebody who wasn’t going to ask them anything they couldn’t answer.

The gate sensor turned out to be moisture in the housing, a seal that had perished with age and let three seasons of rain work its way slowly into a connector that had never been rated for it, corrosion built up green and crusted around two of the pins, a fault she’d seen often enough on outdoor gear in this climate that she’d started keeping the right size of marine-grade seal in her bag specifically for it. She cleaned it, resealed it with dielectric grease, and had it triggering clean off a single test drive-through before she’d been on the property twenty minutes, Mrs. Henderson watching the gate swing with the satisfaction of somebody who’d expected this to take all afternoon.

The trail camera was further out, a walk along the property line to where the fence met a second fence, older, chain-link gone the particular grey that galvanised steel went after decades of weather, three strands of barbed wire along the top that had rusted enough in places to have lost most of their actual barb. Mrs. Henderson walked her out to it and left her to it, saying she had somewhere to be and Nadia could let herself back through the side gate when she was done, the kind of trust extended to a person because the alternative, standing over a stranger’s shoulder for twenty minutes in the cold, cost more than the trust did.

The camera’s battery compartment had corroded shut, the same slow water ingress as the gate sensor, a design that had never accounted for a Vancouver winter properly and had been failing on some fraction of every unit sold in this climate for exactly that reason. She worked the compartment open with a flathead and cleaned the contacts, and while she waited for the replacement cells to seat properly, looked, for the first time since she’d walked out here, at what was actually on the other side of the second fence.

* * *

It was a building, mostly below grade, a low concrete structure with maybe a metre and a half of it showing above the ground and the rest of it, by the shape of the access hatch and the ventilation stacks rising out of the grass around it, clearly running much further down than that. The concrete had gone the colour concrete went after fifty or sixty years of weather, streaked dark under every seam and every place water had ever run off it, moss thick in the low corners where the sun never quite reached, a fine crust of lichen spreading pale across the flattest section of roof where nothing had disturbed it in longer than she’d been alive, older than her by a wide enough margin that the comparison had stopped meaning much once she started making it.

Two ventilation stacks rose out of the grass a short distance apart, capped with the kind of mushroom vents that had been standard on municipal infrastructure for the better part of a century, both rusted through in patches, one of them missing its cap entirely and open straight down into whatever ran beneath the ground, a black circle in the grass that swallowed the afternoon light rather than reflecting any of it back.

She had grown up around enough municipal infrastructure to recognise the shape of it before she understood any of the specifics: a pumping station, built at a scale meant to move a genuinely large volume of water, from an era when a piece of infrastructure like this one had been built to be visited, maintained, staffed. There were the remains of a proper access road running up to it from somewhere off to the west, cracked now into separate plates of asphalt with grass growing thick through every seam, wide enough once for a service vehicle and unused long enough that nothing wider than a deer trail actually crossed it any more.

Layered on top of the original structure, and clearly much newer, was a second set of additions: a heavier door welded onto what had probably once been a simple maintenance hatch, the weld beads still visible along every seam where somebody had reinforced a frame that had never been designed to be locked against anything more determined than weather. Conduit ran along the outside of the wall rather than through it, in a way that told her whoever installed it hadn’t wanted to core through concrete that was already fifty years old and possibly load-bearing, clipped to the surface at intervals with brackets that had gone rust-orange but were still holding. A small weatherproofed panel sat bolted beside the door at chest height, the kind of housing that had once held a card reader or a keypad, empty now, its cover cracked and hanging from a single hinge, a faded logo above it that she didn’t recognise and a barcode strip gone nearly white with UV exposure, illegible even up close.

Somebody had come through here once, after the original builders were long finished with it, and repurposed the whole structure for something that had needed sealed access and its own power run, a second electrical service she could trace by the newer meter housing bolted to the wall a short distance from the door, its glass face clouded with age but the dial inside still visibly seized at zero, whatever had once drawn power through it disconnected or dead. A junction box sat below the meter with its cover gone entirely, wires capped off and taped rather than removed, the same lazy half-measure she’d seen inside a dozen buildings that had changed hands once too often for anyone left to fully understand what they’d inherited. They had done it the same way everyone did everything to old infrastructure in this city: bolted on top of what already existed rather than built fresh, because building fresh cost money nobody had ever budgeted and old concrete, whatever else was wrong with it, was still concrete.

Past that layer, closer to the door itself, were the marks of a third pass entirely, cruder than the second and much more recent by the sharpness of the edges: cut faces in the concrete where something had been removed rather than added, two of them, clean rectangular gouges with drill marks still visible around the perimeter of each, core-drilled rather than jackhammered, the unmistakable signature of somebody removing equipment that had been bolted in and worth the trouble of extracting intact rather than smashing free. Whoever had done it had known what they were doing and hadn’t cared what the wall looked like afterward, because the wall had never been the thing they wanted.

A spray-painted survey mark sat near the door at chest height, faded orange, a code and a string of digits she couldn’t read from this distance without the light on her phone, and beneath it a second mark in a different colour entirely, blue, half worn away, that looked to her eye like an earlier survey pass that the orange one had been painted directly over rather than beside, two separate visits by two separate crews who had never coordinated with each other closely enough to reuse the same colour. Lower down, an access ladder had been bolted onto the outside wall, clearly added specifically to reach the roof and the ventilation stacks rather than part of the building’s original design, the mounting brackets a different metal entirely from anything else on the structure, rungs rusted through in at least two places she could see from here, the kind of ladder installed by someone in a hurry on a job with a deadline, who had climbed it exactly as many times as the job required and never once since.

And over all three layers, holding the whole thing together, a fourth: a sign, newer than everything except the wire around it, mounted on a post that had been driven into the ground at an angle and never straightened, warning of restricted access in language that assumed anyone reading it already knew what it meant. The wire itself, a single strand strung between four posts around the perimeter she could see, had a sag to it in two places that suggested nobody had walked this line to check its tension in longer than the paint on the sign had been fresh.

She stood there for a while longer than the camera actually needed, the replacement cells long since seated, looking at the whole thing the way she looked at any board with four generations of repair stacked on top of each other, each layer telling her something about who’d been here and what they’d wanted and how much anyone had cared, in order, right up until the last person had stopped caring at all.

* * *

She packed her tools away slowly, in no particular hurry to get back to the Henderson gate, and let herself walk the fence line a little further than the job strictly required, the way she’d let herself do with any fault that hadn’t finished telling her its story yet.

The wire ran for perhaps two hundred metres along the boundary between the watershed land and the last of the private lots before it turned away into trees too thick to see through, four posts holding a single strand that had clearly been strung once and never revisited, insulators cracked on at least two of them, one post leaning at an angle that suggested something heavier than wind had hit it at some point and nobody had bothered straightening it back up. It was not the kind of fence built to keep a determined person out. It was the kind of fence built to satisfy a line item on a form, proof that a perimeter existed, filed and forgotten the same way the site itself had been.

She found the gap without particularly looking for it, a spot where the original chain-link had rusted through at the base and been folded back rather than repaired, wide enough for a person who wasn’t especially large to duck through without touching either edge, grass grown up tall enough on both sides that it had clearly gone unused by anyone in a long time, deer more likely than people if the droppings scattered near it were any indication.

She didn’t go through it. Not yet. She crouched at the edge of it instead, close enough to look through into the cleared area around the structure without crossing the line herself, cataloguing the ground on the other side the way she catalogued the inside of any device before she committed a tool to it: a service apron of cracked concrete immediately around the building, weeds forcing their way up through every seam of it, and beyond that, rougher ground, grass and low brush that had reclaimed whatever had once been kept clear for access.

The access ladder she’d clocked from a distance was closer now, close enough to see that it terminated at a hatch on the low roof section, a second point of entry entirely separate from the heavier welded door at ground level, and closer still she could make out a smaller detail she’d missed before: a section of the perimeter fence nearer the structure itself, a proper one this time, taller, with a gate in it, and the gate’s latch mechanism hanging loose from one screw, the kind of failure that happened to any outdoor hardware left unmaintained for years rather than any kind of forced entry.

Nobody had broken into this place. Nobody had needed to. It had simply been left, the way a house gets left when the people who owned it stop coming back, not vandalised, not stripped, just slowly given up by weather and time and the absence of anyone with a reason to check on it.

She thought about Szabo, briefly, standing at the gap in the fence with her tools still packed and the light starting to lean toward late afternoon, and about the flat way he’d delivered the whole warning, no more weight in it than he’d given the price of a connector. He’d been right that there wasn’t much salvage left worth the walk, if the two core-drilled holes in the concrete meant what she thought they meant, whoever harvested this place having taken exactly what was worth taking and left the rest. He hadn’t said anything about what the rest of it looked like, though, the shape of it, four different decades of human attention stacked on top of each other and then abandoned all at once, and she didn’t think he’d known, not really, only that the place existed and that nobody bothered with it any more, which was a different kind of knowing than actually standing here.

A crow called somewhere in the trees behind her, once, and didn’t call again, and the quiet that came back afterward had a quality to it she associated more with somewhere much further from a city than this technically was, the ordinary traffic-and-machinery hum of Vancouver reduced out here to something she could no longer consciously hear even though she knew, if she thought about it, that it was still there.

Mrs. Henderson was back at the house by the time she let herself through the side gate, standing in the driveway checking something on her own phone, and paid her in cash from a jar kept just inside the kitchen door for exactly this purpose, forty for the gate and twenty for the camera, more than Nadia would have quoted for either job on its own but about right for a callout this far out of the city. She asked whether the camera had caught anything interesting in its dead weeks, and Nadia said she hadn’t checked the footage, which was true, and that whatever showed up now onward would at least actually reach the app instead of failing silently, which was the part that had been broken. Mrs. Henderson thanked her twice, once with the cash and once again walking her back to the gate, and said, almost as an afterthought, that she hoped the walk back to the bus wasn’t too much, since it got dark early out here and there wasn’t much lit along that stretch of road. Nadia said she’d be fine, and meant it, since dark had never once been the part of a walk that worried her.

* * *

The survey mark by the door was close enough to read once she walked the fence line down to where it ran nearest the structure, a site code and a string of digits stencilled in paint gone thin enough that she had to angle her phone light across it to make out the last two characters and check them twice against what she thought she’d read the first time. She typed it into the Guild’s public registry, the same app half the province had installed and never opened more than once, the icon sitting somewhere on the second screen of her phone between a banking app she barely used and a bus schedule she used constantly, and got a result back in under a second, the connection out here weaker than in the city but still enough to load three lines of plain text.

A site classification. A status field reading depleted. A harvest date more than a decade old, before she’d have been old enough to remember the year even if anyone had told her about it at the time. And a category code she’d seen before somewhere, on a form or a notice she couldn’t place, without ever having a reason to know what it actually meant until this exact moment.

Category-0. She read the plain-language summary underneath it, three sentences written by somebody a long time ago in the flattest bureaucratic tone available: fully surveyed, primary resources extracted, residual threat assessed as negligible, no further monitoring scheduled. She scrolled for more and found none, no photographs, no follow-up notes, no revision history beyond a single timestamp from the harvest date itself, as though whoever had written the entry had closed it the same afternoon and nobody in the years since had ever opened it again. The kind of language that closed a file rather than describing a place, and she stood there with her phone light still on the fence, running the same three sentences twice the way she ran anything that told her something and nothing at the same time.

No further monitoring scheduled. She looked back at the sagging wire, the two rusted-through rungs on the ladder, the moss thick in every seam the sun didn’t reach, the meter dial seized at zero, the loose latch on the inner gate. Four completely different eras of attention, and every one of them long since finished, and she understood, in the same flat way she understood most facts about how systems actually worked once you looked closely enough at them, that the sentence on her phone and the structure in front of her were describing the same place from two completely different distances, one of them decades out of date and the other one standing right in front of her, breathing, in whatever sense a sealed concrete box could be said to breathe, and that nobody currently employed by anyone had any reason to notice the gap between them.

The cold reached her before she worked out where it was coming from, a drop sharp enough against the mild afternoon that she checked her own arms for gooseflesh before she checked anything else, the fine hair on them standing up in a way the temperature alone didn’t fully account for. Air pooled somewhere near the base of the structure the way cold air pools in a basement with the door left open, except there was no door open, not that she could see, nothing to explain a temperature drop that specific arriving from a sealed concrete box with a locked hatch and a fence around it. She checked the open ventilation stack, the one missing its cap, and felt nothing coming off it at all, no draft, no smell, nothing moving through the black circle in the grass except whatever she was imagining into it.

She stood very still for a moment, cataloguing it the only way she knew how to catalogue anything: not frightened, not yet, only noting that a fact had just presented itself that did not fit anywhere in the model she already had, and that a fact like that was worth more than most of what she’d find out here on purpose. She had spent six years learning that the things which didn’t fit the model were the only things ever worth a second look, every real fault she’d ever chased down having started life as exactly this, a reading that made no sense next to everything around it.

* * *

Mrs. Henderson’s gate was a fifteen-minute walk back, and Nadia made it in ten, glancing back twice at the low concrete shape sitting behind its sagging wire before the trees closed over it and it was gone.

She caught the once-an-hour bus with four minutes to spare and rode it back down through the same strip mall and the same thinning trees in reverse, the city rebuilding itself around her one storey at a time, and by the time the SkyTrain pulled into the station nearest Kelso House the whole afternoon had the slightly unreal quality of something that had happened a long way from anywhere she actually lived.

She didn’t say anything about it to Ferreira, who was gone for the day by the time she got back, or write it anywhere she’d normally write a job’s details down. She sat at the bench instead with her jacket still on, pulled up the registry entry a second time on her phone just to confirm the three sentences hadn’t changed in the ninety minutes since she’d last read them, and started, without deciding to in any way she could have pointed to afterward, making a list on the back of an old invoice: a headlamp, spare batteries, the multitool rather than the single flathead, something to mark her own trail with in case the phone lost signal out there the way it clearly did.

She looked at the list for a while once she’d finished it, four items in her own small careful print, and didn’t cross a single one off, and didn’t add a reason next to any of them either, because she didn’t have one yet that would have made sense written down.

The honest answer, if anyone had asked her directly, would have been closer to a question than a reason: what a depleted, unmonitored site with a cold spot at its base and two drill holes where its valuables used to sit actually looked like from the inside, and whether the three flat sentences on her phone were describing the whole truth of it or only the part somebody had bothered to write down before they stopped looking. It was the same question that had made her open the housing on the induction ring in the Kelso House kitchen, the same one that had put her on her knees in the rain outside a courier’s basement suite. A thing existed that she didn’t yet understand, close enough to reach, and the not understanding it sat in her the way an unsolved fault always did, present at the edge of everything else she did until she either solved it or ran out of reasons to keep trying.

She put the invoice with the list on it into the drawer under the bench, next to the cash tin, and turned to the board she’d left half finished two nights running now, and worked on it until the light outside the window had gone all the way to dark, the four items on the list sitting closed in the drawer the whole time, waiting on nothing but a day with enough hours in it to use them.

Somewhere under the working of the board, in the part of her attention that ran underneath whatever she was actually doing, she was already sorting through her week, the placement she still didn’t have, the wrestling schedule Val had posted for the coming month, the two jobs already booked for tomorrow, looking for the shape of a day that had a few clear hours sitting somewhere in the middle of it, out past the reservoir, with enough light left afterward to see her way back to the bus.

She hadn’t found one yet by the time she went to bed, but she hadn’t stopped looking either, and the list stayed in the drawer, four items deep, waiting.
