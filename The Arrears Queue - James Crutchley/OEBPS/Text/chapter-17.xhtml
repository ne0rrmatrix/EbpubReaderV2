Chapter 17: Ballast

The notification arrived at the worst possible moment, which was, she was beginning to notice, the only moment it ever seemed to choose.

The session had already run longer than she’d planned for, the Elite grinding through its slow scavenging circuit two catwalks over, close enough that she’d spent the last twenty minutes tracking its pace against her own instead of working the drills she’d actually come down here to run. She’d caught three clean hits in the last exchange, none of them flat, all of them redirected along the angle Val had spent a season correcting, forty points apiece into a queue that still had room in it, and she’d let herself feel, for a moment, the specific satisfaction of a technique finally living in her hands instead of in her head where she had to think it through first.

The fourth hit hadn’t redirected as cleanly. She’d felt it land wrong, a fraction of a degree off the angle, more of the force going straight through rather than around, and had braced for the number the meter would show her, three hundred instead of forty, the flat-contact math she’d hated since the first time she’d learned it. She’d taken the hit, held her footing, and kept moving, because the session wasn’t over and a bad number wasn’t a reason to stop, only a reason to be more careful about the next one.

[LEVEL UP: 2]

She read it upside down and blurred, from a position on the concrete she hadn’t chosen so much as arrived at, both knees drawn up, the Arrears queue draining into her active bar in the slow, unhurried way it always drained, no faster for her wanting it to be. Six weeks of sessions sat behind that single bracket. Six weeks of catwalk hits absorbed at the angle Val had spent a season correcting and still hadn’t fully corrected, six weeks of a Window she kept opening and never once got close to using in full, six weeks of an experience total that had crawled up in increments too small to notice individually and had apparently, tonight, crossed a line she hadn’t seen coming even though she’d been the one pushing toward it the whole time.

It did not feel like an achievement. It felt like a bill arriving for a purchase she barely remembered making.

Her whole body ached in the specific, deep, structural way it only ached after something had genuinely changed inside her rather than merely been used hard, a soreness with no clean origin point, nothing she could trace back to a single hit or a single hour, only a general sense that something under her skin had spent the last six weeks quietly rebuilding itself into a slightly different shape without asking her opinion on the matter.

Congratulations, Pip said, arriving fully present, underground, the way it always arrived fully present underground, its voice carrying none of the surface delay she’d learned to expect and work around aboveground. I want to say that properly, because I don’t think either of us has said it properly for a level before. That’s two. That took you longer than the first one did, and it cost you more, and you earned every part of it the slow way.

“Doesn’t feel like it,” Nadia said, pushing herself up off the concrete with an arm that shook more than it should have for the weight it was moving. “Feels like it cost me and I don’t get to see the bill until later.”

That may be the most accurate description of levelling anyone has ever given me, Pip said. For what it’s worth, the number underneath it is real. Max HP went up. Not by a great deal, but by enough that the arithmetic changes.

“Show me.”

Rough estimate, not exact, because I don’t have your full Arrears cap logged precisely: your theoretical Window just got longer. Not the ninety seconds themselves, that clock never moves, but how much of it you could survive using if you had the mitigation efficiency to fill it. Call it four, maybe five seconds more before the queue would kill you outright, assuming a flat, unmanaged hit rate you’d never actually let happen.

Nadia did the arithmetic herself anyway, the way she always did the arithmetic herself even when Pip had already done it, because a number she’d checked personally was a number she trusted, and a number she’d only been told was, at best, a number she was borrowing.

Four or five seconds. Against a Window that still closed at ninety and a session that still ended, every time, somewhere in the low twenties, because her mitigation efficiency hadn’t moved even slightly, because a longer theoretical ceiling didn’t translate into anything she could actually spend down on the floor of the pit tonight. It was, she thought, climbing back toward the catwalk rail for the last few minutes of a session she didn’t have the energy left to properly use, the same shape as every other number the site had ever handed her. A resource she technically possessed and could not yet touch.

You’re thinking about the ceiling again, Pip said. You always go quiet in a specific way when you’re thinking about the ceiling.

“It’s a good ceiling,” Nadia said. “I just can’t reach it. I don’t know why that’s supposed to feel like winning.”

I don’t think it’s supposed to feel like winning tonight, Pip said. I think it’s supposed to feel like winning eventually, and tonight is just the part where the number moves and you haven’t caught up to it yet. That’s not nothing. That’s just not finished.

She sat with that for a moment, boots dangling off the catwalk edge, the Elite’s slow grinding circuit still audible two levels down, patient and indifferent to whatever had just happened to her. It occurred to her that the machine down there had never once celebrated a level. It simply scavenged, folded what it found into itself, and kept moving, and she wondered, not for the first time, whether that indifference was closer to how she actually felt about her own progress than any of the language the System offered her for it.

You’re doing the thing where you compare yourself to it again, Pip said, and there was something in its voice that might have been amusement, if amusement were a thing it fully had access to underground where its attention ran wide rather than sharp. I’ve noticed you do that more when something changes and you don’t know what to do with the feeling.

“It doesn’t stop to think about whether it earned anything,” Nadia said. “It just keeps going. That seems easier.”

It also doesn’t get to stop, Pip said. I don’t think you’d actually want that trade, if it were offered to you honestly.

“No,” Nadia admitted, after a moment, watching the machine’s slow circuit two levels down. “Probably not. Not even close.”

I didn’t think so, Pip said, something almost gentle in it. For what it’s worth, I’d rather have you tired and levelling than tireless and stuck the way it is. I’ve watched it do the same circuit for longer than I’ve been able to properly count. There’s nothing underneath that I’d want for you.

She didn’t answer, because she wasn’t sure, and pushed herself up off the rail instead, testing the ache in her legs against the climb still ahead of her, a soreness that sat differently tonight than it usually did, deeper, less like an hour’s work and more like something that had reorganised itself under her skin while she wasn’t paying attention.

She didn’t have an answer for that one that felt honest either, so she didn’t give one, and climbed the rest of the way back to the entry point in silence, the queue still draining behind her at its own unhurried pace, and rode the scooter home through a drizzle that had started sometime while she was underground and hadn’t bothered announcing itself.

* * *

The wobble started somewhere around the third kilometre, small enough at first that she assumed the road, a seam in the asphalt she hadn’t clocked, a patch of standing water pulling at something. She adjusted her weight the way she always adjusted for a rough patch of road, shifting forward over the deck, and the scooter corrected, mostly, and she filed the whole thing under nothing and kept riding.

It came back on the next straight stretch, worse this time, a lean toward the left she had to actively fight rather than simply lean into, the kind of drift that had no business existing on a machine with vectored thrust and no drivetrain to develop play in, a machine that did not corner the way a wheeled vehicle cornered and therefore had no earthly reason to be pulling like one now.

She pulled over under the one working streetlight on that stretch of road and crouched beside the frame, running her hands along the deck the way she’d run them a hundred times before, checking mounting points, checking the truing of the front wheel out of an old habit from a machine that no longer had a motor to blame for anything, checking the rear axle where it had never once, in months of riding, given her a single problem.

Everything read correct. The wheel spun true when she lifted it and gave it a spin, no wobble in the rim, no play in the bearing she could feel with a thumb pressed against it. She checked the deck bolts next, each one seated exactly where she’d left it the last time she’d gone over the whole machine, and ran a hand along the underside of the frame feeling for a hairline crack in the weld, the kind of fault that could throw a machine’s balance without ever showing itself to the eye, and found nothing there either, the metal cold and smooth and entirely unremarkable under her fingers.

She thought, crouched there in the wet with the rain finding its way under her collar, about every ride she’d taken on this machine going back to the night it had first done the thing it wasn’t supposed to do, the balance point she’d learned so completely she no longer thought about it, the exact lean angle that kept the deck level under her at speed, a piece of knowledge so deeply built into her hands by now that she could have described the machine’s handling with her eyes closed. Tonight’s ride hadn’t matched that description. Nothing about the machine had explained why.

She sat back on her heels in the wet and looked at the machine the way she looked at any fault that refused to present a cause, cataloguing what she knew. The scooter had shifted. The scooter had never shifted before, not once, not across thousands of kilometres of exactly this kind of riding, in exactly this kind of weather, at exactly this kind of speed.

Which meant, by the only logic she had ever trusted, that the fault wasn’t in the scooter at all.

She stood back up, straddled the deck again, and rode the rest of the way home paying close attention to her own weight the way she’d never had to before, correcting for a lean that came from somewhere above the deck rather than below it, a shift in her own centre of mass she hadn’t asked for and hadn’t noticed happening, the machine behaving exactly as it always had, responding honestly to a rider who had quietly become a slightly different rider without telling anyone, including herself.

You went very still again, Pip observed, once they were back inside the tunnel and it was fully present to notice. Same as the hallway, Tuesday. I’m starting to recognise the shape of it.

“I think I need to true the front wheel again when I get home,” Nadia said, which was not an answer to the thing Pip had actually said, and they both seemed to know it.

You could, Pip said, careful. I’d let you, if you wanted to spend an hour confirming the wheel is fine, which I already think it is. Or you could consider the possibility that the machine isn’t the part of this that changed.

She didn’t answer that either, not out loud, and rode the rest of the way home in a silence that had nothing restful in it at all, turning the machine into the driveway with a care she hadn’t needed a week ago, and stood for a long moment afterward with both hands still on the grips, feeling the whole familiar weight of the scooter under her palms and finding, for the first time since she’d built it, that she couldn’t entirely trust her own read of what it was telling her.

* * *

She trued the wheel anyway, that night, at the bench, because a diagnosis she hadn’t confirmed wasn’t a diagnosis, only a guess dressed up as one, and she had learned a long time ago not to trust a guess just because it was the one she wanted.

The wheel came off, spun clean on the truing stand, showed no runout worth the name, went back on exactly as it had come off. She checked the headset next, the stem bolts, the deck mounts, every point of the frame where play could conceivably hide, working through the machine methodically the way she worked through any board with a fault she couldn’t yet locate, and found nothing, at every step, that told her anything except that the machine was correct.

I think you already know what the wheel is going to tell you, Pip said, quiet, watching from the bench light’s edge.

“I know what it’s not going to tell me,” Nadia said, setting the wrench down. “That’s different. That’s just ruling things out.”

Is it, Pip said, not quite a question, and she didn’t answer that one either, and went to bed with the scooter reassembled and correct and no closer to an explanation than she’d been standing under the streetlight an hour earlier, turning over, in the dark, the specific discomfort of a fault she couldn’t locate anywhere outside herself.

* * *

Weigh-in Thursday told her the rest of it, plainly, in the flat unarguable language a scale had always used with her.

She stepped onto the wrestling room scale, the same scale she’d rebuilt herself weeks ago, calibrated it with her own hands, trusted the way she trusted very little else in the building, and watched the number settle somewhere she had not expected it to settle, half a kilo over where six days of careful eating and a full cut cycle should have put her.

She stepped off, reset it, stepped back on. Same number, to the tenth of a kilo, the readout as stable and unbothered as it had been every other time she’d tested it. She thought, standing there in the cold fluorescent light of the room before practice properly started, about every possible mechanical explanation available to her, the platform, the load cells, a battery drifting low, a calibration that had somehow slipped since she’d set it, and ran through each of them the way she’d run through the scooter’s frame the night before, methodically, refusing to let the first plausible answer stand in for a confirmed one.

She pulled the back panel off the scale right there on the equipment room floor, the same four screws she’d removed to rebuild it weeks earlier, and checked the load cells one at a time against the reference weight she still had in her bag from that repair, a habit she hadn’t broken even after the scale had passed every test she’d thrown at it. Each cell read within the tolerance she’d set them to. The battery held a full charge. The calibration hadn’t drifted a single gram from where she’d left it.

She sat back on her heels, the panel still off, and made herself say the next part honestly, the way she made herself say anything she didn’t want to be true. If the platform was correct and the load cells were correct and the calibration was correct, then the number the scale had given her wasn’t a fault in the scale at all. It was a measurement, taken correctly, of something that had actually changed.

She thought about the week just behind her, logged carefully in the notebook she kept in her bag for exactly this purpose, every meal weighed, every session accounted for, a deficit she’d run cleanly and confirmed twice against her own math before trusting it. There was no error in that ledger either. She’d checked it the way she checked everything, more than once, because a ledger she hadn’t double checked wasn’t a ledger she was willing to build a conclusion on top of.

Two clean instruments and one clean ledger, and none of them agreed with each other, and somewhere underneath all three sat a single stubborn number that refused to explain itself no matter which piece of the system she interrogated.

Coach Val kept a second scale in the equipment closet, an older mechanical one nobody used any more because the digital scale had replaced it two seasons back, and she dug it out before anyone else arrived for the session, dusted it off, set it level on the same patch of floor, and stepped on.

The needle swung and settled at the same number, near enough that the tenth of a kilo of difference was well inside what two different scales, one digital and one mechanical, should be expected to disagree by.

She stood in the equipment closet for a long moment with both scales in front of her, agreeing with each other in a way that left her nothing left to blame, and felt the specific, cold arrival of a conclusion she had been quietly avoiding since the streetlight two nights earlier.

It wasn’t the scooter. It had never been the scooter.

Two instruments, Pip said, gently, present now in the daylight the way it wasn’t always present in the daylight, as though it had judged this particular moment worth the effort of full attention. Independent of each other, calibrated separately, agreeing to within their own margin of error. I know how that reads to you. I want to say it before you have to.

She stood there a moment longer, holding both possibilities open the way she held any unresolved fault open before committing to a repair, the way she’d learned, from years of boards that lied about themselves right up until the last test, not to trust her first read of anything just because it was the read she wanted. But two instruments built on entirely different mechanisms, one digital and one nothing but a spring and a needle, had never once agreed with each other by accident in her experience. Agreement like that meant something real sat underneath both readings, whether or not she had a name for it yet.

“I gained half a kilo of something that isn’t fat and isn’t water,” Nadia said, quiet, mostly to herself. “In a week where I ran a deficit the whole time. That doesn’t happen. There’s no version of the math where that happens.”

There’s no version of the math you’ve been taught where that happens, Pip said. I don’t think that’s the same statement.

She thought about mass without volume, a phrase she didn’t actually have, only the shape of the idea it would have named if anyone had ever bothered to document it for her, density climbing somewhere under a level bracket nobody had warned her carried a physical cost measured in kilograms rather than experience points. She thought about the scooter’s honest, unbothered response to a rider whose centre of mass had shifted without permission. She thought about six weeks of sessions, each one costing her more than the last in ways she’d filed under ordinary fatigue because ordinary fatigue was a category she already knew how to survive.

She thought, too, about what the number actually threatened, laid out plainly the way she laid out any problem once she’d stopped avoiding it. Half a kilo wasn’t nothing on a 52 kilogram frame. It was most of a percentage point she didn’t have room to spare, stacked on top of a cut that was already the tightest she’d run all season, and if the pattern held, if every level from here forward cost her another half kilo of lean mass she couldn’t train off and couldn’t starve off because it wasn’t fat and wasn’t water to begin with, then the collision waiting for her at the end of that arithmetic wasn’t abstract at all. It was a weight class. It was a category she might not be able to make, however hard she worked, however carefully she logged every gram that went into her mouth.

She had no one to say any of that to. Val would read it as a coaching problem, adjust her training load, tell her to manage her cut more carefully, none of which would touch the actual mechanism sitting underneath it. A doctor would read it as a health concern, if she had a guardian who could sign her into one, and would find nothing on any chart built for an ordinary sixteen-year-old body anyway. The notebook in her bag was, as far as she could tell, the only place in the world any of this could honestly go, and even the notebook didn’t have an answer, only a growing list of readings that agreed with each other and refused, collectively, to explain themselves.

She put both scales back exactly where she’d found them, the mechanical one dusted and returned to its shelf, the digital one reset to zero, and walked out to the mat for a practice she was going to have to work considerably harder to make weight for than she had six weeks ago, carrying a half kilo of herself she had no instrument fine enough to argue with and no explanation she was willing to say out loud to anyone in the building.

Val ran them through takedowns for the first half hour, the same drill they’d run a dozen times before, and pulled her aside partway through with the particular economy of a coach who’d already decided what he wanted to say before he said it.

“You’re carrying your weight differently today,” he said, watching her reset her stance rather than looking at her directly, the way he did most of his real observations. “Lower. More through the hips. It’s not wrong. It’s just not how you were standing last week.”

“I feel the same,” Nadia said, which was true in every way that mattered to the conversation she was willing to have with him.

“Probably nothing,” Val said, in the tone of a man filing an observation rather than closing a case. “Bodies shift through a season. Just flagging it so you watch it yourself before I have to.”

He moved on to the next pair before she had to say anything further, and she ran the rest of the drill with a closer attention to her own stance than she’d ever needed before, feeling for the first time a weight settling through her hips and legs that hadn’t been there a month ago, distributed in a way that made her harder to shift off balance and, she suspected, would make the last half kilo of the cut considerably harder to find. Her partner, working the same drill across from her, commented once, lightly, that she felt like she’d been eating her spinach, and laughed at her own joke, and Nadia managed something close to a smile back and said nothing else, filing that comment away too, next to Val’s, another small unprompted data point from someone with no reason to be looking for one. She filed Val’s comment next to the scale reading and the scooter’s wobble, three separate instruments now, none of them mechanical in any sense she could repair, all of them pointing at the same unwelcome conclusion.

He noticed in less than an hour of drilling, Pip said, quiet, once they’d moved on to live rounds and the room’s noise gave her enough cover to think without anyone watching her think. The people who watch bodies for a living notice fast, and they notice something is different even when they can’t say what.

“He thinks it’s a season thing,” Nadia said, low, circling an opponent half a head taller than her. “Bodies shift. He wasn’t wrong to think that. It just isn’t what’s actually happening.”

No, Pip agreed. It isn’t. But he’s not wrong that something is different. He’s only wrong about which season is causing it.

* * *

The cafeteria, an hour later, told her the last part of it before she’d consciously worked out she was hungry for anything specific.

She stood in line with her tray already half decided before she reached the hot counter, the craving arriving the way it always arrived lately, ahead of thought, ahead of any deliberate choice on her part, a pull toward the darkest cuts of meat on offer and the tray of sautéed spinach nobody else at the table ever touched, a hunger with a shape to it rather than a size, specific in a way plain exhaustion never was.

She built a plate around it without questioning the instinct, the way she’d stopped questioning most of these instincts months ago, and ate through the first several minutes of lunch with an intensity she usually reserved for the days after a session that had gone badly, a hard flat hit taken somewhere the arrears queue would remember for a while, mechanical damage that always, without fail, called for exactly this.

It was sharper than she remembered it being. Sharper than the version of this same craving she’d catalogued back in October, back when she’d first started noticing the pattern and started, quietly, keeping track. She ate through it methodically, the tray emptying faster than the hour strictly required, and sat afterward with the specific fullness of a body that had gotten something it had been asking for loudly and would, she suspected, be asking for again by dinner.

That was more than usual, Pip observed, quiet under the cafeteria noise, present enough at this hour to notice, not present enough to sound entirely sure of itself. I’ve been tracking it since October, roughly. Today’s the sharpest version I’ve logged.

“I know,” Nadia said, low, mouth half full, the tray in front of her nearly cleared in a time that would have alarmed her a year ago and now barely registered as unusual at all. “I felt it in the line before I even got to the counter.”

Do you want to know what I think that means, Pip asked, or would you rather I just keep logging it.

She considered the question honestly, the fork still in her hand, the last of the spinach disappearing without her fully deciding to eat it.

“Keep logging it,” she said. “I already know what it means. I just don’t have anywhere to put it yet.”

Pip didn’t answer that, which she understood, by now, as its own kind of agreement, and she finished the tray in silence, the noise of the cafeteria rolling on around her the way it always did, indifferent to the fact that she’d just quietly confirmed, across three separate instruments in a single morning, that something in her was changing faster than she had any framework for managing.

Math class, an hour later, asked nothing of her that the morning hadn’t already exceeded by a wide margin. She sat near the back, notebook open to a page of equations she was technically capable of solving and found herself reading twice before the numbers settled into anything meaningful, her attention snagging instead on the small, persistent hum of her own body, the ache in her hips from the drill Val had flagged, the specific fullness left over from a lunch that had gone further than usual toward actually satisfying her, the low residual awareness of a level bracket sitting somewhere under her skin that she had no way of examining directly.

The teacher called on her once, a question about a system of equations she’d already half solved without meaning to, purely out of the habit of working a problem the moment it was in front of her, and she gave the answer correctly and without hesitation, and the teacher moved on, satisfied, and Nadia went back to the margin of her notebook where she’d started, without quite deciding to, a short list of her own.

Scooter, she’d written. Scale x2. Val. Cafeteria. Four separate readings, four separate instruments, and beneath them, in smaller letters, a line she looked at for a long moment before closing the notebook on it: no explanation available.

It sat, she thought, uncomfortably close to the refrain she’d been logging in the other notebook for weeks now, the field meter’s flat, unhelpful verdict returned against every reading she’d taken since October. No result. This was the opposite problem, in a way that didn’t comfort her nearly as much as it should have. Too much result, and nowhere at all to put it.

She closed the notebook properly then, slid it into her bag, and let the rest of the class pass in the ordinary grey blur of an afternoon that had already, by ten in the morning, told her more about her own body than she had any real capacity left to process before the bell rang and sent her back out into a day that expected her to keep functioning exactly as though nothing about any of it had changed at all.
