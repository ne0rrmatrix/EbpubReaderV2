Chapter 1: Eleven Days

The rain had come three weeks early and Kelso House had not adjusted to it. The radiators were off until October, the second-floor hallway smelled of wet carpet, and somebody had propped the fire door open again to move air through the building.

Nadia worked with the window cracked and her hood up. Through the gap she could see the parking lot behind the building, three cars and a dumpster with its lid propped open by something wedged underneath, and beyond that the back of the block on the next street, brick going dark with the rain the way brick did, a shade at a time.

The room was two metres by three, with a single bed she had pushed against the wall to make room for the bench, and the bench was a door laid flat across two filing cabinets she had found in the alley behind the building and carried up one flight at a time. Everything on it had a place. The hot-air station sat at the back, out of the draft from the window, because hot air and a draft did not agree with each other and she had learned that the hard way in June, on a board that still had a faint brown ghost across two pads where she had cooked it. The totes were labelled in her own handwriting, by package size and not by function, because package size was the thing that mattered when a part failed at eleven at night and she needed to know in four seconds whether she had a donor for it.

The board in front of her was a mobility controller out of a chair belonging to a woman four blocks east, who had been quoted eleven hundred for a replacement and had said no. The fault was a swollen tantalum on the five-volt rail. It had been swollen a long time. There was a brown crust at one end of the casing where it had been venting slowly for months, and the board underneath had taken the worst of it: a stain, one lifted trace, a via she would have to jumper.

She had already traced the rail with the multimeter, in diode mode, working outward from the connector until the reading went wrong, which was faster than looking and more honest than guessing. It told her what it told her. The cap was the cap.

She brought the hot air up to three-fifty and had the cap off in under a minute.

The replacement came out of a dead washing-machine controller she had stripped in June. Same package, same rating, forty years newer than the chair. She tinned the pads, set it, checked it under the loupe, and ran the jumper in thirty-gauge, and the whole repair cost her nothing but solder.

It came up on the bench supply at four hundred and eighty milliamps and held there.

She wrote the current on the back of the voucher letter, because the voucher letter was the paper nearest her hand, and then she turned it over and looked at the front of it again.

Eleven days.

The letter had been printed in a typeface that had been chosen by somebody in about 2090 and never revisited. It thanked her for her cooperation. It said the transitional accommodation period was thirty days from the date of transition and gave the date of transition, which was a Tuesday in August that she remembered mostly as hot. It gave a number to call. She had called the number twice. The first time it had told her the expected wait was fifty minutes and the second time it had told her nothing at all, just silence on the line for longer than any silence had a reason to run, until it disconnected on its own.

She put the letter under the bench light where she would see it, because that was what it was for, and packed the board into a takeout container with a paper towel folded under it so it wouldn’t slide against the sides on the walk over.

The stairwell light on the second-floor landing had been out since before she arrived. Somebody had reported it. There was a work order, and the work order had a number, and Ferreira had shown her the number once with the expression of a person showing you a photograph of a relative who had died a long time ago.

* * *

Delacourt’s building had a ramp that was too steep and a buzzer that worked, which put it ahead of most. The elevator had a sign taped over the call button that said out of service and a second, older sign underneath it, half peeled, that said the same thing in a different font, which meant it had been out of service long enough to have been re-announced at least once.

The chair was in the front room with its side panel off and a tea towel over the exposed connector, which was not the worst thing Nadia had seen somebody do and was better than most. There was a smell of something cooking two doors down that had nothing to do with Delacourt, and a television on low in the next room that nobody was watching. Nadia knelt on the carpet, seated the board, ran the harness, and had the panel back on in about four minutes.

Delacourt drove it up the hall and back twice. On the second pass she took the corner at the doorway without the hesitation she had been doing before, which meant the encoder was reading clean and the deadband she’d set was tight enough to trust.

Sixty in cash, in tens, out of a tin that lived on top of the microwave.

Delacourt asked whether she ought to worry about the other one, the one on the left side, because it looked like the first one had looked.

Nadia said no. That one was fine. It only looked bad.

It did look bad. The casing was discoloured across the top and there was a rime of something white at the base of it that would have made a lot of people nervous. But the discolouration was old and even and dry, and there was no crust at the vent, and it had been sitting at exactly the right voltage on the bench when she checked it with the chair unplugged and the panel open, twenty minutes she hadn’t billed for. It would outlast the chair.

Delacourt said she’d feel better if it was done anyway.

Nadia said that would be forty dollars for a part that did not need replacing, and Delacourt laughed, which Nadia did not understand, because it had not been a joke, it had been the price. She picked up her bag and said she would come back if the left side ever started actually failing, and that she would be able to tell, and Delacourt said all right, and thanked her twice, once at the start and once at the door, which some people did and Nadia had never worked out why the second one felt necessary to them.

On the step outside she checked the tin’s worth of tens against the number in her head. Sixty. That was correct. The rain had gone from something you could walk through to something you had to commit to, and she pulled her hood back up and committed to it.

* * *

The Safeway at Commercial and Broadway had the doors open despite the rain, and the air inside the entrance was the specific cold of a produce section running too hard, the kind that made the sweat under her jacket go from something she’d stopped noticing to something she noticed all over again.

She took a four-litre of milk. Two dozen eggs, because the two dozen worked out cheaper per egg than the dozen and she would use them before they turned. A two-kilo bag of rice, the store brand, not the name brand, which tasted the same once it was cooked and cost four dollars less. Four tins of sardines, because they kept and because thirty grams of protein for a dollar-forty was a number she had run before and it still held. The chicken thighs on the far end of the case with the yellow sticker, marked down because tomorrow was the date, eight of them in the tray, more than she needed and cheap enough that it didn’t matter.

Half the shelf where the good tinned tomatoes used to be was the store’s own brand now, in a can that dented if you looked at it. She noticed this the way she noticed everything that had quietly gotten worse without anyone deciding to make it worse, filed it, and kept moving.

Twenty-six eighty at the till.

The stipend card declined.

She turned it over, checked the chip against the light, and tried it again. Declined. There was no message on the terminal, no code, nothing to trace. There had been a message the first time this happened, two weeks ago, and she had spent an afternoon on the phone establishing that the message did not correspond to anything and that nobody at the number could tell her why.

The girl at the till had started saying something about the machine doing that sometimes, so Nadia said it was fine and paid the twenty-six eighty out of the sixty, counting it out in tens and a five and change, and did the subtraction in her head before the girl had finished counting it back to her.

The girl bagged the milk separately, which was correct, and said something else that Nadia did not catch over the compressor behind the deli case. She said thanks and took the bags, one in each hand, weight matched.

Thirty-three twenty.

Outside, under the overhang, with the rain coming off the edge of the awning in a line she could have set a level to, she did the rest of it. Eleven days. Thirty-three twenty in her pocket and eighty-one in the account that worked, which was the account with the repair money in it, which was not really an account so much as a prepaid card she had bought at a pharmacy with cash, because opening a real one had wanted a guardian’s signature on a form that did not have a box for her situation. The chicken would go in the freezer compartment that iced over if she didn’t watch it, so two days on that before it had to be cooked. The rice was two weeks if she was careful with it and four days if she wasn’t, which came down to how many nights she let herself use it as the whole meal instead of a third of one.

Nine hundred a month for a room, if a room existed, which it did not, not at a price that had ever once appeared on a listing she could actually take.

She adjusted the bags so the weight sat even on both sides and started walking, keeping to the side of the street where the awnings still had most of their length.

* * *

Back in the room she set the groceries on the end of the bench, farthest from the hot-air station, and opened the parts board to look for a donor controller before she’d even taken her jacket off.

The Integration notice sat at the top of the feed, where it had sat every day of her life.

[INTEGRATION NOTICE: STANDING]

Conditions established. Parameters open.

Interval: preliminary.

She scrolled past it and filtered the listings by postal code.

Mostly nothing. A gutted inverter in Burnaby, stripped of everything worth having before it was ever listed. Three laptop boards with liquid damage in the same corner, which meant one seller and a story, the same coffee cup knocked over three separate times or one photograph reused three separate times, she couldn’t tell which and it didn’t matter, she wasn’t buying any of them. A box of assorted in Richmond, forty dollars, which would be forty dollars of nothing, connectors with no boards attached and boards with the wrong connectors, the kind of lot that only ever sold to somebody who hadn’t opened one before.

Second page: a controller board, right family, wrong revision, eight dollars, and the photograph was in focus, taken flat-on under real light instead of a phone flash at an angle. Somebody who photographs a board in focus has at least looked at it, which was not nothing.

She sent the message and got a reply inside two minutes, which was fast for a listing that price, and arranged to pick it up Thursday from an address in Grandview she’d have to take two buses to reach.

Then, because the tab was already open and her hands hadn’t stopped moving, she looked at rooms.

There were four hundred and six listings inside the transit zone she could reach in under an hour each way. She sorted by price. The bottom of the list was six hundred a month for a shared bed in a room with three other beds in it, which required a co-signer, which required an adult with a stable address who was prepared to be financially liable for a stranger’s daughter. The next eleven listings said the same thing in different words. At eight hundred and fifty the co-signer requirement thinned out and the age requirement appeared in its place instead, plain in the listing text, no room for asking around it. Must be 19 or accompanied by guardian. She read it on four more listings before she stopped counting.

One listing further down had no age requirement at all, a room above a restaurant kitchen in Mount Pleasant for seven hundred, and she had the message half typed before she reached the line near the bottom asking for two years of paystubs, which she did not have, because paystubs were not a thing that got produced by what she did, no matter how well she did it.

Nineteen was the number the province had used until three years ago, when somebody had moved it to sixteen in a budget line she’d never seen and nobody she knew had ever heard discussed. It was how she had come to be at Kelso House at all, three years earlier than the system around her still expected. The listings had not moved with the number. There was no reason they should have. Nobody had told them, and nobody was going to, because updating four hundred and six listings was somebody’s afternoon and nobody’s job.

She closed the tab, put the milk and eggs away, and put the chicken in the freezer compartment, angling the tray so it wouldn’t touch the ice buildup on the back wall.

The rain had got heavier while she was not watching it, and the sill under the cracked window had gone dark with the wet, a stain spreading along the paint that would need to be wiped before it soaked into the wood underneath.

* * *

The kitchen was on the ground floor and had four induction rings, two of which worked.

It was a good kitchen, or had been. Somebody had specified it properly, in a year when there had been money: a long steel counter, a deep double sink, extraction that actually pulled instead of just moving the smell from one part of the room to another. There was a laminated sheet on the wall about cleaning up after yourself that had been laminated at a time when laminating things was a reasonable use of somebody’s afternoon, the corners gone soft and rounded from years of hands.

Marek was at the near end with a pot of the same pasta he made every night, and a girl Nadia had seen twice before was sitting on the counter by the window with her feet on a chair, not cooking anything, just there for the warmth the two working rings put into the room.

Nadia put the chicken thighs in the sink to thaw the corners off and turned on the third ring, which lit, and then went dead about four minutes later while she was cutting, the display blanking without so much as a flicker first.

Marek said don’t bother, that one does that.

She asked how long it had been doing it.

He said since he got here, and he had got here in January, which put it at eight months minimum and probably longer, because nobody clocked the start of a fault, only the point where they stopped trying it.

She put her knife down and pulled the ring unit forward on the counter to get at the back of it, which nobody had done, because there was no reason anybody would when you could just use one of the other two. The vents on the underside were grey with dust, and there was a smell off it that she recognised before she had the panel all the way open: hot dust and hot lacquer, the particular smell of something running at a temperature it was not built to run at.

The fan was seized. She turned it with a finger and it went a quarter turn and stopped hard against whatever had got into the bearing, which after eight months uninspected could have been anything from dried grease to a strand of something that had worked its way in through the vent slots and wound itself tight.

So it ran, and it cooked itself, and the thermal cutout did exactly what a thermal cutout is for and shut it down before it burned the building down, and then it cooled off over a few hours and worked again, and everybody who used the kitchen had learned it was broken and stopped using it, which meant nobody ever gave it the hours it needed to cool fully and reset, which meant it never got the chance to look fixed even by accident.

She had a forty-millimetre fan in the drawer upstairs, off a dead router she’d stripped for the exact same reason she stripped everything, on the chance it would matter later. Right voltage, wrong connector.

It took her about twenty minutes, most of which was making the connector work rather than fitting the fan itself.

When she put it back and turned it on, the ring lit and stayed lit, and the fan came up with the specific high whine that a cheap forty-millimetre always makes, loud enough that Marek looked over, and she went back to cutting the chicken like nothing had happened, because nothing had, not from where she was standing.

Marek watched the ring for a while like he expected it to change its mind.

The girl on the counter asked if she could do the one on the end too.

Nadia went and looked at the one on the end. The one on the end had a cracked ceramic top and water damage under the control board and a green bloom of corrosion across two of the pads that meant it had been getting wet for a long time, a different problem and a worse one, not a fan and a bearing but a board that had been slowly eaten from underneath, and she said so.

The girl said okay, and then asked how she knew how to do that, and Nadia said the same thing she always said, which was that it was mostly just looking, and the girl seemed to accept it, because people usually did, and the conversation ended there the way most of her conversations ended, at the exact point where either of them would have had to say something more.

She cooked all six of the thighs, because they were on the date and there was no other option that didn’t waste two of them, and ate two standing at the counter while the other four cooled enough to go in a container, the steam fogging the window glass behind her for a moment before it cleared.

Marek said she should charge for that.

She said the fan cost nothing, it was out of a router.

He said that was not what he meant, and she said she knew, and left it there, because he was right and there was no version of that conversation that ended anywhere useful for either of them.

She washed her pan and the knife and the board, wiped the counter down twice, because of the laminated sheet, and dried the sink with the cloth that hung on the oven handle for exactly that purpose.

In eleven days she would not have this kitchen. She would not have a sink, or a fridge with a freezer compartment that iced over, or a room upstairs with a door and a table she could put a bench light on. She worked out, standing there with the cloth still in her hand, what all of that came to in dollars a month at the going rate, and got a number, and the number was more than she made on a good week, not an average one, and she put the cloth back on the handle and carried the container up the stairs.

* * *

The office at Kelso House was a converted linen store with a desk in it and a window that did not open, the old shelving still bolted to one wall with a row of binders on it that nobody had opened in longer than Nadia had been coming here.

Ferreira was doing two things at once and had been doing two things at once every time Nadia had seen her, one hand on the keyboard and the phone wedged against her shoulder, on hold, the hold music tinny and looping every forty seconds or so. She hung up without saying anything to whoever was on the other end, found the file, and then found the right file inside it, and read it with her chin down, the way a person reads something they already know isn’t going to say what they want it to.

The answer was no, and it was not a difficult no, not one Ferreira seemed to be enjoying or resisting, just a no that the file had already contained before Nadia sat down. The thirty days was thirty days. There was no extension mechanism, because the programme had not been written with one, and the reason it had not been written with one, Ferreira explained, half to herself, was that it had originally run alongside a placement service that had since been folded into something else in a reorganisation two budget cycles back, and the something else had a waiting list that was currently fourteen months, a number she pulled up to check because she didn’t trust it from memory.

She said Nadia could reapply after ninety days.

Nadia asked where she was supposed to be for the ninety days.

Ferreira said that was a good question, and wrote something down on a pad that already had several other good questions written on it in the same handwriting, and said she would ask, and Nadia understood from the way she said it, flat, already moving to close the file, that she would in fact ask, and that asking was the whole of what could be done from this desk, and that they both already knew what the answer to the asking would be, because it was the same answer the fourteen months had already given them.

Then Ferreira said the thing she had said before, more than once, which was that Nadia should come and talk to her if things got difficult.

Nadia said thanks.

She meant it, in the sense that Ferreira had done every part of her job correctly and there was nothing left over that she had failed to do, no missing signature or overlooked form that would have changed anything if only someone had caught it in time. It was not clear to Nadia what else the woman was supposed to say, or what she herself was supposed to do with the sentence once it had been said, or why people offered that particular line so often right after telling you no, as if the no and the offer belonged to two different conversations that happened to be sharing a room. She had stopped trying to work it out some years ago. It came up often enough that she had a response ready, and the response worked, in that it ended the exchange cleanly, and that was as far as she had ever taken it.

She went back up to the room and put her shoes on, the wet ones from the morning, because the dry pair were the ones she needed to still be dry tomorrow, for the two buses to Grandview and the walk after that to wherever the eight-dollar board turned out to be waiting.

* * *

The rain was steady by then and the streetlights had come on early because of it, throwing wet cones of orange down onto pavement that hadn’t dried since June. She went east and then south, past the shuttered dry cleaner with the paper still taped inside the window from whenever it had closed, and the lot where they had knocked down the bakery two years ago and not built anything, just gravel now, weeds coming up through it in patches, down to the tracks and along the fence line where the path ran flat and mostly straight.

The first ten minutes were the ten minutes she disliked, her lungs catching up to what she was asking of them, the cold air scraping on the way in. After that her breathing settled into the thing it did, level and unremarkable, and stopped being something she was managing and became something that simply happened underneath everything else.

She ran the arithmetic again while she went, because it was there and running was where she did it.

Eleven days. Thirty-three twenty and eighty-one. Eight dollars committed to a board that would let her fix the next chair or the next drone or the next mobility harness for somebody who had been quoted eleven hundred and said no, because there was always somebody who’d been quoted eleven hundred and said no. There was work. There was always work. She had turned down two jobs in July because she hadn’t had the parts on hand, and both of them had come back around once she did. What there was not was a room, and what there was not was a person who would sign for a room, and there was no version of the arithmetic, run any number of times, where a sixteen-year-old with a prepaid card from a pharmacy solved that in eleven days.

So it did not solve. That was what the number said, every time she ran it. She checked it twice more anyway, the way she’d check a rail before putting a scope on it even when she already knew what the scope would show, and it came out the same both times.

She turned at the crossing and went up the hill instead of along the flat, past the last of the streetlights and into the dark stretch where the road climbed between two rows of houses with their windows lit yellow against the wet.

The hill was four blocks and it was the part she did on the days when the numbers did not work, because there was nothing at the top of it that needed anything from her and she did not have to think about anything on the way up, only the next ten metres of grade. Her quads were on fire by the second block, the good kind, the kind she’d chosen. Her hood had gone heavy and cold against the back of her neck from the rain collecting in it, so she pushed it off and let the rain get at her properly, running down her collar, and kept climbing.

At the top she put her hands on her knees and let her lungs do what they were going to do, drawing hard, her breath showing white for a second before the rain took it. The city was down there under the weather with all of its lights on, spread out flat toward the water, none of it looking back.

She went down the other side and did the hill again, and a third time after that, until her legs had stopped being about the numbers and were only about themselves.

* * *

Kelso House was quiet when she got back, the front hall smelling of Marek’s pasta from an hour before, the stairwell light on the second floor still out. She peeled off the wet jacket and hung it over the radiator that wasn’t on, where it would drip onto a towel she’d left there for exactly that, and set her shoes by the door with the insoles pulled out to dry.

The board for Thursday’s pickup was where she’d left it, and the container with the last two thighs was in the mini-fridge under the window, and the voucher letter was still under the bench light with the milliamp reading written across the back of it in pencil. Somewhere above her a pipe knocked twice and went quiet, the building settling the way it did every night around this time, a sound she’d stopped hearing as anything but the building doing what old buildings did.

She turned the letter over once more, read the date on it, did the subtraction she had already done four times that day, and got eleven again.

Then she turned off the bench light, and the room went to the colour of the streetlight through the rain on the glass, and she got into bed with her hair still wet, and set the alarm for the earlier of the two times she might need it, on the chance the buses to Grandview ran the way the schedule said they would.
